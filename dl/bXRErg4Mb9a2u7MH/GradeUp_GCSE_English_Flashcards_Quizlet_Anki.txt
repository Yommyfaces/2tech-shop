The Exams 01.01.1: AQA Paper 1: name, time and marks.	Explorations in Creative Reading and Writing. 1 hour 45 minutes, 80 marks: 40 reading (one fiction extract) + 40 writing. | Example: A 105-minute plan: 15 min reading, 45 min on Q1–Q4, 45 min on Q5.
The Exams 01.01.2: AQA Paper 1: the five questions and their marks.	Q1 list four things (4). Q2 language (8). Q3 structure (8). Q4 evaluate a statement (20). Q5 description or story (40). | Example: Q1: list four things you learn about the house from lines 1–5 (one mark each).
The Exams 01.02.1: Edexcel Paper 1: name, time and marks.	Fiction and Imaginative Writing. 1 hour 45 minutes, 64 marks: 24 reading (a 19th-century fiction extract) + 40 writing. | Example: A 105-minute plan: 10 min reading, about 45 min on Q1–Q4, 50 min on the writing task.
The Exams 01.02.2: Edexcel Paper 1: the questions and their marks.	Q1 identify a word or phrase (1). Q2 give two things (2). Q3 language and structure (6). Q4 evaluate how successfully (15). Q5 or Q6 imaginative writing (40). | Example: Q1 might ask for a word that shows the character is afraid: one mark for 'trembling'.
The Exams 01.03.1: OCR Component 02: name, time and marks.	Exploring Effects and Impact. 2 hours, 80 marks: two literary prose texts + imaginative writing. | Example: Text 1 could be a modern story and Text 2 a 19th-century extract on the same theme.
The Exams 01.03.2: OCR Component 02: the questions and their marks.	Q1 retrieval (4). Q2 language and structure, Text 1 (6). Q3 language and structure, Text 2 (12). Q4 compare and evaluate both texts (18). Q5 or Q6 imaginative writing (40). | Example: Q4 might say 'Both texts present isolation as frightening', and you evaluate both.
The Exams 01.04.1: What are AO1 and AO2?	AO1: find and interpret information, select evidence. AO2: analyse how writers use language and structure for effect. | Example: AO1: listing what we learn about a character. AO2: explaining how 'snarled' makes him seem animal-like.
The Exams 01.04.2: What are AO3 and AO4?	AO3: compare writers' ideas and perspectives. AO4: evaluate texts critically with evidence. | Example: AO3: 'Writer A finds the sea peaceful, whereas Writer B finds it terrifying.' AO4: 'I strongly agree, because…'
The Exams 01.05.1: How are the 40 writing marks split on every board?	24 for content and organisation (AO5) + 16 for technical accuracy: spelling, punctuation, grammar and vocabulary (AO6). | Example: A brilliant story with careless spelling might get 20/24 for AO5 but only 8/16 for AO6.
The Exams 01.05.2: Golden timing rule for any question.	About 1 minute per mark, plus reading time. A 4-mark question should take about 5 minutes, not 15. | Example: An 8-mark question: about 8 to 10 minutes. A 20-mark question: about 20 to 25 minutes.
The Exams 01.05.3: What's the most common reason students lose writing marks?	Running out of time on the reading section. Watch the clock and move to the writing on time. | Example: Spending 40 minutes on Q4 leaves 20 minutes for a 40-mark writing task.
Language Techniques 02.01.1: Simile: what is it, and what's the effect?	A comparison using like or as: silver as a spoon. Makes an image vivid and easy to picture. | Example: 'The lake was as still as glass': the simile makes the water seem perfectly calm and smooth.
Language Techniques 02.01.2: Metaphor: what is it, and what's the effect?	Says something is something else: the waves were white teeth. A stronger, more direct comparison than a simile. | Example: 'Her words were knives': the metaphor shows they hurt like being cut.
Language Techniques 02.01.3: Personification: what is it, and what's the effect?	Giving human qualities to objects or nature: the wind stole their words. Can make a setting feel threatening or alive. | Example: 'The branches clawed at the window': 'clawed' makes the tree seem like a monster trying to get in.
Language Techniques 02.02.1: Onomatopoeia: what is it, and what's the effect?	Words that sound like what they describe: hiss, crack, thud. Brings the scene to life through sound. | Example: 'The ice cracked beneath her feet': 'cracked' lets us hear the danger.
Language Techniques 02.02.2: Alliteration: what is it, and what's the effect?	Repeated consonant sounds at the start of nearby words: bright, bold beam. Adds rhythm and emphasis. | Example: 'Cold, cruel, clattering cobbles': the hard 'c' sounds make the street feel harsh.
Language Techniques 02.02.3: Sibilance: what is it, and what's the effect?	Repeated s or sh sounds: the sea sighed softly. Can sound calm, secretive or sinister. | Example: 'Shadows slid silently across the stairs' creates a sinister, secretive mood.
Language Techniques 02.03.1: Imagery: what is it, and what's the effect?	Language that appeals to the senses, creating a picture in the reader's mind. Helps the reader feel present. | Example: 'The smell of burnt toast and the hiss of the kettle filled the cramped kitchen' appeals to smell, sound and sight.
Language Techniques 02.03.2: Semantic field: what is it, and what's the effect?	A group of words linked to one topic: bruised, wound, bleeding (injury). Builds a consistent mood. | Example: 'Bloom', 'blossom', 'petals', 'fragrant': a semantic field of flowers that makes the garden feel lively and beautiful.
Language Techniques 02.04.1: Connotation: what does it mean?	The ideas or feelings a word suggests beyond its dictionary meaning: crimson suggests blood, danger or passion. | Example: 'Home' suggests safety and warmth; 'house' is just a building.
Language Techniques 02.04.2: Why do verbs matter in analysis?	Strong verbs show force and tone: crept vs. walked. Zooming in on a verb often gives the best analysis. | Example: 'She dragged herself up the stairs' shows exhaustion far more than 'she went up the stairs'.
Language Techniques 02.05.1: Pathetic fallacy: what is it, and what's the effect?	Weather or setting that reflects mood: a storm before a crisis. Creates atmosphere and foreshadows events. | Example: Bright sunshine as two friends make up after an argument reflects their happiness.
Language Techniques 02.05.2: Symbolism: what is it?	An object that represents a bigger idea: a lamp in a window can symbolise hope or waiting. | Example: A wilting rose on the table could symbolise a dying relationship.
Language Techniques 02.05.3: Foreshadowing: what is it?	Hints about what will happen later, e.g. a dark sky before a tragedy. Builds suspense. | Example: A character saying 'Nothing can go wrong now' hints that something will.
Language Techniques 02.06.1: Juxtaposition: what is it, and what's the effect?	Placing contrasting ideas side by side: a tiny lamp in a huge storm. Highlights the difference. | Example: A lavish feast described beside a starving beggar outside the window highlights inequality.
Language Techniques 02.06.2: Oxymoron: what is it, and what's the effect?	Two opposite words together: deafening silence. Creates tension or shows confused feelings. | Example: 'Cruel kindness' suggests help that actually hurts.
Language Techniques 02.06.3: Hyperbole: what is it, and what's the effect?	Deliberate exaggeration: I've told you a million times. Shows strong feeling or creates humour. | Example: 'This bag weighs a tonne' exaggerates to show how heavy and annoying it is.
Language Techniques 02.07.1: Emotive language: what is it?	Words chosen to make the reader feel strongly: helpless, innocent, heartbreaking. Common in persuasive texts. | Example: 'Innocent children are suffering in silence' makes the reader feel pity and anger.
Language Techniques 02.07.2: Rhetorical question: what is it?	A question asked for effect, not an answer: How could anyone ignore this? Makes the reader reflect or agree. | Example: 'Do we really want our town to be remembered for its litter?' makes the reader agree that it must change.
Language Techniques 02.07.3: Direct address: what is it?	Speaking to the reader using you. Makes writing personal and involving. | Example: 'You can make a difference today' involves the reader personally.
Language Techniques 02.08.1: Repetition: what is it, and what's the effect?	Repeating a word, phrase or structure for emphasis. It can show obsession, panic or determination. | Example: 'Run. Run. Don't look back.' shows panic and urgency.
Language Techniques 02.08.2: Rule of three (triplet): what is it?	Grouping ideas in threes: cold, dark and silent. Sounds complete and memorable and builds rhythm. | Example: 'The night was cold, silent and endless' builds a bleak rhythm.
Language Techniques 02.09.1: Tone: what is it? Give 4 examples.	The writer's attitude or mood in a text: sarcastic, nostalgic, menacing, celebratory (also: hopeful, bitter, playful). | Example: A grandparent describing childhood summers with warm, fond detail has a nostalgic tone.
Sentences & Grammar 03.01.1: Simple sentence: what is it, and what's the effect?	One main clause: She waited. Short simple sentences create tension, shock or emphasis. | Example: 'The door slammed.' A short simple sentence for sudden shock.
Sentences & Grammar 03.01.2: Compound sentence: what is it?	Two main clauses joined by and, but, or, so: The door opened, but nobody came in. | Example: 'She wanted to scream, but she stayed silent.' The 'but' shows inner conflict.
Sentences & Grammar 03.01.3: Complex sentence: what is it, and what's the effect?	A main clause plus a subordinate clause: Although it was late, she kept reading. Adds detail and shows connections. | Example: 'Because the bridge had collapsed, the village was cut off.' The subordinate clause explains the cause.
Sentences & Grammar 03.01.4: Minor sentence: what is it, and what's the effect?	A fragment with no main verb: Silence. Five o'clock. Creates pace, drama or the feeling of time passing. | Example: 'Midnight. Rain. Then footsteps.' The fragments build suspense.
Sentences & Grammar 03.02.1: What is a declarative, imperative and exclamatory sentence?	Declarative: a statement. Imperative: a command (Look out.). Exclamatory: strong emotion (What a disaster!). | Example: Imperative: 'Don't move.' Exclamatory: 'How beautiful it is!' Declarative: 'It was late.'
Sentences & Grammar 03.03.1: Why vary sentence length in your own writing?	It controls pace: long sentences build or overwhelm; short ones shock. Variety is in the top-band criteria for writing. | Example: 'The storm raged for hours, flattening fences, tearing tiles from roofs and turning the lane into a river. Then silence.'
Sentences & Grammar 03.03.2: Name 4 ways to start a sentence differently.	With an adverb (Slowly,), a verb (Gasping,), a preposition (Beneath the bridge,), or a subordinate clause (As the clock struck,). | Example: Adverb: 'Silently, she slipped out.' Verb: 'Shaking, he opened the letter.'
Sentences & Grammar 03.03.3: What is a list sentence (asyndetic list) used for?	A list without and: mud, rain, blood, noise. Feels fast, chaotic or overwhelming. | Example: 'Shouts, sirens, smoke, screams' makes the scene feel chaotic.
Sentences & Grammar 03.04.1: 1st vs 3rd person narration: effect?	1st (I): personal, we share the narrator's thoughts but may not trust them. 3rd (she/he): can show several viewpoints, feels more distant. | Example: First person: 'I knew he was lying.' Third person: 'She knew he was lying, but he believed she trusted him.'
Structure 04.01.1: What's the difference between language and structure?	Language: word and sentence choices. Structure: the order and organisation of the whole text: what comes when, and why. | Example: Language: 'gloomy' creates a dark mood. Structure: the writer delays revealing the body until the final line.
Structure 04.01.2: Name 6 things to look for in a structure question.	Opening, focus shifts, time (flashback, time jumps), perspective, turning point/climax, ending (and links to the start). | Example: Opening: a calm village. Turning point: a stranger arrives. Ending: the village is silent again, but changed.
Structure 04.02.1: What does 'in medias res' mean?	Starting in the middle of the action, with no build-up. Hooks the reader instantly. | Example: 'The car was already in the river when Sam woke up.'
Structure 04.02.2: Cyclical structure: what is it, and what's the effect?	The ending returns to the opening image or idea. Creates a sense of completeness, or shows what has changed. | Example: A story that opens and closes with the same empty swing, which is now swaying as if someone has just left.
Structure 04.02.3: Cliffhanger: what is it?	Ending at a moment of suspense, leaving the reader desperate to know what happens. | Example: A chapter ending with: 'Then the lights went out.'
Structure 04.03.1: Flashback: what is it, and what's the effect?	A scene from earlier in time. Explains a character's feelings or backstory and builds empathy. | Example: In the middle of a hospital scene, the narrator remembers teaching her daughter to ride a bike.
Structure 04.03.2: Focus shift: what is it?	Moving the reader's attention, e.g. wide to narrow (sky → house → face), outside to inside, or person to person. | Example: A shift from the whole crowded station to one old man's trembling hands.
Structure 04.04.1: Climax: what is it?	The point of greatest tension or drama in a text. | Example: The moment the climber's rope begins to fray.
Structure 04.04.2: What's a one-line paragraph used for?	Isolating a key moment so it stands out, e.g. a shock, a decision or a turning point. | Example: After a long paragraph of normal life, one line: 'That was the last time I saw him.'
Structure 04.04.3: Dialogue in structure: why might a writer place speech at a certain point?	To break up description, reveal character, change pace, or deliver a key piece of information at a dramatic moment. | Example: A sudden line of speech, 'Who's there?', breaks a long silent description and raises tension.
Structure 04.05.1: AQA Q3: the beginning–middle–end method.	Three paragraphs in text order: what we focus on at the start and why, how and why the focus shifts in the middle, and how the ending links back. | Example: Paragraph 1: the opening focuses on a peaceful garden. Paragraph 2: the focus shifts to a broken window. Paragraph 3: the ending returns to the garden, now threatening.
Analysis & Evaluation 05.01.1: What–How–Why: what is the analysis method?	WHAT effect does the writer create? HOW (quote and method)? WHY: zoom in on a word and explain its effect on the reader. | Example: WHAT: the city feels threatening. HOW: the buildings 'loomed'. WHY: 'loomed' suggests something huge and menacing about to fall.
Analysis & Evaluation 05.01.2: Why does feature spotting score low?	Naming a technique ('this is a simile') without explaining its effect shows no analysis. Effect earns the marks. | Example: Weak: 'This is a metaphor.' Strong: 'The metaphor makes the reader see her anger as uncontrollable fire.'
Analysis & Evaluation 05.02.1: How long should quotations be?	Short: 1–6 words, embedded in your own sentence. Long quotations waste time and hide your analysis. | Example: Instead of copying a whole sentence, embed a short quotation: the sky 'bled' red.
Analysis & Evaluation 05.02.2: What does 'zooming in' mean?	Picking one word from a quotation and exploring what it suggests. Top answers give 2–3 possible meanings. | Example: 'Bled' suggests violence and injury, perhaps hinting that something terrible is coming.
Analysis & Evaluation 05.03.1: Give 5 strong analysis verbs.	Conveys, implies, evokes, emphasises, foreshadows (also: highlights, reinforces, reveals). | Example: 'The writer evokes a sense of loneliness…' sounds more precise than 'shows'.
Analysis & Evaluation 05.03.2: Give 3 tentative phrases for exploring meaning.	This could suggest…, Perhaps the writer implies…, Alternatively, it may show… | Example: 'This could suggest she is frightened; alternatively, it may show she is angry.'
Analysis & Evaluation 05.04.1: AQA Q4: what is it asking you to do?	Evaluate a statement about the text: say how far you agree, and support your judgement with analysed evidence. Worth 20 marks. | Example: Statement: 'The writer makes the ending surprising.' You judge how far you agree, with evidence.
Analysis & Evaluation 05.04.2: Edexcel Q4: what is it asking you to do?	Evaluate how successfully the writer creates an effect (e.g. tension, atmosphere). Judge and analyse, citing evidence. Worth 15 marks. | Example: 'How successfully does the writer create tension?' Judge how well, quote, and analyse the methods.
Analysis & Evaluation 05.04.3: OCR Q4: what is it asking you to do?	Compare both texts against a statement and evaluate how well each writer achieves it. Worth 18 marks. | Example: 'Both texts present nature as dangerous.' Compare how far each writer achieves this.
Analysis & Evaluation 05.05.1: The 4-step evaluation paragraph.	1 Judge (use the statement's words). 2 Evidence. 3 Analyse methods. 4 Evaluate why it's effective. | Example: Judge: 'The writer powerfully conveys fear.' Evidence: 'trembling'. Analyse: the verb shows uncontrollable fear. Evaluate: the reader feels the tension too.
Analysis & Evaluation 05.05.2: Give 4 judgement phrases for evaluation.	The writer powerfully conveys…, This is particularly effective because…, The most convincing moment is…, I partly agree, because… | Example: 'The most convincing moment is when the door creaks open, because…'
Analysis & Evaluation 05.06.1: Give 4 comparison connectives.	Similarly, In contrast, Whereas, Both writers… but… | Example: 'Both writers describe the city at night, but Writer A finds it magical, whereas Writer B finds it threatening.'
Analysis & Evaluation 05.07.1: How should you use context in Language papers?	Lightly, if at all. One sentence can support a point, but marks are for analysing the writing. | Example: One line such as 'Children often worked in Victorian factories' before analysing the language.
19th-Century Words 06.01.1: Countenance: what does it mean?	A person's face or expression. | Example: 'His countenance darkened' = his face showed anger.
19th-Century Words 06.01.2: Melancholy: what does it mean?	Deep, lasting sadness. | Example: 'A melancholy mood settled over the house' = a lasting sadness.
19th-Century Words 06.01.3: Apprehension: what does it mean?	Worry or fear about the future. | Example: 'She waited with apprehension for the doctor' = she was worried about bad news.
19th-Century Words 06.01.4: Composure: what does it mean?	Calmness and self-control. | Example: 'He lost his composure and wept' = he lost his self-control.
19th-Century Words 06.01.5: Wretched: what does it mean?	Very unhappy or in a terrible state. | Example: 'The wretched child shivered in rags' = the child was in a terrible state.
19th-Century Words 06.01.6: Vexed: what does it mean?	Annoyed or frustrated. | Example: 'She was vexed by his constant lateness' = annoyed.
19th-Century Words 06.01.7: Gaunt: what does it mean?	Very thin and bony, often from illness or hunger. | Example: 'His gaunt face showed weeks without food' = thin and bony.
19th-Century Words 06.02.1: Ascertain: what does it mean?	To find out for certain. | Example: 'He set out to ascertain the cause of the noise' = to find out for certain.
19th-Century Words 06.02.2: Hitherto: what does it mean?	Until now. | Example: 'Hitherto, no one had dared to enter' = until now.
19th-Century Words 06.02.3: Ere: what does it mean?	Before. | Example: 'Ere the sun set, they reached the castle' = before.
19th-Century Words 06.02.4: Lest: what does it mean?	In case; to avoid the risk that. | Example: 'She hid the letter lest he find it' = in case he found it.
19th-Century Words 06.02.5: Whence: what does it mean?	From where. | Example: 'Whence came that terrible cry?' = where did it come from?
19th-Century Words 06.02.6: Presently: what does it mean?	Soon (not 'now'). | Example: 'The doctor will see you presently' = soon.
19th-Century Words 06.02.7: Forthwith: what does it mean?	Immediately. | Example: 'He left the room forthwith' = immediately.
19th-Century Words 06.03.1: Desolate: what does it mean?	Empty, bleak and lonely. | Example: 'A desolate cottage stood on the empty hill' = bleak and lonely.
19th-Century Words 06.03.2: Sombre: what does it mean?	Dark, serious or gloomy. | Example: 'The sombre mourners walked in silence' = serious and gloomy.
19th-Century Words 06.03.3: Ominous: what does it mean?	Giving the feeling that something bad will happen. | Example: 'An ominous silence fell over the camp' = something bad was coming.
19th-Century Words 06.03.4: Spectre: what does it mean?	A ghost; a frightening threat. | Example: 'The spectre of poverty haunted the family' = a frightening threat.
19th-Century Words 06.04.1: Name 4 big themes in 19th-century fiction.	Class and poverty, the supernatural and the Gothic, duty and religion, nature and isolation. | Example: A wealthy family ignoring a starving servant (class), or a ghost on a stormy moor (the Gothic).
19th-Century Words 06.04.2: Tip for long 19th-century sentences.	Split them at semicolons and commas, and find the main verb. The intro above the extract tells you who and where. | Example: Long sentence: '…; she paused; she listened; at last she spoke.' Split at the semicolons: four short actions.
Creative Writing 07.01.1: How should you split 45 minutes of writing?	Plan 5 minutes, write 35, check 5. | Example: Start writing by 10:55 if the section starts at 10:50, and stop to check at 11:30.
Creative Writing 07.01.2: Why write about fewer events?	One moment described in rich detail scores higher than a long plot. Examiners reward crafting, not action. | Example: Describe ten minutes in a waiting room in detail, rather than a whole day out.
Creative Writing 07.01.3: How many characters should a GCSE story have?	Ideally 1–3. More characters means less depth and more confusion. | Example: A girl and her grandfather on a boat, rather than a whole family on holiday.
Creative Writing 07.02.1: The 5-part story plan.	Hook → build-up → turn (problem) → climax (slowed down) → ending (cyclical or quiet twist). | Example: Hook: a strange knock. Build-up: an empty house. Turn: the door is unlocked. Climax: a figure on the stairs. Ending: it was Gran, home early, holding a cake.
Creative Writing 07.02.2: Name 4 endings to avoid.	'It was all a dream', 'Then I woke up', everyone dies, or an ending that just stops mid-action. | Example: Instead of 'then I woke up', end with a quiet image: the empty chair, still rocking.
Creative Writing 07.03.1: Description method: drop, zoom, shift.	Drop into a moment, zoom in on one detail, zoom out to the wider scene, shift time or weather, return to the opening detail. | Example: Drop: a station at dawn. Zoom: a single dropped glove. Shift: the train arrives and the crowd surges. Return: the glove lies alone on the platform.
Creative Writing 07.03.2: Why use all five senses?	Sound, smell, touch and taste make a scene feel real. Most students only use sight. | Example: Touch: 'The banister was sticky and cold.' Taste: 'The air tasted of salt and diesel.'
Creative Writing 07.04.1: Give 4 types of strong opening.	One-word sentence (Silence.), dialogue, weather as mood, or a short mystery (Nobody had used the back door for eleven years.) | Example: Weather as mood: 'The fog came in like a rumour, slowly swallowing the town.'
Creative Writing 07.05.1: What does 'show, don't tell' mean?	Show emotions through actions and details, not labels. Not She was nervous but She twisted her sleeve until the thread snapped. | Example: Tell: 'He was happy.' Show: 'He took the stairs two at a time, humming.'
Creative Writing 07.06.1: What is a motif, and why use one?	A detail that returns through the piece (a ticking clock, a red scarf). It gives structure and a satisfying ending. | Example: A red scarf that she wears at the start, loses in the middle, and finds snagged on a fence at the end.
Creative Writing 07.07.1: How do you set out dialogue?	New speaker, new line. Speech marks around spoken words, punctuation inside: “Wait,” she said. | Example: "Are you coming?" asked Mum. (new line) "In a minute," I said.
Non-Fiction Writing 08.01.1: Which papers include non-fiction writing?	AQA Paper 2 Q5, Edexcel Paper 2 Q8/9, and OCR Component 01: articles, letters, speeches and essays. | Example: AQA Paper 2 Q5 might ask for an article arguing that school holidays should be shorter.
Non-Fiction Writing 08.01.2: How do you match register to audience?	Formal and polite for headteachers and councils; lively and direct for teenagers. Read the task for the audience. | Example: To teenagers: 'Let's be honest – who actually enjoys Monday mornings?' To a council: 'I would like to raise a serious concern.'
Non-Fiction Writing 08.02.1: Persuasive techniques: what does AFOREST stand for?	Anecdote, Facts, Opinion, Rhetorical questions, Emotive language, Statistics, Triplets (rule of three). | Example: Anecdote: 'Last winter, my neighbour slipped on an icy, unlit path…'
Non-Fiction Writing 08.03.1: Article: key features.	A headline, an optional subheading, a hooking opening, paragraphs, and a strong conclusion. | Example: Headline: 'Our Parks Are Dying – And It's Our Fault'. Subheading: 'Why we must act before summer.'
Non-Fiction Writing 08.03.2: Formal letter: key features.	Address the recipient (Dear Sir/Madam), clear purpose early, formal tone, sign off Yours faithfully (unknown name) or Yours sincerely (named). | Example: 'Dear Sir or Madam, I am writing to express my concern about the closure of our library.'
Non-Fiction Writing 08.03.3: Speech: key features.	Address the audience directly, use rhetorical questions and repetition, and end with a call to action. | Example: Ending call to action: 'So I ask you: join us on Saturday. Bring a bag. Bring a friend. Bring hope.'
Non-Fiction Writing 08.04.1: What is a counter-argument and why include one?	Stating the opposing view, then rebutting it. It makes your argument sound balanced and convincing. | Example: 'Some argue uniforms are expensive. However, they cost far less than keeping up with fashion trends.'
Punctuation & SPaG 09.01.1: Semicolon (;): when do you use it?	To join two linked complete sentences: The door creaked open; nobody was there. | Example: 'I had trained for months; I was ready.'
Punctuation & SPaG 09.01.2: Colon (:): when do you use it?	To introduce a list, explanation or reveal: She knew one thing: she had to run. | Example: 'Pack three things: a torch, a map and water.'
Punctuation & SPaG 09.02.1: Dashes for parenthesis: when do you use them?	To add extra information mid-sentence: The house – empty for years – waited. | Example: 'My brother – who never apologises – said sorry.'
Punctuation & SPaG 09.02.2: Ellipsis (…): when should you use it?	To show trailing off or suspense. Use it once or twice at most; overuse looks weak. | Example: 'She opened the box and…'
Punctuation & SPaG 09.03.1: Comma splice: what is it, and how do you fix it?	Two sentences joined by a comma: It was dark, I was scared. Fix with a full stop, a semicolon or a conjunction. | Example: Comma splice: 'I was late, the bus had gone.' Fixed: 'I was late because the bus had gone.'
Punctuation & SPaG 09.04.1: Their, there, they're?	Their = belonging to them. There = a place. They're = they are. | Example: 'They're leaving their bags over there.'
Punctuation & SPaG 09.04.2: Your vs you're?	Your = belonging to you. You're = you are. | Example: 'You're going to need your coat.'
Punctuation & SPaG 09.04.3: Affect vs effect?	Affect is usually a verb (to influence). Effect is usually a noun (the result). The storm affected her; its effect was terrifying. | Example: 'The cold affected my hands; the effect was numb fingers.'
Punctuation & SPaG 09.04.4: Its vs it's?	Its = belonging to it. It's = it is / it has. | Example: 'It's raining, and the dog has hurt its paw.'
Punctuation & SPaG 09.05.1: Could of / would of: what's wrong?	It should be could have / would have (or could've). | Example: 'I could have won' (not 'could of').
Punctuation & SPaG 09.05.2: Name 6 commonly misspelt words.	definitely, separate, necessary, beginning, rhythm, embarrass (also: occasion, believe, environment) | Example: Memory trick: 'There is a rat in separate' (sep-A-RAT-e).
Punctuation & SPaG 09.05.3: What are tense slips, and how do you avoid them?	Switching between past and present by accident. Pick a tense (usually past) and check it in your final 5 minutes. | Example: Slip: 'She opened the door and sees a shadow.' Fixed: 'She opened the door and saw a shadow.'
Punctuation & SPaG 09.06.1: What punctuation should a top-band answer show?	A range used accurately: full stops, commas, apostrophes, speech marks, plus some semicolons, colons, dashes and question marks. | Example: 'The house was silent: no footsteps, no voices – nothing. Was anyone there?'
