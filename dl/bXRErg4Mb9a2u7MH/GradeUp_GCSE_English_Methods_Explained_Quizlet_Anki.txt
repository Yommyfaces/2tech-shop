The Exams 01.01 – AQA Paper 1: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Explorations in Creative Reading and Writing: the name of AQA Paper 1. | Section A (reading): questions 1 to 4 on one fiction extract, 40 marks. | Section B (writing): question 5, a description or a story, 40 marks. | Total: 80 marks in 1 hour 45 minutes. | USE IT WHEN – Planning your time and knowing what each AQA Paper 1 question wants. | STEPS – 1. Spend about 15 minutes reading the extract and the questions. | 2. Give each reading question roughly 1 minute per mark. | 3. Leave 45 minutes for Question 5 (the writing is half the marks). | 4. Check the clock after Question 4 and move on.
The Exams 01.01 – Worked example: Plan a timing schedule for AQA Paper 1 (105 minutes).	Reading the extract and questions: 15 min | Q1 (4 marks): 5 min. Q2 (8 marks): 10 min | Q3 (8 marks): 10 min. Q4 (20 marks): 20 min | Q5 (40 marks): 45 min (5 plan, 35 write, 5 check) | Answer: 15 + 5 + 10 + 10 + 20 + 45 = 105 minutes
The Exams 01.01 – AQA Paper 1: what is the common mistake?	Spending too long on Q4 and rushing Q5. Question 5 is worth 40 marks, half the paper.

Q3 (language and structure, 6 marks) and Q4 (evaluation, 15 marks) need analysis. | 4. Choose ONE writing task (Q5 or Q6) and spend about 45 minutes on it.

The Exams 01.03 – OCR Component 02: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Exploring Effects and Impact: the name of OCR Component 02. | Section A: questions on two literary prose texts (retrieval, language and structure, comparison and evaluation). | Section B: one imaginative writing task. | Total: 80 marks in 2 hours. | USE IT WHEN – Planning your time and knowing what each OCR Component 02 question wants. | STEPS – 1. Read both texts and the questions (about 15 minutes). | 2. Q1 retrieval is quick; Q2 and Q3 analyse language and structure. | 3. Q4 compares BOTH texts against a statement (18 marks). | 4. The Exams 01.03 – OCR Component 02: what is the common mistake?	Writing about only one text in Question 4. It asks you to compare and evaluate BOTH.
The Exams 01.04 – Assessment objectives: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Assessment objective (AO): what the examiner is marking. | AO1: find and interpret information and evidence. | AO2: analyse how writers use language and structure. | AO3: compare writers' ideas and perspectives. | AO4: evaluate texts critically. | AO5 and AO6: writing content and technical accuracy. | USE IT WHEN – Understanding what each question is really marking, so your answer targets it. | STEPS – 1. Check which AO the question tests (it is in the mark scheme and the question wording). | 2. AO1: select and explain evidence. | 3. AO2: analyse methods and effects. AO3: compare. AO4: give a judgement with evidence. | 4. Write in the way that AO rewards.

The Exams 01.04 – Assessment objectives: what is the common mistake?	Retelling the story (AO1 only) when the question asks for analysis (AO2).
The Exams 01.05 – Writing marks and timing: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – AO5 (24 marks): content and organisation: ideas, structure, tone and vocabulary for the task. | AO6 (16 marks): technical accuracy: spelling, punctuation, grammar and a range of sentences. | Timing rule: about 1 minute per mark, plus reading time. | USE IT WHEN – Getting the most marks from the writing task and managing your time. | STEPS – 1. Plan before you write, so the piece is organised (AO5). | 2. Use ambitious vocabulary and varied sentences (AO5 and AO6). | 3. Save 5 minutes at the end to check spelling and punctuation (AO6). | 4. Watch the clock: move to the writing on time.
The Exams 01.05 – Worked example: You have 45 minutes for the writing task. How should you use them?	Plan: 5 minutes (ideas, structure, a strong ending) | Write: 35 minutes, crafting one focused piece | Check: 5 minutes for spelling, punctuation and tense slips | Answer: 5 + 35 + 5 = 45 minutes
The Exams 01.05 – Writing marks and timing: what is the common mistake?	Skipping the check. AO6 is 16 marks, and many errors are easy to fix in the last 5 minutes.
Language Techniques 02.01 – Simile, metaphor and personification: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Simile: compares using 'like' or 'as' (her eyes were bright as lanterns). | Metaphor: says one thing IS another (the classroom was a zoo). | Personification: gives human qualities to things (the wind whispered). | Effect: the language effect on the reader: what it makes you picture, feel or think. | USE IT WHEN – Analysing comparisons in a reading question, or using them in your own writing. | STEPS – 1. Spot the comparison and name it. | 2. Ask: what two things are being compared? | 3. Explain what the comparison makes the reader picture or feel. | 4. Zoom in on one word and explore its connotations.
Language Techniques 02.01 – Worked example: Analyse: 'The old house crouched at the end of the lane, waiting.'	Personification: the house 'crouched' and is 'waiting', like a living creature | 'Crouched' suggests an animal ready to pounce | So the house feels threatening, as if it is watching the reader | Answer: The personification makes the house seem alive and dangerous
Language Techniques 02.01 – Simile, metaphor and personification: what is the common mistake?	Writing 'this simile makes it more interesting'. Say exactly WHAT the reader pictures and WHY.
Language Techniques 02.02 – Sound: onomatopoeia, alliteration and sibilance: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Onomatopoeia: words that sound like their meaning (crash, sizzle, thud). | Alliteration: the same consonant sound at the start of nearby words (dark, dripping doorway). | Sibilance: repeated s or sh sounds (the snake slid silently). Sound techniques make writing feel loud, soft, harsh or gentle. | USE IT WHEN – Analysing how sound is used for effect, or adding it to your own writing. | STEPS – 1. Read the line aloud in your head. | 2. Name the sound technique and quote it. | 3. Describe the sound: harsh, soft, hissing, sudden. | 4. Link it to the mood (calm, menace, violence).
Language Techniques 02.02 – Worked example: Analyse: 'The sea sighed and shushed against the silent shore.'	Sibilance: 'sighed', 'shushed', 'silent', 'shore' | The soft 's' and 'sh' sounds imitate gentle waves | This creates a calm, peaceful, almost sleepy atmosphere | Answer: The sibilance makes the scene sound soft and calm
Language Techniques 02.02 – Sound: onomatopoeia, alliteration and sibilance: what is the common mistake?	Just listing letters ('it uses the letter s'). Explain what the SOUND does to the mood.
Language Techniques 02.03 – Imagery and semantic fields: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Imagery: language that creates a picture, using the senses (sight, sound, smell, touch, taste). | Sensory language: words linked to the five senses. | Semantic field: a group of words linked to one topic (frost, icy, numb, shiver: a semantic field of cold), which builds a consistent mood. | USE IT WHEN – Spotting patterns of words in an extract, and describing scenes in your own writing. | STEPS – 1. Look for words that link to the same topic or sense. | 2. Name the semantic field or sense (e.g. a semantic field of war). | 3. Quote two or three of the words. | 4. Explain the overall mood or idea the pattern creates.
Language Techniques 02.03 – Worked example: Analyse: 'The market was a battlefield: traders shouted orders, shoppers charged and crates lay wounded in the gutter.'	Semantic field of war: 'battlefield', 'orders', 'charged', 'wounded' | The ordinary market is described like a battle | This makes it feel chaotic, noisy and aggressive | Answer: The semantic field of war shows how wild and hectic the market is
Language Techniques 02.03 – Imagery and semantic fields: what is the common mistake?	Quoting one word for a semantic field. You need a PATTERN of several linked words.
Language Techniques 02.04 – Word choice: connotations and verbs: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Connotation: the ideas or feelings a word suggests beyond its dictionary meaning (scarlet suggests blood, danger or passion). | Denotation: the literal meaning. | Verb: a doing word; strong verbs show exactly how something is done (stormed vs walked). | USE IT WHEN – Getting the highest marks in language analysis by exploring single words. | STEPS – 1. Pick one powerful word, often a verb or adjective. | 2. Give its literal meaning. | 3. List two or three things it suggests (connotations). | 4. Explain what this tells the reader about the character or scene.
Language Techniques 02.04 – Worked example: Analyse the verb: 'He slithered into the room.'	'Slithered' literally means moved like a snake | Connotations: sly, silent, dangerous, untrustworthy | So the character seems sneaky and threatening before he even speaks | Answer: 'Slithered' suggests he is snake-like and not to be trusted
Language Techniques 02.04 – Word choice: connotations and verbs: what is the common mistake?	Choosing a dull word like 'the' or 'went' to zoom in on. Pick the most loaded word in the quotation.
Language Techniques 02.05 – Mood: pathetic fallacy, symbolism and foreshadowing: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Pathetic fallacy: weather or setting that reflects a mood (rain at a funeral). | Symbolism: an object that represents a bigger idea (a broken mirror for a broken family). | Foreshadowing: hints about what will happen later (crows circling before a death). | USE IT WHEN – Analysing how settings and objects create atmosphere and hint at the future. | STEPS – 1. Notice descriptions of weather, light and objects. | 2. Ask: what mood or idea does this reflect? | 3. Name the technique (pathetic fallacy, symbolism or foreshadowing). | 4. Explain how it prepares the reader for what comes next.
Language Techniques 02.05 – Worked example: Analyse: 'As she opened the letter, thunder rolled across the darkening sky.'	Pathetic fallacy: the storm reflects her feelings | 'Darkening' suggests things are about to get worse | It foreshadows bad news in the letter, building suspense | Answer: The storm reflects her dread and foreshadows bad news
Language Techniques 02.05 – Mood: pathetic fallacy, symbolism and foreshadowing: what is the common mistake?	Calling every mention of weather pathetic fallacy. It must reflect a character's mood or the atmosphere.
Language Techniques 02.06 – Contrast and exaggeration: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Juxtaposition: placing contrasting ideas side by side (a child laughing beside a grave). | Oxymoron: two opposite words together (bittersweet, deafening silence). | Hyperbole: deliberate exaggeration (I've waited forever). Contrast makes ideas stand out; exaggeration shows strong feeling or humour. | USE IT WHEN – Analysing contrast and exaggeration, and using them in your own writing. | STEPS – 1. Look for opposites placed close together. | 2. Name the technique and quote both parts. | 3. Explain what the contrast highlights. | 4. For hyperbole, explain the feeling behind the exaggeration.
Language Techniques 02.06 – Worked example: Analyse: 'In the glittering ballroom, a ragged boy pressed his face to the window.'	Juxtaposition: 'glittering ballroom' against 'ragged boy' | The wealth inside contrasts with the poverty outside | This highlights inequality and makes us sympathise with the boy | Answer: The juxtaposition exposes the gap between rich and poor
Language Techniques 02.06 – Contrast and exaggeration: what is the common mistake?	Confusing an oxymoron (two words) with juxtaposition (two ideas or images).
Language Techniques 02.07 – Persuasive language: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Emotive language: words chosen to stir strong feelings (innocent, defenceless, heartbreaking). | Rhetorical question: asked for effect, not an answer (How can we stand by?). | Direct address: speaking to the reader as 'you'. | Persuasive writing: aims to change the reader's mind. | USE IT WHEN – Analysing persuasive texts, and persuading in your own non-fiction writing. | STEPS – 1. Identify the writer's purpose: what do they want us to think or do? | 2. Spot emotive words, rhetorical questions and 'you'. | 3. Explain how each one makes the reader feel involved or responsible. | 4. Link back to the purpose.
Language Techniques 02.07 – Worked example: Analyse: 'Would you leave a defenceless puppy in the freezing cold?'	Rhetorical question and direct address ('you') involve the reader | Emotive words 'defenceless' and 'freezing' create pity | The reader feels guilty, so is persuaded to help | Answer: It makes the reader feel personally responsible
Language Techniques 02.07 – Persuasive language: what is the common mistake?	Saying a rhetorical question 'makes the reader think'. Say WHAT it makes them think or feel.
Language Techniques 02.08 – Repetition and the rule of three: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Repetition: repeating a word, phrase or structure for emphasis (Never again. Never.). | Rule of three (triplet): grouping ideas in threes (fast, fierce and fearless). Both create rhythm and make ideas memorable. | USE IT WHEN – Analysing emphasis and rhythm, and making your own writing memorable. | STEPS – 1. Spot the repeated word, or the group of three. | 2. Quote it. | 3. Explain what is being emphasised. | 4. Describe the effect: rhythm, urgency, obsession or determination.
Language Techniques 02.08 – Worked example: Analyse: 'She checked the lock. She checked it again. She checked it a third time.'	Repetition of 'She checked' | Short repeated sentences show an obsessive, anxious state | It builds tension: the reader senses she is afraid | Answer: The repetition reveals her growing anxiety
Language Techniques 02.08 – Repetition and the rule of three: what is the common mistake?	Overusing triplets in your own writing. One or two well-placed ones are far more powerful.
Language Techniques 02.09 – Tone: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Tone: the writer's attitude or the mood of the text, shown through word choice. | Examples: sarcastic, nostalgic, menacing, celebratory, hopeful, bitter, playful, urgent. Tone can shift during a text. | USE IT WHEN – Describing the writer's attitude in a reading answer, or controlling tone in your own writing. | STEPS – 1. Read the passage and decide how it feels. | 2. Choose a precise tone word (not just 'happy' or 'sad'). | 3. Support it with a quotation. | 4. Comment on any shift in tone and why it happens.
Language Techniques 02.09 – Worked example: What is the tone of: 'Oh, wonderful. Another Monday. Just what I needed.'	'Wonderful' and 'just what I needed' say the opposite of what is meant | So the tone is sarcastic | It shows the speaker's frustration in a humorous way | Answer: Sarcastic
Language Techniques 02.09 – Tone: what is the common mistake?	Using vague tone words like 'nice' or 'good'. Precise words (bitter, nostalgic, menacing) earn more marks.
Sentences & Grammar 03.01 – Simple, compound, complex and minor sentences: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Clause: a group of words with a verb. | Main clause: makes sense on its own. | Simple sentence: one main clause (She ran.). | Compound: two main clauses joined by and, but, or, so. | Complex: a main clause plus a subordinate clause (Although it rained, we played.). | Minor sentence: a fragment with no main verb (Silence.). | USE IT WHEN – Analysing sentence forms in a reading question, and varying your own sentences. | STEPS – 1. Identify the sentence type. | 2. Quote it. | 3. Explain its effect: short sentences create shock or tension; long ones build detail or pace. | 4. Link the effect to the moment in the story.
Sentences & Grammar 03.01 – Worked example: Analyse: 'He reached for the handle. Locked.'	A simple sentence followed by a minor sentence | The one-word 'Locked.' creates a sudden stop | It mirrors his shock and traps the reader with him | Answer: The minor sentence creates sudden shock and tension
Sentences & Grammar 03.01 – Simple, compound, complex and minor sentences: what is the common mistake?	Naming the sentence type without explaining its effect. The effect earns the marks.
Sentences & Grammar 03.02 – Statements, commands and exclamations: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Declarative sentence: makes a statement (The train was late.). | Imperative: gives a command (Close the door.). | Exclamatory: shows strong emotion, ending in ! (What a mess!). | Interrogative: asks a question (Where is she?). | USE IT WHEN – Analysing sentence functions, especially in persuasive and dialogue-heavy texts. | STEPS – 1. Identify the function: statement, command, exclamation or question. | 2. Quote it. | 3. Explain why the writer uses it here. | 4. Commands often show urgency or authority; exclamations show emotion.
Sentences & Grammar 03.02 – Worked example: Analyse: 'Act now. Sign the petition today.'	Two imperative sentences (commands) | They are short and direct | They create urgency and push the reader to act immediately | Answer: The imperatives make the call to action urgent
Sentences & Grammar 03.02 – Statements, commands and exclamations: what is the common mistake?	Overusing exclamation marks in your own writing. One strong one is better than many.
Sentences & Grammar 03.03 – Varying sentences in your writing: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Sentence variety: mixing short, long, simple and complex sentences to control pace. | Sentence openers: starting with an adverb (Slowly,), a verb (Gasping,), a preposition (Beneath the bridge,) or a subordinate clause (As the clock struck,). | Asyndetic list: a list without 'and' (mud, rain, noise). | USE IT WHEN – Making your own writing reach the top band for AO5 and AO6. | STEPS – 1. Avoid starting every sentence with 'I', 'He' or 'The'. | 2. Use a short sentence after a long one for impact. | 3. Try an asyndetic list to create speed or chaos. | 4. Read your paragraph back and check the rhythm.
Sentences & Grammar 03.03 – Worked example: Improve: 'I walked into the forest. It was dark. I was scared. I heard a noise.'	Vary the openers and lengths: | 'Slowly, I stepped between the trees, where the light thinned to nothing.' | 'Then, a crack.' | 'I froze.' | Answer: Varied openers and lengths create tension
Sentences & Grammar 03.03 – Varying sentences in your writing: what is the common mistake?	Writing every sentence the same length. Examiners want deliberate variety for effect.
Sentences & Grammar 03.04 – First and third person: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Narrative perspective: who tells the story. | First person (I, we): personal and intimate, but the narrator may not be reliable. | Third person (he, she, they): can show several viewpoints, and can feel more distant. | Narrator: the voice telling the story. | USE IT WHEN – Analysing how perspective affects the reader, and choosing it for your own story. | STEPS – 1. Identify the perspective from the pronouns. | 2. Explain what it lets us see or know. | 3. First person: we share thoughts, but only one view. | 4. Third person: wider view, more distance.
Sentences & Grammar 03.04 – Worked example: Why might a writer tell a ghost story in the first person?	We only know what the narrator knows | We share their fear and confusion directly | So the suspense is stronger | Answer: First person traps us in the narrator's fear
Sentences & Grammar 03.04 – First and third person: what is the common mistake?	Switching between 'I' and 'she' by accident in your own story. Choose one and stick to it.
Structure 04.01 – Language vs structure: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Language: the writer's choices of words, phrases, techniques and sentences. | Structure: how the whole text is organised: what comes when, and why. | Structural features: the opening, focus shifts, time shifts, perspective, the turning point or climax, and the ending. | USE IT WHEN – Telling a structure question apart from a language question, and finding things to write about. | STEPS – 1. Language = zoom in on words. Structure = zoom out to the whole text. | 2. Read the beginning, middle and end separately. | 3. Ask: where is the focus at each point, and why does it change? | 4. Use structure terms: shift, introduces, develops, contrasts, returns.
Structure 04.01 – Worked example: An extract opens on a busy beach, then focuses on one child alone at the water's edge. Is this language or structure?	It is about where the reader's attention moves | So it is structure: a shift in focus from wide to narrow | Effect: it singles out the child, making us wonder why they are alone | Answer: Structure: a focus shift from wide to narrow
Structure 04.01 – Language vs structure: what is the common mistake?	Analysing individual words in a structure question. Structure is about order and organisation.
Structure 04.02 – Openings and endings: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – In medias res: starting in the middle of the action. | Cyclical structure: the ending returns to the opening image or idea. | Cliffhanger: ending at a moment of suspense. | Opening: the first lines, which hook the reader. | Resolution: how the story ends. | USE IT WHEN – Analysing openings and endings in a reading question, and planning your own. | STEPS – 1. Look at how the text begins: description, dialogue or action? | 2. Explain how it hooks the reader. | 3. Compare the ending with the opening: has something returned or changed? | 4. Explain the effect of the ending on the reader.
Structure 04.02 – Worked example: A story begins and ends with a lamp glowing in a window. What is the effect?	This is a cyclical structure | It creates a sense of completeness | If the meaning of the lamp has changed (e.g. from waiting to grief), it shows how events have changed the character | Answer: A cyclical structure gives closure and highlights change
Structure 04.02 – Openings and endings: what is the common mistake?	Saying 'it's cyclical' without explaining what has changed or why it matters.
Structure 04.03 – Flashbacks and focus shifts: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Flashback: a scene from an earlier time. | Time shift: a jump forwards or backwards. | Focus shift: moving the reader's attention, e.g. from wide to narrow, outside to inside, or one character to another. | Perspective: whose point of view we see through. | USE IT WHEN – Tracking how a text moves through time and place in a structure question. | STEPS – 1. Notice when the time or place changes. | 2. Name the shift (flashback, time jump, focus shift). | 3. Explain why the writer does it at that point. | 4. Link it to the reader's understanding or feelings.
Structure 04.03 – Worked example: Mid-extract, a soldier remembers his mother's kitchen. Why might the writer include this flashback?	It interrupts the war scene with a warm memory | It shows what the soldier has lost | It builds sympathy and makes the war feel crueller by contrast | Answer: The flashback builds sympathy through contrast
Structure 04.03 – Flashbacks and focus shifts: what is the common mistake?	Retelling what happens in the flashback instead of explaining WHY it is placed there.
Structure 04.04 – Building tension: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Climax: the point of greatest tension. | Tension: the feeling that something is about to happen. | One-line paragraph: isolates a key moment so it stands out. | Dialogue: speech, which can change the pace or reveal a key detail at a dramatic moment. | USE IT WHEN – Explaining how a writer builds and releases tension. | STEPS – 1. Track how the tension rises through the extract. | 2. Find the climax: the most dramatic moment. | 3. Look for short paragraphs, sudden dialogue or a change of pace. | 4. Explain how each choice affects the reader.
Structure 04.04 – Worked example: After a long description of a quiet house, a paragraph says only: 'Then the phone rang.' Effect?	A one-line paragraph after a long, calm one | The sudden change of pace shocks the reader | It marks a turning point and raises the tension | Answer: The one-line paragraph creates a sudden turning point
Structure 04.04 – Building tension: what is the common mistake?	Saying 'it builds tension' without explaining HOW (pace, placement, sentence length).
Structure 04.05 – Answering a structure question: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Beginning–middle–end method: three paragraphs in text order for a structure question (AQA Q3). | Focus: what the reader is looking at. | Shift: a change of focus. | Structural terms: opens with, shifts to, develops, contrasts, returns to, concludes. | USE IT WHEN – Writing a clear, well-organised answer to a structure question. | STEPS – 1. Paragraph 1: what we focus on at the start, and why. | 2. Paragraph 2: how and why the focus shifts in the middle. | 3. Paragraph 3: how the ending links back, or what has changed. | 4. Use a structural term in every paragraph.
Structure 04.05 – Worked example: Write the first sentence of a structure answer for an extract that opens on a stormy sky.	'The writer opens with a wide focus on the stormy sky, which immediately establishes a threatening atmosphere.' | It names the focus (the sky) | It explains the purpose (the atmosphere) | Answer: Name the focus, then explain why the writer chose it
Structure 04.05 – Answering a structure question: what is the common mistake?	Jumping around the text. Work through it in order: beginning, middle, end.
Analysis & Evaluation 05.01 – The What–How–Why method: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – What–How–Why: an analysis method. WHAT effect does the writer create? HOW do they create it (a short quotation and the method)? WHY does it work (zoom in on a word and explain its effect on the reader)? Feature spotting: naming techniques without explaining their effect, which scores low. | USE IT WHEN – Writing a strong analysis paragraph for any language question. | STEPS – 1. WHAT: make a clear point about the effect. | 2. HOW: embed a short quotation and name the method if useful. | 3. WHY: zoom in on a key word and explain what it suggests. | 4. Link back to the question.
Analysis & Evaluation 05.01 – Worked example: Write a What–How–Why paragraph on: 'The fog swallowed the street.'	WHAT: The writer makes the setting feel threatening. | HOW: The personification in 'swallowed' presents the fog as a living creature. | WHY: 'Swallowed' suggests the street is being eaten and destroyed, so the reader feels the town is helpless and in danger. | Answer: Point, quotation and method, then the effect of one word
Analysis & Evaluation 05.01 – The What–How–Why method: what is the common mistake?	Stopping after HOW ('this is personification'). The WHY is where the marks are.
Analysis & Evaluation 05.02 – Quotations and zooming in: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Embedded quotation: a short quotation woven into your own sentence (The fog 'swallowed' the street.). | Zooming in: picking one word and exploring what it suggests. | Tentative language: showing more than one possible meaning (could suggest, perhaps, alternatively). | USE IT WHEN – Using evidence precisely so your analysis is focused and detailed. | STEPS – 1. Choose 1 to 6 words, not whole sentences. | 2. Embed them in your own sentence. | 3. Zoom in on the most powerful word. | 4. Give two or three possible meanings.
Analysis & Evaluation 05.02 – Worked example: Rewrite with an embedded quotation and zoom: The writer says 'the old man shuffled slowly down the corridor'. This shows he is old.	The old man 'shuffled' down the corridor. | 'Shuffled' suggests his feet barely leave the ground, | perhaps from age or illness, or maybe from exhaustion and defeat. | Answer: Short quotation, one word zoomed, two meanings
Analysis & Evaluation 05.02 – Quotations and zooming in: what is the common mistake?	Copying out long quotations. They waste time and hide your analysis.
Analysis & Evaluation 05.03 – Analytical vocabulary: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Analysis verbs: words that describe what the writer does (conveys, implies, evokes, emphasises, reveals, highlights, reinforces, foreshadows). | Tentative phrases: 'This could suggest…', 'Perhaps…', 'Alternatively…'. They make writing precise and show you can explore meaning. | USE IT WHEN – Making your analysis sound precise and sophisticated. | STEPS – 1. Replace 'shows' with a precise verb (implies, conveys, evokes). | 2. Use tentative phrases to explore more than one meaning. | 3. Vary your verbs across the answer. | 4. Keep the focus on the writer's choices.
Analysis & Evaluation 05.03 – Worked example: Improve: 'This shows that the character is sad.'	'The writer conveys the character's grief…' | '…which perhaps implies she blames herself.' | Answer: Precise verbs and a tentative second meaning
Analysis & Evaluation 05.03 – Analytical vocabulary: what is the common mistake?	Using 'shows' in every sentence. Varied analysis verbs signal a higher-level answer.
Analysis & Evaluation 05.04 – Evaluation questions: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Evaluation: judging how well the writer achieves something, with evidence. | AQA Q4 (20 marks): how far do you agree with a statement? Edexcel Q4 (15 marks): how successfully does the writer create an effect? OCR Q4 (18 marks): compare both texts against a statement. | Judgement: your opinion, clearly stated. | USE IT WHEN – Understanding what an evaluation question wants on your exam board. | STEPS – 1. Underline the key words in the statement. | 2. Decide how far you agree. | 3. Support your judgement with short quotations. | 4. Analyse the methods, then explain why they are effective.
Analysis & Evaluation 05.04 – Worked example: Statement: 'In this part of the story, the writer makes the reader feel sorry for the girl.' How should you start?	Use the statement's words in your judgement: | 'The writer powerfully makes the reader feel sorry for the girl, particularly when…' | Then give evidence and analyse it | Answer: Start with a clear judgement using the statement's words
Analysis & Evaluation 05.04 – Evaluation questions: what is the common mistake?	Analysing language without ever giving a judgement. Evaluation needs your opinion.
Analysis & Evaluation 05.05 – Writing an evaluation paragraph: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Judgement phrases: 'The writer powerfully conveys…', 'This is particularly effective because…', 'I partly agree, because…'. | USE IT WHEN – Writing evaluation paragraphs that hit the top band. | STEPS – 1. Judge: open with your view, using the statement's words. | 2. Evidence: embed a short quotation. | 3. Analyse: explain the method and zoom in. | 4. Evaluate: say why it works so well for the reader.
Analysis & Evaluation 05.05 – Worked example: Write an evaluation paragraph: 'The writer makes the storm terrifying.'	Judge: I strongly agree the storm is terrifying, especially at its height. | Evidence: the thunder 'tore' the sky apart. | Analyse: 'tore' suggests violent destruction, as if the sky is being ripped. | Evaluate: this is effective because the reader feels the storm's raw power. | Answer: Judge, evidence, analyse, evaluate
Analysis & Evaluation 05.05 – Writing an evaluation paragraph: what is the common mistake?	Forgetting step 4. Without 'why it's effective', it is analysis, not evaluation.
Analysis & Evaluation 05.06 – Comparing two texts: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Comparison: showing similarities and differences between two texts. | Comparison connectives: Similarly, Likewise, Both writers (similar); In contrast, Whereas, However, On the other hand (different). | Perspective: each writer's attitude or viewpoint. | USE IT WHEN – Comparing two texts (e.g. OCR Q4, or Paper 2 comparison questions). | STEPS – 1. Find a point both texts share, or differ on. | 2. Write about Text 1 with evidence. | 3. Use a connective and write about Text 2 with evidence. | 4. Explain what the similarity or difference reveals.
Analysis & Evaluation 05.06 – Worked example: Write a comparison sentence about two writers who describe a city.	'Writer A presents the city as exciting, describing its "dazzling" lights, | whereas Writer B finds it lonely, calling the streets "empty" and "grey".' | Answer: Both texts in one sentence, linked by 'whereas'
Analysis & Evaluation 05.06 – Comparing two texts: what is the common mistake?	Writing about each text separately with no links. Compare throughout, using connectives.
Analysis & Evaluation 05.07 – Using context: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Context: the time, place and background of a text (e.g. Victorian attitudes to class). | In Language papers, context is used lightly: marks come from analysing the writing, not from historical facts. | AO2: analysing language and structure. | USE IT WHEN – Knowing how much background to include in a Language paper. | STEPS – 1. Focus on the writer's methods and effects first. | 2. Add context only if it helps explain a choice. | 3. Keep it to one short sentence. | 4. Link it straight back to the text.
Analysis & Evaluation 05.07 – Worked example: An extract shows a servant bowing to a wealthy lady. Should you write a paragraph about Victorian class?	No: one short link is enough | e.g. 'The bow reflects the strict class divisions of the time,' | then return to analysing the language | Answer: One short sentence of context, then back to analysis
Analysis & Evaluation 05.07 – Using context: what is the common mistake?	Writing a history essay. In Language papers, context earns little or nothing on its own.
19th-Century Words 06.01 – Words for people and feelings: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – 19th-century vocabulary for people: countenance (face or expression), melancholy (deep sadness), apprehension (worry about the future), composure (calmness), wretched (very unhappy or in a terrible state), vexed (annoyed), gaunt (very thin and bony). | USE IT WHEN – Understanding how characters are described in 19th-century fiction extracts. | STEPS – 1. Underline unfamiliar words in the extract. | 2. Use the words around them to guess the meaning. | 3. Learn the most common ones in advance. | 4. Use the meaning to explain the character's feelings.
19th-Century Words 06.01 – Worked example: What does this tell us: 'Her countenance was pale, and a deep melancholy hung upon her.'	Countenance = face; melancholy = deep sadness | Her face is pale and she is very sad | Perhaps she is ill, grieving or has had bad news | Answer: She looks pale and is deeply sad
19th-Century Words 06.01 – Words for people and feelings: what is the common mistake?	Skipping unknown words. Working out one key word can unlock the whole sentence.
19th-Century Words 06.02 – Old-fashioned time and logic words: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Time and logic words in 19th-century texts: ascertain (find out for certain), hitherto (until now), ere (before), lest (in case), whence (from where), presently (soon, not 'now'), forthwith (immediately). | USE IT WHEN – Following the meaning of sentences in 19th-century extracts. | STEPS – 1. Spot old-fashioned linking words. | 2. Swap in the modern meaning. | 3. Reread the sentence with the modern word. | 4. Check it makes sense in context.
19th-Century Words 06.02 – Worked example: Rewrite in modern English: 'He resolved to ascertain the truth ere nightfall, lest it be lost forever.'	ascertain = find out for certain; ere = before; lest = in case | 'He decided to find out the truth before nightfall, in case it was lost forever.' | Answer: He wanted to find out the truth before dark, in case it was lost
19th-Century Words 06.02 – Old-fashioned time and logic words: what is the common mistake?	Reading 'presently' as 'now'. In 19th-century texts it means 'soon'.
19th-Century Words 06.03 – Words for atmosphere: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – 19th-century words for atmosphere: desolate (empty, bleak and lonely), sombre (dark, serious or gloomy), ominous (suggesting something bad will happen), spectre (a ghost, or a frightening threat). These words often build a Gothic or threatening mood. | USE IT WHEN – Analysing atmosphere in 19th-century and Gothic extracts. | STEPS – 1. Collect the atmosphere words in the extract. | 2. Work out their meanings. | 3. Decide on the overall mood they create. | 4. Analyse one word closely for its connotations.
19th-Century Words 06.03 – Worked example: Analyse: 'The desolate moor lay sombre beneath an ominous sky.'	Desolate (empty, lonely), sombre (gloomy), ominous (threatening) | Together they build a bleak, threatening atmosphere | 'Ominous' foreshadows danger, creating suspense | Answer: A bleak, threatening atmosphere that hints at danger
19th-Century Words 06.03 – Words for atmosphere: what is the common mistake?	Translating the words but not analysing their effect on the mood.
19th-Century Words 06.04 – Reading 19th-century extracts: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Themes in 19th-century fiction: class and poverty, the supernatural and the Gothic, duty and religion, nature and isolation. | Long sentences: often split by semicolons and commas. | Introduction: the text above the extract tells you who and where. | USE IT WHEN – Reading long, old-fashioned extracts quickly and confidently. | STEPS – 1. Read the introduction above the extract first. | 2. Split long sentences at semicolons and commas. | 3. Find the main verb to see what is actually happening. | 4. Look for familiar themes (class, the supernatural, nature).
19th-Century Words 06.04 – Worked example: Simplify: 'The carriage, having laboured for some hours along the rutted road, at length drew up before the gates.'	Main clause: 'The carriage … drew up before the gates.' | Extra detail: it had struggled along a bumpy road for hours | 'At length' = finally | Answer: After hours on a bumpy road, the carriage finally stopped at the gates
19th-Century Words 06.04 – Reading 19th-century extracts: what is the common mistake?	Giving up on a long sentence. Find the main verb and the rest falls into place.
Creative Writing 07.01 – Planning and timing: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Plan: a quick outline before writing (5 minutes). | Crafting: choosing words and structure carefully. | Focus: one moment or event described in rich detail scores higher than a long plot. | Characters: ideally 1 to 3 in a GCSE story. | USE IT WHEN – Using your writing time well and keeping the story focused. | STEPS – 1. Spend 5 minutes planning: the moment, the mood, the ending. | 2. Limit yourself to 1 to 3 characters and a short time span. | 3. Write for 35 minutes, crafting each paragraph. | 4. Spend the last 5 minutes checking.
Creative Writing 07.01 – Worked example: Plan a story titled 'The Storm' in 5 minutes.	Moment: a girl waiting alone for her father's boat in a storm | Characters: the girl (and her father, at the end) | Mood: fear, then relief | Ending: the boat's light appears; cyclical return to the lighthouse | Answer: One moment, few characters, a clear ending
Creative Writing 07.01 – Planning and timing: what is the common mistake?	Planning an epic plot with lots of events. It gets rushed and loses marks for crafting.
Creative Writing 07.02 – Structuring a story: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Climax: the most dramatic moment, written in slow motion with detail. | Endings to avoid: 'it was all a dream', 'then I woke up', everyone dies, or stopping mid-action. | USE IT WHEN – Giving your story a satisfying shape with a strong ending. | STEPS – 1. Hook the reader in the first line. | 2. Build up the setting and character briefly. | 3. Introduce a problem (the turn). | 4. Slow down the climax, then end quietly or with a twist that links back to the start.
Creative Writing 07.02 – Worked example: Plan the 5 parts of a story about a lost dog.	Hook: 'The lead was still warm in my hand.' | Build-up: the park at dusk, the empty collar | Turn: a storm rolls in; the search grows desperate | Climax (slowed): a bark from the old bandstand | Ending: the dog asleep at home, the lead hung back on its hook | Answer: Hook, build-up, turn, slowed climax, quiet ending
Creative Writing 07.02 – Structuring a story: what is the common mistake?	Ending with 'it was all a dream'. Examiners see it as a lazy way to end.
Creative Writing 07.03 – Writing a description: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Description: writing that paints a picture of a place, person or moment, with little plot. | Drop, zoom, shift: drop into a moment, zoom in on one detail, zoom out to the wider scene, shift time or weather, then return to the opening detail. | Five senses: sight, sound, smell, touch and taste. | USE IT WHEN – Writing a top-band description from a picture or a title. | STEPS – 1. Drop: start in one precise moment. | 2. Zoom in on a small detail, then out to the wider scene. | 3. Shift the time, weather or light to show change. | 4. Use all five senses, and return to your first detail at the end.
Creative Writing 07.03 – Worked example: Write two sentences describing a market using two senses other than sight.	'The sharp tang of oranges cut through the smoke from the hot-dog stall.' | 'Coins clattered into tins, and somewhere a radio crackled an old song.' | Answer: Smell and sound make the scene feel real
Creative Writing 07.03 – Writing a description: what is the common mistake?	Only describing what things look like. Most students forget sound, smell, touch and taste.
Creative Writing 07.04 – Strong openings: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Hook: an opening line that grabs the reader. | Types of strong opening: a one-word sentence (Silence.), dialogue ("Don't open it."), weather as mood, or a short mystery (Nobody had used the back door for eleven years.). | USE IT WHEN – Starting your story or description in a way that grabs the examiner. | STEPS – 1. Avoid 'One day…' or waking up in the morning. | 2. Choose an opening type: one word, dialogue, weather or mystery. | 3. Keep the first line short and intriguing. | 4. Make the second sentence add to the mystery or mood.
Creative Writing 07.04 – Worked example: Rewrite this opening: 'One day I went to my gran's house.'	Mystery: 'Gran's front door had been painted black overnight.' | Dialogue: '"Whatever you do," Gran whispered, "don't go upstairs."' | Answer: A mystery or dialogue hook instead of 'One day'
Creative Writing 07.04 – Strong openings: what is the common mistake?	Starting with the alarm clock and breakfast. It is slow and predictable.
Creative Writing 07.05 – Show, don't tell: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Show, don't tell: show emotions through actions, body language and details instead of naming them. | Telling: 'She was nervous.' Showing: 'She twisted her sleeve until the thread snapped.' This lets the reader work out the feeling. | USE IT WHEN – Making characters' feelings vivid in your own writing. | STEPS – 1. Find a sentence that names a feeling (sad, angry, scared). | 2. Ask: what would this person DO or notice? | 3. Write the action or detail instead of the label. | 4. Let the reader work out the emotion.
Creative Writing 07.05 – Worked example: Show, don't tell: 'He was angry.'	'His jaw tightened.' | 'He set the cup down so hard that tea slopped over the rim.' | Answer: Actions reveal the anger without naming it
Creative Writing 07.05 – Show, don't tell: what is the common mistake?	Overloading every sentence with body language. Use it for key moments.
Creative Writing 07.06 – Motifs: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Motif: a detail that returns through a piece of writing (a ticking clock, a red scarf, a robin). It links the beginning, middle and end, gives the writing structure, and creates a satisfying ending. | USE IT WHEN – Giving your writing structure and a memorable ending. | STEPS – 1. Choose one small, meaningful object or sound. | 2. Introduce it in the opening. | 3. Bring it back once in the middle, perhaps changed. | 4. End with it to show how things have changed.
Creative Writing 07.06 – Worked example: Use a motif in a story about a girl leaving home.	Opening: her old bedroom clock ticks loudly | Middle: on the train, she still hears it ticking in her head | Ending: in her new flat, she hangs the same clock on the wall | Answer: The clock links the start, middle and end
Creative Writing 07.06 – Motifs: what is the common mistake?	Mentioning the motif only once. It must return to work.
Creative Writing 07.07 – Setting out dialogue: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Dialogue: characters' speech. | Rules: a new speaker starts a new line; speech marks go around the spoken words; punctuation goes inside the speech marks ("Wait," she said.). | Reporting clause: the 'she said' part. | USE IT WHEN – Setting out dialogue correctly and using it sparingly. | STEPS – 1. Start a new line each time the speaker changes. | 2. Put speech marks around the spoken words only. | 3. Put the comma, full stop or question mark inside the closing speech mark. | 4. Use a little dialogue to reveal character; don't write a whole script.
Creative Writing 07.07 – Worked example: Punctuate: wait she said where are you going	"Wait," she said. "Where are you going?" | Answer: Speech marks, a comma inside, and a question mark inside
Creative Writing 07.07 – Setting out dialogue: what is the common mistake?	Writing pages of chat. A few lines of well-placed dialogue are far more effective.
Non-Fiction Writing 08.01 – Non-fiction tasks and audience: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Non-fiction writing tasks: AQA Paper 2 Q5, Edexcel Paper 2 Q8 or Q9, and OCR Component 01. | Forms: articles, letters, speeches, essays. | GAP: Genre (form), Audience and Purpose. | Register: how formal or informal your writing is. | USE IT WHEN – Reading the task carefully and matching your writing to it. | STEPS – 1. Underline the form, audience and purpose (GAP). | 2. Choose your register: formal for officials, lively for teenagers. | 3. Plan 4 to 6 paragraphs with a clear viewpoint. | 4. Keep the audience in mind all the way through.
Non-Fiction Writing 08.01 – Worked example: Task: 'Write a letter to your headteacher arguing for a longer lunch break.' What are the GAP and register?	Genre: a formal letter | Audience: the headteacher | Purpose: to argue and persuade | Register: formal and polite, but still persuasive | Answer: Formal letter, headteacher, persuade: a polite, formal register
Non-Fiction Writing 08.01 – Non-fiction tasks and audience: what is the common mistake?	Writing in a chatty, slangy style to a headteacher or council.
Non-Fiction Writing 08.02 – Persuasive techniques (AFOREST): key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – AFOREST: persuasive techniques. Anecdote (a short personal story), Facts, Opinion, Rhetorical questions, Emotive language, Statistics, Triplets (rule of three). Use them naturally, not as a checklist. | USE IT WHEN – Writing persuasive and argument pieces. | STEPS – 1. Pick 3 or 4 techniques that suit your argument. | 2. Use an anecdote early to hook the reader. | 3. Back up opinions with facts or statistics (you can invent plausible ones). | 4. Finish with a strong rhetorical question or triplet.
Non-Fiction Writing 08.02 – Worked example: Write two persuasive sentences for 'Schools should ban mobile phones.'	Statistic + emotive: 'Studies suggest that two in three students feel anxious after scrolling late at night.' | Rhetorical question + triplet: 'Shouldn't school be a place to think, talk and learn?' | Answer: A statistic, emotive language, a rhetorical question and a triplet
Non-Fiction Writing 08.02 – Persuasive techniques (AFOREST): what is the common mistake?	Stuffing in every AFOREST technique. Choose the ones that strengthen your argument.
Non-Fiction Writing 08.03 – Articles, letters and speeches: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Article: a headline, an optional subheading, a hooking opening, paragraphs and a strong conclusion. | Formal letter: Dear Sir/Madam, a clear purpose early, a formal tone, and Yours faithfully (name unknown) or Yours sincerely (named). | Speech: address the audience directly, use rhetorical questions and repetition, and end with a call to action. | USE IT WHEN – Including the right features for the form you are asked to write. | STEPS – 1. Identify the form in the task. | 2. Article: headline and subheading. | 3. Letter: greeting, purpose, formal sign-off. | 4. Speech: greet the audience, use direct address, end with a call to action.
Non-Fiction Writing 08.03 – Worked example: How should you start and end a formal letter to 'The Editor' (name unknown)?	Start: 'Dear Sir or Madam,' | State your purpose in the first paragraph | End: 'Yours faithfully,' then your name | Answer: 'Dear Sir or Madam' … 'Yours faithfully'
Non-Fiction Writing 08.03 – Articles, letters and speeches: what is the common mistake?	Using 'Yours sincerely' when you don't know the person's name. That needs 'Yours faithfully'.
Non-Fiction Writing 08.04 – Counter-arguments: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Counter-argument: the opposing view. | Rebuttal: explaining why the opposing view is wrong or weaker. Including both makes your argument sound balanced, fair and more convincing. | Useful phrases: 'Some may argue that…', 'However, this ignores…'. | USE IT WHEN – Making your argument writing more persuasive and sophisticated. | STEPS – 1. Choose the strongest opposing point. | 2. Introduce it fairly: 'Some may argue that…' | 3. Rebut it with evidence: 'However…' | 4. Return to your own viewpoint.
Non-Fiction Writing 08.04 – Worked example: Write a counter-argument and rebuttal for 'Homework should be banned.'	'Some may argue that homework builds independence.' | 'However, this ignores the many students who have no quiet space to work at home,' | 'which turns homework into a test of circumstances, not ability.' | Answer: State the opposing view, then knock it down
Non-Fiction Writing 08.04 – Counter-arguments: what is the common mistake?	Giving the counter-argument and never rebutting it, which weakens your case.
Punctuation & SPaG 09.01 – Semicolons and colons: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Semicolon (;): joins two closely linked complete sentences (The door creaked open; nobody was there.). Colon (:): introduces a list, an explanation or a reveal (She knew one thing: she had to run.). Both sides of a semicolon must be complete sentences. | USE IT WHEN – Using semicolons and colons accurately to reach the top band for AO6. | STEPS – 1. Semicolon: check both halves make sense on their own. | 2. Use it when the ideas are closely linked. | 3. Colon: the first part must be a complete sentence. | 4. After a colon, give the list, explanation or reveal.
Punctuation & SPaG 09.01 – Worked example: Add a semicolon or colon: The wind howled the windows rattled. / He had one aim to win.	'The wind howled; the windows rattled.' (two linked sentences) | 'He had one aim: to win.' (a reveal) | Answer: A semicolon links two sentences; a colon reveals
Punctuation & SPaG 09.01 – Semicolons and colons: what is the common mistake?	Using a semicolon where a comma or colon belongs ('I bought; eggs, milk and bread').
Punctuation & SPaG 09.02 – Dashes and ellipses: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Dashes for parenthesis: add extra information in the middle of a sentence (The house – empty for years – waited.). Brackets and commas can do the same job. | Ellipsis (…): shows trailing off or suspense. Use it once or twice at most. | USE IT WHEN – Using dashes and ellipses for effect without overusing them. | STEPS – 1. Use a pair of dashes around extra information. | 2. Check the sentence still makes sense without the part in dashes. | 3. Use an ellipsis for a pause or suspense at a key moment. | 4. Limit ellipses to one or two per piece.
Punctuation & SPaG 09.02 – Worked example: Add a pair of dashes: The old man who had not spoken for days finally looked up.	'The old man – who had not spoken for days – finally looked up.' | Without the dashes: 'The old man finally looked up.' still makes sense ✓ | Answer: Dashes around the extra information
Punctuation & SPaG 09.02 – Dashes and ellipses: what is the common mistake?	Ending lots of sentences with '…'. Overuse looks weak and loses the effect.
Punctuation & SPaG 09.03 – Comma splices: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Comma splice: two complete sentences wrongly joined with just a comma (It was dark, I was scared.). Fix it with a full stop, a semicolon, or a conjunction (and, but, so, because). It is one of the most common errors that lowers AO6 marks. | USE IT WHEN – Finding and fixing comma splices in your own writing. | STEPS – 1. Find each comma in your writing. | 2. Check if there is a complete sentence on BOTH sides. | 3. If so, it is a comma splice. | 4. Fix it: full stop, semicolon, or add a conjunction.
Punctuation & SPaG 09.03 – Worked example: Fix: 'The rain stopped, we went outside.'	Full stop: 'The rain stopped. We went outside.' | Semicolon: 'The rain stopped; we went outside.' | Conjunction: 'The rain stopped, so we went outside.' | Answer: Three correct fixes
Punctuation & SPaG 09.03 – Comma splices: what is the common mistake?	Thinking commas can join any two sentences. They need a conjunction or a stronger punctuation mark.
Punctuation & SPaG 09.04 – Homophones: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Homophones: words that sound the same but are spelt differently. Their (belonging to them), there (a place), they're (they are). Your (belonging to you), you're (you are). Its (belonging to it), it's (it is or it has). Affect (usually a verb), effect (usually a noun). | USE IT WHEN – Avoiding the most common spelling errors in your writing. | STEPS – 1. Expand contractions to check: they're = they are, you're = you are, it's = it is. | 2. If the expanded version doesn't make sense, use the other spelling. | 3. Affect = verb (to influence); effect = noun (the result). | 4. Check each one in your final 5 minutes.
Punctuation & SPaG 09.04 – Worked example: Choose the right words: '(Their/They're) dog lost (its/it's) collar, and (your/you're) going to find it.'	'They're dog' = 'they are dog' ✗, so 'Their' | 'it's collar' = 'it is collar' ✗, so 'its' | 'you're going' = 'you are going' ✓, so 'you're' | Answer: Their, its, you're
Punctuation & SPaG 09.04 – Homophones: what is the common mistake?	Using 'it's' for possession ('the dog wagged it's tail'). Possessive 'its' has no apostrophe.
Punctuation & SPaG 09.05 – Common errors and spellings: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Could have / would have: never 'could of' or 'would of' (could've sounds like 'could of'). | Tense slip: switching between past and present by accident. | Commonly misspelt words: definitely, separate, necessary, beginning, rhythm, embarrass, occasion, believe, environment. | USE IT WHEN – Removing the common errors that cost AO6 marks. | STEPS – 1. Search your writing for 'of' after could, would or should. | 2. Pick one tense (usually past) and keep to it. | 3. Learn the commonly misspelt words with memory tricks (separate has 'a rat' in it). | 4. Use your final 5 minutes to check.
Punctuation & SPaG 09.05 – Worked example: Correct: 'I should of known. She walks in and looked definately scared.'	'should of' → 'should have' | 'walks' → 'walked' (keep to the past tense) | 'definately' → 'definitely' | Answer: 'I should have known. She walked in and looked definitely scared.'
Punctuation & SPaG 09.05 – Common errors and spellings: what is the common mistake?	Not leaving time to check. Most of these errors are easy to spot when you reread.
Punctuation & SPaG 09.06 – A top-band range of punctuation: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Range of punctuation: using more than the basics, accurately. | Basics: full stops, commas, capital letters, apostrophes, speech marks, question marks. | Range: semicolons, colons, dashes, brackets and the occasional ellipsis or exclamation mark. Accuracy matters more than variety. | USE IT WHEN – Showing a top-band range of accurate punctuation (AO6). | STEPS – 1. Get the basics right first: full stops and commas. | 2. Plan to use one semicolon, one colon and one pair of dashes in the right places. | 3. Use them for effect, not just to show off. | 4. Check each one is correct.
Punctuation & SPaG 09.06 – Worked example: Rewrite with a colon and a dash: 'The kitchen was a mess. There were plates everywhere. Even the cat looked shocked.'	'The kitchen was a mess: plates were stacked everywhere – even the cat looked shocked.' | Answer: A colon for the explanation, a dash for the final twist
Punctuation & SPaG 09.06 – A top-band range of punctuation: what is the common mistake?	Using lots of fancy punctuation incorrectly. One accurate semicolon beats five wrong ones.
