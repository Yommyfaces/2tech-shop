The Exams: AQA Paper 1: name, time and marks.	Explorations in Creative Reading and Writing. 1 hour 45 minutes, 80 marks: 40 reading (one fiction extract) + 40 writing.
The Exams: AQA Paper 1: the five questions and their marks.	Q1 list four things (4). Q2 language (8). Q3 structure (8). Q4 evaluate a statement (20). Q5 description or story (40).
The Exams: Edexcel Paper 1: name, time and marks.	Fiction and Imaginative Writing. 1 hour 45 minutes, 64 marks: 24 reading (a 19th-century fiction extract) + 40 writing.
The Exams: Edexcel Paper 1: the questions and their marks.	Q1 identify a word or phrase (1). Q2 give two things (2). Q3 language and structure (6). Q4 evaluate how successfully (15). Q5 or Q6 imaginative writing (40).
The Exams: OCR Component 02: name, time and marks.	Exploring Effects and Impact. 2 hours, 80 marks: two literary prose texts + imaginative writing.
The Exams: OCR Component 02: the questions and their marks.	Q1 retrieval (4). Q2 language and structure, Text 1 (6). Q3 language and structure, Text 2 (12). Q4 compare and evaluate both texts (18). Q5 or Q6 imaginative writing (40).
The Exams: How are the 40 writing marks split on every board?	24 for content and organisation (AO5) + 16 for technical accuracy: spelling, punctuation, grammar and vocabulary (AO6).
The Exams: Golden timing rule for any question.	About 1 minute per mark, plus reading time. A 4-mark question should take about 5 minutes, not 15.
The Exams: What are AO1 and AO2?	AO1: find and interpret information, select evidence. AO2: analyse how writers use language and structure for effect.
The Exams: What are AO3 and AO4?	AO3: compare writers' ideas and perspectives. AO4: evaluate texts critically with evidence.
The Exams: What's the most common reason students lose writing marks?	Running out of time on the reading section. Watch the clock and move to the writing on time.
Language Techniques: Simile: what is it, and what's the effect?	A comparison using like or as: silver as a spoon. Makes an image vivid and easy to picture.
Language Techniques: Metaphor: what is it, and what's the effect?	Says something is something else: the waves were white teeth. A stronger, more direct comparison than a simile.
Language Techniques: Personification: what is it, and what's the effect?	Giving human qualities to objects or nature: the wind stole their words. Can make a setting feel threatening or alive.
Language Techniques: Pathetic fallacy: what is it, and what's the effect?	Weather or setting that reflects mood: a storm before a crisis. Creates atmosphere and foreshadows events.
Language Techniques: Onomatopoeia: what is it, and what's the effect?	Words that sound like what they describe: hiss, crack, thud. Brings the scene to life through sound.
Language Techniques: Alliteration: what is it, and what's the effect?	Repeated consonant sounds at the start of nearby words: bright, bold beam. Adds rhythm and emphasis.
Language Techniques: Sibilance: what is it, and what's the effect?	Repeated s or sh sounds: the sea sighed softly. Can sound calm, secretive or sinister.
Language Techniques: Semantic field: what is it, and what's the effect?	A group of words linked to one topic: bruised, wound, bleeding (injury). Builds a consistent mood.
Language Techniques: Hyperbole: what is it, and what's the effect?	Deliberate exaggeration: I've told you a million times. Shows strong feeling or creates humour.
Language Techniques: Juxtaposition: what is it, and what's the effect?	Placing contrasting ideas side by side: a tiny lamp in a huge storm. Highlights the difference.
Language Techniques: Oxymoron: what is it, and what's the effect?	Two opposite words together: deafening silence. Creates tension or shows confused feelings.
Language Techniques: Imagery: what is it, and what's the effect?	Language that appeals to the senses, creating a picture in the reader's mind. Helps the reader feel present.
Language Techniques: Connotation: what does it mean?	The ideas or feelings a word suggests beyond its dictionary meaning: crimson suggests blood, danger or passion.
Language Techniques: Symbolism: what is it?	An object that represents a bigger idea: a lamp in a window can symbolise hope or waiting.
Language Techniques: Foreshadowing: what is it?	Hints about what will happen later, e.g. a dark sky before a tragedy. Builds suspense.
Language Techniques: Emotive language: what is it?	Words chosen to make the reader feel strongly: helpless, innocent, heartbreaking. Common in persuasive texts.
Language Techniques: Rhetorical question: what is it?	A question asked for effect, not an answer: How could anyone ignore this? Makes the reader reflect or agree.
Language Techniques: Direct address: what is it?	Speaking to the reader using you. Makes writing personal and involving.
Language Techniques: Why do verbs matter in analysis?	Strong verbs show force and tone: crept vs. walked. Zooming in on a verb often gives the best analysis.
Language Techniques: Repetition: what is it, and what's the effect?	Repeating a word, phrase or structure for emphasis. It can show obsession, panic or determination.
Language Techniques: Rule of three (triplet): what is it?	Grouping ideas in threes: cold, dark and silent. Sounds complete and memorable and builds rhythm.
Language Techniques: Tone: what is it? Give 4 examples.	The writer's attitude or mood in a text: sarcastic, nostalgic, menacing, celebratory (also: hopeful, bitter, playful).
Sentences & Grammar: Simple sentence: what is it, and what's the effect?	One main clause: She waited. Short simple sentences create tension, shock or emphasis.
Sentences & Grammar: Compound sentence: what is it?	Two main clauses joined by and, but, or, so: The door opened, but nobody came in.
Sentences & Grammar: Complex sentence: what is it, and what's the effect?	A main clause plus a subordinate clause: Although it was late, she kept reading. Adds detail and shows connections.
Sentences & Grammar: Minor sentence: what is it, and what's the effect?	A fragment with no main verb: Silence. Five o'clock. Creates pace, drama or the feeling of time passing.
Sentences & Grammar: Why vary sentence length in your own writing?	It controls pace: long sentences build or overwhelm; short ones shock. Variety is in the top-band criteria for writing.
Sentences & Grammar: Name 4 ways to start a sentence differently.	With an adverb (Slowly,), a verb (Gasping,), a preposition (Beneath the bridge,), or a subordinate clause (As the clock struck,).
Sentences & Grammar: What is a list sentence (asyndetic list) used for?	A list without and: mud, rain, blood, noise. Feels fast, chaotic or overwhelming.
Sentences & Grammar: 1st vs 3rd person narration: effect?	1st (I): personal, we share the narrator's thoughts but may not trust them. 3rd (she/he): can show several viewpoints, feels more distant.
Sentences & Grammar: What is a declarative, imperative and exclamatory sentence?	Declarative: a statement. Imperative: a command (Look out.). Exclamatory: strong emotion (What a disaster!).
Structure: Cyclical structure: what is it, and what's the effect?	The ending returns to the opening image or idea. Creates a sense of completeness, or shows what has changed.
Structure: Flashback: what is it, and what's the effect?	A scene from earlier in time. Explains a character's feelings or backstory and builds empathy.
Structure: Focus shift: what is it?	Moving the reader's attention, e.g. wide to narrow (sky → house → face), outside to inside, or person to person.
Structure: Climax: what is it?	The point of greatest tension or drama in a text.
Structure: Cliffhanger: what is it?	Ending at a moment of suspense, leaving the reader desperate to know what happens.
Structure: What's a one-line paragraph used for?	Isolating a key moment so it stands out, e.g. a shock, a decision or a turning point.
Structure: Name 6 things to look for in a structure question.	Opening, focus shifts, time (flashback, time jumps), perspective, turning point/climax, ending (and links to the start).
Structure: AQA Q3: the beginning–middle–end method.	Three paragraphs in text order: what we focus on at the start and why, how and why the focus shifts in the middle, and how the ending links back.
Structure: What's the difference between language and structure?	Language: word and sentence choices. Structure: the order and organisation of the whole text: what comes when, and why.
Structure: Dialogue in structure: why might a writer place speech at a certain point?	To break up description, reveal character, change pace, or deliver a key piece of information at a dramatic moment.
Structure: What does 'in medias res' mean?	Starting in the middle of the action, with no build-up. Hooks the reader instantly.
Analysis & Evaluation: What–How–Why: what is the analysis method?	WHAT effect does the writer create? HOW (quote and method)? WHY: zoom in on a word and explain its effect on the reader.
Analysis & Evaluation: How long should quotations be?	Short: 1–6 words, embedded in your own sentence. Long quotations waste time and hide your analysis.
Analysis & Evaluation: What does 'zooming in' mean?	Picking one word from a quotation and exploring what it suggests. Top answers give 2–3 possible meanings.
Analysis & Evaluation: Why does feature spotting score low?	Naming a technique ('this is a simile') without explaining its effect shows no analysis. Effect earns the marks.
Analysis & Evaluation: Give 5 strong analysis verbs.	Conveys, implies, evokes, emphasises, foreshadows (also: highlights, reinforces, reveals).
Analysis & Evaluation: Give 3 tentative phrases for exploring meaning.	This could suggest…, Perhaps the writer implies…, Alternatively, it may show…
Analysis & Evaluation: AQA Q4: what is it asking you to do?	Evaluate a statement about the text: say how far you agree, and support your judgement with analysed evidence. Worth 20 marks.
Analysis & Evaluation: Edexcel Q4: what is it asking you to do?	Evaluate how successfully the writer creates an effect (e.g. tension, atmosphere). Judge and analyse, citing evidence. Worth 15 marks.
Analysis & Evaluation: OCR Q4: what is it asking you to do?	Compare both texts against a statement and evaluate how well each writer achieves it. Worth 18 marks.
Analysis & Evaluation: The 4-step evaluation paragraph.	1 Judge (use the statement's words). 2 Evidence. 3 Analyse methods. 4 Evaluate why it's effective.
Analysis & Evaluation: Give 4 judgement phrases for evaluation.	The writer powerfully conveys…, This is particularly effective because…, The most convincing moment is…, I partly agree, because…
Analysis & Evaluation: Give 4 comparison connectives.	Similarly, In contrast, Whereas, Both writers… but…
Analysis & Evaluation: How should you use context in Language papers?	Lightly, if at all. One sentence can support a point, but marks are for analysing the writing.
19th-Century Words: Countenance: what does it mean?	A person's face or expression.
19th-Century Words: Melancholy: what does it mean?	Deep, lasting sadness.
19th-Century Words: Apprehension: what does it mean?	Worry or fear about the future.
19th-Century Words: Composure: what does it mean?	Calmness and self-control.
19th-Century Words: Wretched: what does it mean?	Very unhappy or in a terrible state.
19th-Century Words: Ascertain: what does it mean?	To find out for certain.
19th-Century Words: Hitherto: what does it mean?	Until now.
19th-Century Words: Ere: what does it mean?	Before.
19th-Century Words: Lest: what does it mean?	In case; to avoid the risk that.
19th-Century Words: Whence: what does it mean?	From where.
19th-Century Words: Presently: what does it mean?	Soon (not 'now').
19th-Century Words: Vexed: what does it mean?	Annoyed or frustrated.
19th-Century Words: Gaunt: what does it mean?	Very thin and bony, often from illness or hunger.
19th-Century Words: Desolate: what does it mean?	Empty, bleak and lonely.
19th-Century Words: Forthwith: what does it mean?	Immediately.
19th-Century Words: Sombre: what does it mean?	Dark, serious or gloomy.
19th-Century Words: Ominous: what does it mean?	Giving the feeling that something bad will happen.
19th-Century Words: Spectre: what does it mean?	A ghost; a frightening threat.
19th-Century Words: Name 4 big themes in 19th-century fiction.	Class and poverty, the supernatural and the Gothic, duty and religion, nature and isolation.
19th-Century Words: Tip for long 19th-century sentences.	Split them at semicolons and commas, and find the main verb. The intro above the extract tells you who and where.
Creative Writing: How should you split 45 minutes of writing?	Plan 5 minutes, write 35, check 5.
Creative Writing: The 5-part story plan.	Hook → build-up → turn (problem) → climax (slowed down) → ending (cyclical or quiet twist).
Creative Writing: Description method: drop, zoom, shift.	Drop into a moment, zoom in on one detail, zoom out to the wider scene, shift time or weather, return to the opening detail.
Creative Writing: Give 4 types of strong opening.	One-word sentence (Silence.), dialogue, weather as mood, or a short mystery (Nobody had used the back door for eleven years.)
Creative Writing: What does 'show, don't tell' mean?	Show emotions through actions and details, not labels. Not She was nervous but She twisted her sleeve until the thread snapped.
Creative Writing: Why use all five senses?	Sound, smell, touch and taste make a scene feel real. Most students only use sight.
Creative Writing: What is a motif, and why use one?	A detail that returns through the piece (a ticking clock, a red scarf). It gives structure and a satisfying ending.
Creative Writing: Name 4 endings to avoid.	'It was all a dream', 'Then I woke up', everyone dies, or an ending that just stops mid-action.
Creative Writing: Why write about fewer events?	One moment described in rich detail scores higher than a long plot. Examiners reward crafting, not action.
Creative Writing: How do you set out dialogue?	New speaker, new line. Speech marks around spoken words, punctuation inside: “Wait,” she said.
Creative Writing: How many characters should a GCSE story have?	Ideally 1–3. More characters means less depth and more confusion.
Non-Fiction Writing: Which papers include non-fiction writing?	AQA Paper 2 Q5, Edexcel Paper 2 Q8/9, and OCR Component 01: articles, letters, speeches and essays.
Non-Fiction Writing: Persuasive techniques: what does AFOREST stand for?	Anecdote, Facts, Opinion, Rhetorical questions, Emotive language, Statistics, Triplets (rule of three).
Non-Fiction Writing: Article: key features.	A headline, an optional subheading, a hooking opening, paragraphs, and a strong conclusion.
Non-Fiction Writing: Formal letter: key features.	Address the recipient (Dear Sir/Madam), clear purpose early, formal tone, sign off Yours faithfully (unknown name) or Yours sincerely (named).
Non-Fiction Writing: Speech: key features.	Address the audience directly, use rhetorical questions and repetition, and end with a call to action.
Non-Fiction Writing: What is a counter-argument and why include one?	Stating the opposing view, then rebutting it. It makes your argument sound balanced and convincing.
Non-Fiction Writing: How do you match register to audience?	Formal and polite for headteachers and councils; lively and direct for teenagers. Read the task for the audience.
Punctuation & SPaG: Semicolon (;): when do you use it?	To join two linked complete sentences: The door creaked open; nobody was there.
Punctuation & SPaG: Colon (:): when do you use it?	To introduce a list, explanation or reveal: She knew one thing: she had to run.
Punctuation & SPaG: Dashes for parenthesis: when do you use them?	To add extra information mid-sentence: The house – empty for years – waited.
Punctuation & SPaG: Ellipsis (…): when should you use it?	To show trailing off or suspense. Use it once or twice at most; overuse looks weak.
Punctuation & SPaG: Comma splice: what is it, and how do you fix it?	Two sentences joined by a comma: It was dark, I was scared. Fix with a full stop, a semicolon or a conjunction.
Punctuation & SPaG: Their, there, they're?	Their = belonging to them. There = a place. They're = they are.
Punctuation & SPaG: Your vs you're?	Your = belonging to you. You're = you are.
Punctuation & SPaG: Could of / would of: what's wrong?	It should be could have / would have (or could've).
Punctuation & SPaG: Affect vs effect?	Affect is usually a verb (to influence). Effect is usually a noun (the result). The storm affected her; its effect was terrifying.
Punctuation & SPaG: Its vs it's?	Its = belonging to it. It's = it is / it has.
Punctuation & SPaG: Name 6 commonly misspelt words.	definitely, separate, necessary, beginning, rhythm, embarrass (also: occasion, believe, environment)
Punctuation & SPaG: What are tense slips, and how do you avoid them?	Switching between past and present by accident. Pick a tense (usually past) and check it in your final 5 minutes.
Punctuation & SPaG: What punctuation should a top-band answer show?	A range used accurately: full stops, commas, apostrophes, speech marks, plus some semicolons, colons, dashes and question marks.
