Energy 01.01 – Energy stores and transfers: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Energy store: where energy is held. | The 8 stores: kinetic (moving), gravitational potential (raised up), elastic potential (stretched or squashed), thermal (hot), chemical (food, fuel, batteries), magnetic, electrostatic and nuclear. | Energy transfer: energy moving between stores, by a force doing work (mechanically), electrically, by heating, or by radiation (light, sound). | USE IT WHEN – Describing what happens to energy in any situation. | STEPS – 1. Identify the store the energy starts in. | 2. Identify the store (or stores) it ends up in. | 3. Name how it is transferred: mechanically, electrically, by heating or by radiation. | 4. Write it as: store → (transfer) → store.
Energy 01.01 – Worked example: Describe the energy transfers when a battery-powered torch is switched on.	Starts in the chemical store of the battery | Transferred electrically to the bulb | Bulb transfers energy by light (radiation) and by heating | Ends in the thermal store of the surroundings | Answer: Chemical → electrically → light and thermal
Energy 01.01 – Energy stores and transfers: what is the common mistake?	Calling light or electricity a 'store'. They are ways energy is TRANSFERRED, not stores.
Energy 01.02 – Conservation and dissipation of energy: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Conservation of energy: energy can be transferred, stored or dissipated, but never created or destroyed. | Closed system: no energy enters or leaves, so the total stays the same. | Dissipated (wasted): energy spread out into less useful stores, usually by heating the surroundings. | USE IT WHEN – Explaining where energy goes, and why the total stays the same. | STEPS – 1. Add up the energy at the start. | 2. Identify the useful output and the wasted energy. | 3. Total energy in = useful energy out + wasted energy. | 4. Explain how the waste happens (e.g. friction heating the surroundings).
Energy 01.02 – Worked example: A lamp is supplied with 100 J and gives out 20 J as light. How much is wasted, and where does it go?	Energy in = 100 J | Useful light = 20 J | Wasted = 100 − 20 = 80 J | It heats the lamp and the surroundings (thermal store) | Answer: 80 J, dissipated as heat
Energy 01.02 – Conservation and dissipation of energy: what is the common mistake?	Saying energy is 'used up' or 'lost'. It is never destroyed, only transferred to less useful stores.
Energy 01.03 – Kinetic energy: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Kinetic energy (Ek): energy in the kinetic store of a moving object, in joules (J). | Equation: Ek = ½ m v². m: mass in kilograms (kg). v: speed in metres per second (m/s). Doubling the speed makes the kinetic energy 4 times bigger. | USE IT WHEN – Calculating the kinetic energy of a moving object, or finding its speed or mass. | STEPS – 1. Write the equation: Ek = ½ m v². | 2. Convert units: grams to kg (÷ 1000). | 3. Square the speed first, then multiply by m and halve. | 4. To find v: v = √(2Ek ÷ m).
Energy 01.03 – Worked example: A 60 kg cyclist moves at 5 m/s. Find their kinetic energy.	Ek = ½ × m × v² | v² = 5² = 25 | Ek = ½ × 60 × 25 = 750 | Answer: 750 J
Energy 01.03 – Kinetic energy: what is the common mistake?	Forgetting to square the speed (½ × 60 × 5 = 150 J is wrong).
Energy 01.04 – Gravitational potential energy: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Gravitational potential energy (Ep): energy in the gravitational store of a raised object, in joules (J). | Equation: Ep = m g h. m: mass in kg. g: gravitational field strength (9.8 N/kg on Earth). h: change in height in metres (m). | USE IT WHEN – Calculating the energy gained when something is lifted, or lost when it falls. | STEPS – 1. Write the equation: Ep = m g h. | 2. Convert units: g to kg, cm to m. | 3. Multiply the three values. | 4. Falling object: Ep lost = Ek gained (ignoring air resistance).
Energy 01.04 – Worked example: A 2 kg book is lifted 1.5 m onto a shelf. How much GPE does it gain? (g = 9.8 N/kg)	Ep = m × g × h | = 2 × 9.8 × 1.5 | = 29.4 | Answer: 29.4 J
Energy 01.04 – Gravitational potential energy: what is the common mistake?	Using the total height instead of the CHANGE in height, or forgetting to convert cm to m.
Energy 01.05 – Elastic potential energy: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Elastic potential energy (Ee): energy stored in a stretched or squashed object, in joules (J). | Equation: Ee = ½ k e². k: spring constant in N/m (how stiff the spring is). e: extension in metres = stretched length − original length. | Limit of proportionality: the point beyond which the equation stops working. | USE IT WHEN – Calculating the energy stored in a stretched spring. | STEPS – 1. Find the extension: new length − original length. | 2. Convert it to metres (cm ÷ 100). | 3. Square the extension first, then multiply by k and halve. | 4. Only valid up to the limit of proportionality.
Energy 01.05 – Worked example: A spring (k = 40 N/m) is stretched from 10 cm to 15 cm. Find the energy stored.	Extension = 15 − 10 = 5 cm = 0.05 m | e² = 0.05² = 0.0025 | Ee = ½ × 40 × 0.0025 = 0.05 | Answer: 0.05 J
Energy 01.05 – Elastic potential energy: what is the common mistake?	Using the stretched length (15 cm) instead of the extension (5 cm), or not converting cm to m.
Energy 01.06 – Specific heat capacity: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Specific heat capacity (c): the energy needed to raise 1 kg of a substance by 1 °C, in J/kg °C. Water's is 4200 J/kg °C. | Equation: ΔE = m c Δθ. ΔE: energy transferred in J. m: mass in kg. Δθ: temperature change in °C (final − start). | USE IT WHEN – Calculating the energy needed to heat something, or finding c, m or the temperature rise. | STEPS – 1. Find the temperature change: final − start. | 2. Convert the mass to kg. | 3. Multiply m × c × Δθ. | 4. Rearrange if needed: c = ΔE ÷ (m Δθ), Δθ = ΔE ÷ (m c).
Energy 01.06 – Worked example: How much energy heats 2 kg of water from 20 °C to 70 °C? (c = 4200 J/kg °C)	Δθ = 70 − 20 = 50 °C | ΔE = m × c × Δθ | = 2 × 4200 × 50 = 420 000 | Answer: 420 000 J (420 kJ)
Energy 01.06 – Specific heat capacity: what is the common mistake?	Using the final temperature (70 °C) instead of the temperature CHANGE (50 °C).
Energy 01.07 – Work done and power: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Work done (W): energy transferred when a force moves an object, in joules (J). W = F s, where F is force in newtons (N) and s is distance moved along the line of the force in metres (m). | Power (P): the rate of energy transfer, in watts (W). 1 W = 1 J per second. P = E ÷ t or P = W ÷ t, with t in seconds. | USE IT WHEN – Calculating work done, power, energy or time. | STEPS – 1. Work done: multiply force × distance (in m). | 2. Power: divide energy (or work) by time in SECONDS. | 3. Convert minutes to seconds (× 60), kJ to J (× 1000). | 4. Rearrange: E = P × t, t = E ÷ P.
Energy 01.07 – Worked example: A 600 N force pushes a box 4 m in 8 s. Find the work done and the power.	W = F × s = 600 × 4 = 2400 J | P = W ÷ t = 2400 ÷ 8 = 300 | Answer: 2400 J and 300 W
Energy 01.07 – Work done and power: what is the common mistake?	Leaving time in minutes. Power uses SECONDS: 2 minutes is 120 s.
Energy 01.08 – Efficiency: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Efficiency: how much of the input energy is usefully transferred. Efficiency = useful output ÷ total input (energy or power). It is a decimal up to 1, or × 100 for a percentage. No device is 100% efficient; the rest is wasted, usually as heat. | USE IT WHEN – Calculating efficiency, or the useful or wasted energy. | STEPS – 1. Identify the useful output and the total input. | 2. Divide useful by total (same units on top and bottom). | 3. × 100 for a percentage. | 4. Wasted = total input − useful output.
Energy 01.08 – Worked example: A motor takes in 500 J and transfers 350 J usefully. Find its efficiency and the energy wasted.	Efficiency = 350 ÷ 500 = 0.7 | 0.7 × 100 = 70% | Wasted = 500 − 350 = 150 J | Answer: 70%, 150 J wasted
Energy 01.08 – Efficiency: what is the common mistake?	Dividing the wrong way round (500 ÷ 350 = 1.43). Efficiency can never be more than 1 (100%).
Energy 01.09 – Reducing wasted energy: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Unwanted energy transfer: energy wasted, usually as heat. | Lubrication: oil or grease that reduces friction between moving parts. | Thermal insulation: material that reduces heat loss. | Thermal conductivity: how quickly heat passes through a material by conduction (metals: high; air, foam, wool: low). | USE IT WHEN – Explaining how to reduce wasted energy in machines and homes. | STEPS – 1. Identify where energy is wasted (friction, or heat escaping). | 2. Friction: use lubrication. | 3. Heat loss: use insulation with low thermal conductivity, or make walls thicker. | 4. Explain the effect: less energy is wasted, so efficiency increases.
Energy 01.09 – Worked example: Explain why cavity wall insulation keeps a house warmer.	The foam traps air, which has a low thermal conductivity | Less heat is conducted through the walls | The rate of heat loss decreases | Answer: It reduces the rate of heat loss by conduction
Energy 01.09 – Reducing wasted energy: what is the common mistake?	Saying insulation 'stops the cold getting in'. Insulation reduces HEAT getting out.
Energy 01.10 – Energy resources: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Non-renewable: will run out (fossil fuels: coal, oil, gas; nuclear fuel). | Renewable: can be replenished as it is used (bio-fuel, wind, hydroelectricity, geothermal, tides, solar, water waves). | Reliable: can supply energy whenever it is needed. Fossil fuels release carbon dioxide, which contributes to climate change. | USE IT WHEN – Comparing energy resources for reliability, cost and environmental impact. | STEPS – 1. Say whether the resource is renewable or non-renewable. | 2. Comment on reliability: does it depend on the weather or time of day? | 3. Comment on the environmental impact (CO₂, land use, habitats). | 4. Comment on cost and how quickly it can meet demand.
Energy 01.10 – Worked example: Compare wind power with a gas power station.	Wind: renewable and no CO₂ while running, but unreliable (needs wind) | Gas: non-renewable and releases CO₂, but reliable and can respond quickly to demand | Answer: Wind is cleaner but unreliable; gas is reliable but polluting
Energy 01.10 – Energy resources: what is the common mistake?	Saying renewable resources are always reliable. Wind and solar depend on the weather and time of day.
Electricity 02.01 – Current, charge and time: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Current (I): the rate of flow of electrical charge, in amps (A). | Charge (Q): measured in coulombs (C). | Equation: Q = I t, with time t in seconds. Current is measured with an ammeter placed in series. | USE IT WHEN – Calculating charge, current or time. | STEPS – 1. Write the equation: Q = I t. | 2. Convert time to seconds. | 3. Substitute and calculate. | 4. Rearrange: I = Q ÷ t, t = Q ÷ I.
Electricity 02.01 – Worked example: A current of 2 A flows for 3 minutes. How much charge flows?	t = 3 × 60 = 180 s | Q = I × t = 2 × 180 = 360 | Answer: 360 C
Electricity 02.01 – Current, charge and time: what is the common mistake?	Leaving time in minutes (2 × 3 = 6 C is wrong). Time must be in seconds.
Electricity 02.02 – Potential difference and resistance: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Potential difference (V): the energy transferred per unit of charge, in volts (V), measured with a voltmeter in parallel. | Resistance (R): how much a component opposes the current, in ohms (Ω). | Equation: V = I R. | USE IT WHEN – Calculating pd, current or resistance. | STEPS – 1. Write V = I R and cover the one you want: R = V ÷ I, I = V ÷ R. | 2. Convert kΩ to Ω (× 1000) and mA to A (÷ 1000). | 3. Substitute and calculate. | 4. Give the unit.
Electricity 02.02 – Worked example: A 6 V battery drives a current of 0.5 A through a resistor. Find its resistance.	R = V ÷ I | = 6 ÷ 0.5 = 12 | Answer: 12 Ω
Electricity 02.02 – Potential difference and resistance: what is the common mistake?	Not converting mA to A: 200 mA is 0.2 A, not 200 A.
Electricity 02.03 – Series and parallel circuits: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Series circuit: one loop; components one after another. | Parallel circuit: separate branches. | In series: the current is the same everywhere, the pd is shared, and total resistance = R1 + R2. | In parallel: the pd across each branch is the same, the currents in the branches add up to the total, and total resistance is LESS than the smallest resistor. | USE IT WHEN – Working out currents, pds and resistances in series and parallel circuits. | STEPS – 1. Decide: series or parallel? | 2. Series: add resistances; the supply pd is shared between components. | 3. Parallel: each branch gets the full supply pd; branch currents add up. | 4. Use V = I R for each component.
Electricity 02.03 – Worked example: A 4 Ω and a 2 Ω resistor are in series with a 12 V battery. Find the current and the pd across each resistor.	Total R = 4 + 2 = 6 Ω | I = V ÷ R = 12 ÷ 6 = 2 A | pd across 4 Ω: 2 × 4 = 8 V | pd across 2 Ω: 2 × 2 = 4 V (8 + 4 = 12 ✓) | Answer: 2 A; 8 V and 4 V
Electricity 02.03 – Series and parallel circuits: what is the common mistake?	Thinking adding a resistor in parallel increases the total resistance. It DECREASES it.
Electricity 02.04 – I–V characteristics: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – I–V characteristic: a graph of current against potential difference. Ohmic conductor (fixed resistor at constant temperature): a straight line through the origin, so resistance is constant. | Filament lamp: a curve, because resistance increases as it heats up. | Diode: lets current flow in one direction only. | USE IT WHEN – Describing and explaining I–V graphs. | STEPS – 1. Straight line through the origin: current ∝ pd, so resistance is constant. | 2. Curve getting flatter: resistance is increasing (e.g. filament lamp). | 3. Current only in one direction: a diode. | 4. Explain using temperature: hotter metal → higher resistance.
Electricity 02.04 – Worked example: Explain the shape of a filament lamp's I–V graph.	As the current increases, the filament gets hotter | The metal ions vibrate more, so resistance increases | So the line curves and gets flatter | Answer: Resistance increases as it heats up, so the graph curves
Electricity 02.04 – I–V characteristics: what is the common mistake?	Saying the filament lamp is ohmic. Its resistance changes with temperature, so it is not.
Electricity 02.05 – Thermistors and LDRs: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Thermistor: a resistor whose resistance DECREASES as temperature increases (used in thermostats and fire alarms). | LDR (light-dependent resistor): resistance DECREASES as light intensity increases (used in automatic night lights). | Sensor: a component that responds to a change in conditions. | USE IT WHEN – Explaining how sensing circuits respond to temperature and light. | STEPS – 1. Identify the sensor: thermistor (temperature) or LDR (light). | 2. Say how its resistance changes. | 3. Lower resistance means a bigger current (V = I R). | 4. Link this to what the circuit does (e.g. a light switches on).
Electricity 02.05 – Worked example: An LDR is in a circuit with a lamp. What happens to its resistance and the current as it gets dark?	Darker means lower light intensity | The LDR's resistance increases | So the current in that part of the circuit decreases | Answer: Resistance increases, current decreases
Electricity 02.05 – Thermistors and LDRs: what is the common mistake?	Getting the direction backwards: thermistor and LDR resistance both go DOWN as temperature or light goes UP.
Electricity 02.06 – Mains electricity and plugs: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Direct current (dc): flows in one direction only (batteries). | Alternating current (ac): keeps changing direction. | UK mains: ac at about 230 V and 50 Hz. | Plug wires: live (brown, 230 V), neutral (blue, about 0 V), earth (green and yellow, 0 V, a safety wire that only carries current if there is a fault). | USE IT WHEN – Questions on mains electricity and plug safety. | STEPS – 1. Mains is ac at 230 V and 50 Hz. | 2. Live carries the current in; neutral completes the circuit. | 3. Earth protects you if a metal case becomes live. | 4. Touching the live wire is dangerous because there is a large pd between it and your body (0 V).
Electricity 02.06 – Worked example: Why is touching the live wire dangerous, even if the switch is off?	The live wire is still at 230 V | Your body is at 0 V | The large pd drives a current through you to earth | Answer: A large current could flow through you
Electricity 02.06 – Mains electricity and plugs: what is the common mistake?	Thinking the switch being off makes the live wire safe. It can still be at 230 V.
Electricity 02.07 – Electrical power and energy: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Electrical power (P): energy transferred per second, in watts (W). P = V I, or P = I² R. | Energy transferred (E): in joules (J). E = P t (t in seconds), or E = Q V. 1 kW = 1000 W. | USE IT WHEN – Calculating the power of an appliance or the energy it transfers. | STEPS – 1. Choose the equation that uses the values you know. | 2. P = V I with pd and current; P = I² R with current and resistance. | 3. E = P t: convert time to seconds and kW to W. | 4. Rearrange if needed.
Electricity 02.07 – Worked example: A 2 kW heater is on for 30 minutes. How much energy does it transfer?	P = 2 kW = 2000 W | t = 30 × 60 = 1800 s | E = P × t = 2000 × 1800 = 3 600 000 | Answer: 3 600 000 J (3.6 MJ)
Electricity 02.07 – Electrical power and energy: what is the common mistake?	Mixing units: using 2 (kW) with seconds, or 2000 (W) with minutes.
Electricity 02.08 – The National Grid: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – National Grid: the system of cables and transformers linking power stations to homes. | Step-up transformer: increases the pd and decreases the current for transmission. | Step-down transformer: decreases the pd to a safe 230 V for homes. Lower current means less energy lost by heating the cables. | USE IT WHEN – Explaining why the National Grid uses transformers. | STEPS – 1. Power stations generate electricity. | 2. Step-up transformers raise the pd so the current is low. | 3. Low current means less energy is wasted heating the cables, so it is more efficient. | 4. Step-down transformers lower the pd to a safe level for homes.
Electricity 02.08 – Worked example: Why is electricity sent across the country at a very high voltage?	P = V I: for the same power, a higher V means a lower I | A lower current heats the cables less | So less energy is wasted | Answer: To reduce the current and so reduce energy losses
Electricity 02.08 – The National Grid: what is the common mistake?	Saying high voltage 'makes the electricity travel faster'. It reduces the CURRENT, so less energy is lost as heat.
Electricity 02.09 – Static electricity: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Static electricity: charge built up on an insulator. Rubbing transfers electrons (negative) from one material to the other. Gaining electrons makes an object negative; losing them makes it positive. Like charges repel; unlike charges attract. | Electric field: the region around a charge where another charge feels a force (Triple). | USE IT WHEN – Explaining how objects become charged and how they affect each other. | STEPS – 1. Rubbing transfers ELECTRONS (only electrons move). | 2. Gained electrons → negative; lost electrons → positive. | 3. Like charges repel; opposite charges attract. | 4. A big enough pd can make charge jump across a gap (a spark).
Electricity 02.09 – Worked example: A polythene rod is rubbed with a cloth and becomes negative. Explain why, and what charge the cloth has.	Electrons move from the cloth to the rod | The rod gains electrons, so it becomes negative | The cloth loses electrons, so it becomes positive | Answer: Electrons transferred; the cloth is positive
Electricity 02.09 – Static electricity: what is the common mistake?	Saying positive charges (protons) move. Only ELECTRONS are transferred.
Particle Model 03.01 – Density: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Density (ρ): mass per unit volume. | Equation: ρ = m ÷ V. | Units: kg/m³ (or g/cm³). 1 g/cm³ = 1000 kg/m³. Objects less dense than water (1 g/cm³) float. | USE IT WHEN – Calculating density, mass or volume. | STEPS – 1. Write the equation: ρ = m ÷ V. | 2. Check the units match (g with cm³, or kg with m³). | 3. Substitute and calculate. | 4. Rearrange: m = ρ × V, V = m ÷ ρ.
Particle Model 03.01 – Worked example: A block has mass 270 g and volume 100 cm³. Find its density in g/cm³ and kg/m³.	ρ = m ÷ V = 270 ÷ 100 = 2.7 g/cm³ | × 1000: 2700 kg/m³ | Answer: 2.7 g/cm³ = 2700 kg/m³
Particle Model 03.01 – Density: what is the common mistake?	Mixing units, e.g. kg with cm³, which gives a meaningless number.
Particle Model 03.02 – States of matter: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Solid: particles close together in a regular pattern, vibrating in place. | Liquid: close together but random, able to move past each other. | Gas: far apart, random, moving quickly. | Changes of state: melting, freezing, boiling (evaporating), condensing, sublimating. | A change of state is a physical change: no new substance, and mass is conserved. | USE IT WHEN – Describing particles in solids, liquids and gases, and changes of state. | STEPS – 1. Describe the arrangement: regular or random, close or far apart. | 2. Describe the movement: vibrate, slide past, or move quickly. | 3. Name the change of state. | 4. State that mass is conserved and the change is reversible.
Particle Model 03.02 – Worked example: Explain why a gas is less dense than the same substance as a liquid.	Same mass of particles | In a gas the particles are much further apart | So the same mass takes up a bigger volume | Answer: Bigger volume for the same mass, so lower density
Particle Model 03.02 – States of matter: what is the common mistake?	Saying particles get bigger when heated. The particles stay the same size; they move faster and further apart.
Particle Model 03.03 – Internal energy and latent heat: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Internal energy: the total kinetic and potential energy of all the particles. Heating raises the temperature OR changes the state. | Specific latent heat (L): the energy to change the state of 1 kg with no temperature change, in J/kg. | Equation: E = m L. | Fusion: solid ↔ liquid. | Vaporisation: liquid ↔ gas. | USE IT WHEN – Calculating the energy to change state, and explaining heating curves. | STEPS – 1. Write the equation: E = m L. | 2. Convert the mass to kg. | 3. Use the right L: fusion for melting, vaporisation for boiling. | 4. On a heating curve, flat sections are changes of state.
Particle Model 03.03 – Worked example: How much energy melts 0.5 kg of ice at 0 °C? (L of fusion = 334 000 J/kg)	E = m × L | = 0.5 × 334 000 | = 167 000 | Answer: 167 000 J
Particle Model 03.03 – Internal energy and latent heat: what is the common mistake?	Using E = m c Δθ during a change of state. The temperature doesn't change then, so use E = m L.
Particle Model 03.04 – Gas pressure: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Gas pressure: caused by particles colliding with the container walls and exerting a force. | Higher temperature (fixed volume): particles move faster, so they hit the walls more often and harder, and pressure rises. | For a fixed mass at constant temperature: p V = constant, so if the volume halves, the pressure doubles (Triple). | USE IT WHEN – Explaining gas pressure and using p V = constant. | STEPS – 1. Pressure comes from particles hitting the walls. | 2. More collisions per second, or harder collisions, mean more pressure. | 3. Constant temperature: p₁V₁ = p₂V₂. | 4. Rearrange to find the missing value.
Particle Model 03.04 – Worked example: A gas at 100 kPa is squashed from 3 m³ to 1.5 m³ at constant temperature. Find the new pressure.	p₁V₁ = p₂V₂ | 100 × 3 = p₂ × 1.5 | p₂ = 300 ÷ 1.5 = 200 | Answer: 200 kPa
Particle Model 03.04 – Gas pressure: what is the common mistake?	Saying the particles hit the walls harder when the volume decreases. They hit more OFTEN; their speed only changes with temperature.
Atomic Structure 04.01 – The structure of the atom: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Atom: radius about 1 × 10⁻¹⁰ m. | Nucleus: tiny and positive, made of protons and neutrons; less than 1/10 000 of the atom's radius. | Electrons: negative, orbit the nucleus in energy levels (shells). | Proton: charge +1, mass 1. | Neutron: charge 0, mass 1. | Electron: charge −1, very small mass. | USE IT WHEN – Describing the structure of an atom and the particles in it. | STEPS – 1. Nucleus in the centre: protons (+) and neutrons (0). | 2. Electrons (−) orbit in shells. | 3. An atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons, so it is neutral overall. | 4. Almost all the mass is in the nucleus.
Atomic Structure 04.01 – Worked example: An atom has 6 protons and 6 neutrons. How many electrons does it have, and what is its overall charge?	A neutral atom has equal protons and electrons | So 6 electrons | +6 − 6 = 0 | Answer: 6 electrons; no overall charge
Atomic Structure 04.01 – The structure of the atom: what is the common mistake?	Putting electrons in the nucleus. Electrons orbit OUTSIDE the nucleus.
Atomic Structure 04.02 – Atomic number, isotopes and ions: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Atomic number: the number of protons. | Mass number: protons + neutrons. Neutrons = mass number − atomic number. | Isotope: same element (same protons), different number of neutrons. | Ion: an atom that has gained or lost electrons, so it is charged. Absorbing EM radiation moves an electron to a higher energy level; emitting it moves it lower. | USE IT WHEN – Working out the particles in an atom, and explaining isotopes and ions. | STEPS – 1. Protons = atomic number (the smaller number). | 2. Neutrons = mass number − atomic number. | 3. Electrons = protons in a neutral atom. | 4. Isotopes: compare neutrons. Ions: compare electrons.
Atomic Structure 04.02 – Worked example: Carbon-14 has atomic number 6 and mass number 14. How many protons, neutrons and electrons?	Protons = 6 | Neutrons = 14 − 6 = 8 | Electrons = 6 (neutral atom) | Answer: 6 protons, 8 neutrons, 6 electrons
Atomic Structure 04.02 – Atomic number, isotopes and ions: what is the common mistake?	Thinking isotopes have different numbers of protons. Then they would be a different element.
Atomic Structure 04.03 – Models of the atom: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Plum pudding model (Thomson): a ball of positive charge with electrons embedded in it. | Alpha scattering experiment (Rutherford): alpha particles were fired at gold foil. | Nuclear model: a tiny, dense, positive nucleus with electrons around it. | Bohr: electrons orbit in fixed energy levels. | Chadwick: discovered the neutron. | USE IT WHEN – Explaining how the model of the atom changed and why. | STEPS – 1. Order: solid sphere → plum pudding → nuclear model → Bohr's energy levels → neutrons. | 2. Alpha scattering: most went straight through → mostly empty space. | 3. Some deflected → the nucleus is positive. | 4. A few bounced back → the mass is concentrated in a tiny nucleus.
Atomic Structure 04.03 – Worked example: Why did a few alpha particles bounce straight back from the gold foil?	They hit the nucleus almost head-on | The nucleus is positive, so it repelled the positive alpha particles | It is very dense, holding most of the mass | Answer: The mass and positive charge are concentrated in a tiny nucleus
Atomic Structure 04.03 – Models of the atom: what is the common mistake?	Saying the plum pudding model was proved right. Alpha scattering DISPROVED it.
Atomic Structure 04.04 – Radioactive decay and activity: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Radioactive decay: a random process where an unstable nucleus gives out radiation to become more stable. | Random: you can't predict which nucleus decays next. | Activity: the rate of decay, in becquerels (Bq): 1 Bq = 1 decay per second. | Count rate: decays recorded per second by a detector (e.g. a Geiger–Müller tube). | USE IT WHEN – Explaining radioactive decay and activity. | STEPS – 1. Unstable nuclei decay randomly to become more stable. | 2. Activity = decays per second, measured in Bq. | 3. A detector measures the count rate, which is lower than the activity. | 4. Subtract background radiation from measured counts.
Atomic Structure 04.04 – Worked example: A detector records 1200 counts in 1 minute. Find the count rate in counts per second.	1 minute = 60 s | Count rate = 1200 ÷ 60 = 20 | Answer: 20 counts per second
Atomic Structure 04.04 – Radioactive decay and activity: what is the common mistake?	Saying decay can be sped up by heating. Radioactive decay is random and not affected by temperature.
Atomic Structure 04.05 – Alpha, beta and gamma: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Alpha (α): 2 protons + 2 neutrons (a helium nucleus). Range a few cm in air; stopped by paper; strongly ionising. | Beta (β): a fast electron from the nucleus (a neutron turns into a proton). Stopped by a few mm of aluminium; moderately ionising. | Gamma (γ): an electromagnetic wave. Stopped by thick lead or concrete; weakly ionising. | Ionising: knocking electrons off atoms. | USE IT WHEN – Comparing alpha, beta and gamma radiation, and choosing one for a use. | STEPS – 1. Identify what each one is. | 2. Penetration: alpha (least) < beta < gamma (most). | 3. Ionising power: alpha (most) > beta > gamma (least). | 4. Match to the use, e.g. paper thickness gauge: beta.

Atomic Structure 04.05 – Alpha, beta and gamma: what is the common mistake?	Mixing up the order: alpha is the MOST ionising but the LEAST penetrating.
Atomic Structure 04.06 – Nuclear equations: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Nuclear equation: shows a decay, with mass numbers on top and atomic numbers below each symbol. | Alpha decay: mass number −4, atomic number −2 (emits ⁴₂He). | Beta decay: mass number unchanged, atomic number +1 (emits ⁰₋₁e). | Gamma emission: no change to either number; the nucleus just loses energy. | USE IT WHEN – Completing and balancing nuclear equations. | STEPS – 1. Write the starting nucleus with both numbers. | 2. Alpha: subtract 4 from the mass number and 2 from the atomic number. | 3. Beta: atomic number goes up by 1; mass number stays the same. | 4. Check: the top numbers balance on both sides, and so do the bottom numbers.
Atomic Structure 04.06 – Worked example: Uranium-238 (atomic number 92) undergoes alpha decay. Find the new mass and atomic numbers.	Mass number: 238 − 4 = 234 | Atomic number: 92 − 2 = 90 | Check: 234 + 4 = 238 ✓ and 90 + 2 = 92 ✓ | Answer: Mass number 234, atomic number 90 (thorium)
Atomic Structure 04.06 – Nuclear equations: what is the common mistake?	Changing the mass number in beta decay. Beta decay keeps the mass number the same.
Atomic Structure 04.07 – Half-life: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Half-life: the time for the number of unstable nuclei in a sample (or the count rate) to halve. Each half-life, the activity halves. Number of half-lives = time ÷ half-life. | Net decline: the amount it has fallen, often given as a fraction of the starting value (Higher). | USE IT WHEN – Calculating activity after a time, or finding the half-life. | STEPS – 1. Work out how many half-lives have passed (time ÷ half-life). | 2. Halve the starting value that many times. | 3. To find the half-life from a graph, read the time for the value to halve. | 4. Net decline = start − end; as a ratio, ÷ start.

Atomic Structure 04.07 – Half-life: what is the common mistake?	Dividing by the number of half-lives (640 ÷ 4 = 160). You HALVE once for each half-life.
Atomic Structure 04.08 – Irradiation, contamination and background radiation: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Irradiation: being exposed to radiation from outside; the object does NOT become radioactive. | Contamination: radioactive atoms get onto or into the object, so it keeps being exposed. | Background radiation: radiation around us all the time (radon gas from rocks, cosmic rays, medical sources, fallout). | Peer review: other scientists checking published findings. | USE IT WHEN – Comparing the risks of irradiation and contamination, and explaining safety precautions. | STEPS – 1. Irradiation stops when you move away from the source. | 2. Contamination is more dangerous because the source stays with you. | 3. Atomic Structure 04.08 – Worked example: Why is swallowing an alpha source more dangerous than standing near one?	Outside the body, alpha is stopped by skin or a few cm of air | Inside, it is right next to cells | It is strongly ionising, so it damages cells | Answer: Inside the body, alpha's strong ionisation damages nearby cells
Atomic Structure 04.08 – Irradiation, contamination and background radiation: what is the common mistake?	Thinking irradiated objects become radioactive. Only contamination leaves radioactive atoms behind.
Atomic Structure 04.09 – Fission, fusion and medical uses: key words and steps? Atomic Structure 04.09 – Worked example: Explain how a nuclear reactor stops a chain reaction getting out of control. Forces & Motion 05.01 – Scalars, vectors and types of force: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Scalar: has size (magnitude) only (distance, speed, mass, energy, time). | Vector: has size AND direction (displacement, velocity, force, acceleration, momentum). | Contact force: objects must touch (friction, air resistance, tension, normal contact force). | Non-contact force: acts at a distance (gravity, magnetic, electrostatic). | USE IT WHEN – Classifying quantities and forces. | STEPS – 1. Ask: does it need a direction to make sense? | 2. Yes: vector. No: scalar. | 3. Forces: do the objects need to touch? | 4. Yes: contact. No: non-contact.
Forces & Motion 05.01 – Worked example: Classify speed, velocity, friction and weight.	Speed: scalar (no direction) | Velocity: vector (speed in a direction) | Friction: contact force | Weight: non-contact force (gravity) | Answer: Speed scalar; velocity vector; friction contact; weight non-contact
Forces & Motion 05.01 – Scalars, vectors and types of force: what is the common mistake?	Thinking speed and velocity are the same. Velocity has a direction; speed doesn't.
Forces & Motion 05.02 – Weight and mass: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Mass: the amount of matter in an object, in kg; the same everywhere. | Weight: the force of gravity on an object, in newtons (N); it depends on the gravitational field strength. | Equation: W = m g. g: gravitational field strength (9.8 N/kg on Earth; about 1.6 N/kg on the Moon). | USE IT WHEN – Calculating weight or mass, and explaining the difference. | STEPS – 1. Write W = m g. | 2. Convert the mass to kg. | 3. Use the g value for the planet in the question. | 4. Rearrange: m = W ÷ g.
Forces & Motion 05.02 – Worked example: An astronaut has mass 80 kg. Find their weight on Earth (g = 9.8 N/kg) and on the Moon (g = 1.6 N/kg).	Earth: W = 80 × 9.8 = 784 N | Moon: W = 80 × 1.6 = 128 N | The mass stays 80 kg on both | Answer: 784 N on Earth, 128 N on the Moon
Forces & Motion 05.02 – Weight and mass: what is the common mistake?	Giving weight in kg. Weight is a force, so it is measured in NEWTONS.
Forces & Motion 05.03 – Resultant forces: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Resultant force: the single force with the same effect as all the forces acting together. | Same direction: add the forces. | Opposite directions: subtract (the resultant is in the direction of the bigger force). | Balanced forces: the resultant is 0 N. Work done against friction transfers energy to thermal stores. | USE IT WHEN – Finding the resultant force and describing its effect. | STEPS – 1. Draw arrows for each force. | 2. Add forces in the same direction; subtract opposite ones. | 3. Give the size AND direction of the resultant. | 4. Resultant 0: steady speed or stationary. Not 0: it accelerates.
Forces & Motion 05.03 – Worked example: A car has 3000 N of driving force and 1200 N of air resistance. Find the resultant force.	The forces are in opposite directions, so subtract | 3000 − 1200 = 1800 N | In the direction of the driving force (forwards) | Answer: 1800 N forwards
Forces & Motion 05.03 – Resultant forces: what is the common mistake?	Adding forces that act in opposite directions. Opposite forces SUBTRACT.
Forces & Motion 05.04 – Hooke's law and springs: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Hooke's law: the extension of a spring is directly proportional to the force applied, up to the limit of proportionality. | Equation: F = k e. k: spring constant in N/m (bigger k = stiffer spring). e: extension in metres. | Elastic deformation: returns to its original shape. | Inelastic: stays deformed. | USE IT WHEN – Calculating force, extension or spring constant. | STEPS – 1. Find the extension: new length − original length. | 2. Convert to metres. | 3. Use F = k e. | 4. Rearrange: k = F ÷ e, e = F ÷ k.
Forces & Motion 05.04 – Worked example: A 6 N force stretches a spring from 20 cm to 23 cm. Find the spring constant.	e = 23 − 20 = 3 cm = 0.03 m | k = F ÷ e = 6 ÷ 0.03 = 200 | Answer: 200 N/m
Forces & Motion 05.04 – Hooke's law and springs: what is the common mistake?	Using the total length instead of the extension, or forgetting to convert cm to m.
Forces & Motion 05.05 – Moments: key words and steps? [Triple]	KEY WORDS – Moment: the turning effect of a force, in newton metres (N m). | Equation: M = F d. d: the PERPENDICULAR distance from the pivot to the line of the force, in m. | Principle of moments: when balanced, total clockwise moments = total anticlockwise moments. | USE IT WHEN – Calculating moments and balancing problems (Triple). | STEPS – 1. Find the perpendicular distance from the pivot. | 2. Moment = force × distance. | 3. Balanced: clockwise moments = anticlockwise moments. | 4. Solve for the unknown force or distance.
Forces & Motion 05.05 – Worked example: A 400 N child sits 1.5 m from the pivot of a seesaw. Where must a 600 N adult sit to balance it? [Triple]	Child's moment: 400 × 1.5 = 600 N m | Balanced: 600 × d = 600 | d = 1 m | Answer: 1 m from the pivot
Forces & Motion 05.05 – Moments: what is the common mistake? [Triple]	Using the distance along a slanted line instead of the PERPENDICULAR distance from the pivot.
Forces & Motion 05.06 – Pressure and upthrust: key words and steps? [Triple]	KEY WORDS – Pressure: force per unit area, in pascals (Pa). | On a surface: p = F ÷ A, with area in m². | In a liquid: p = h ρ g (Higher), where h is depth in m, ρ is density in kg/m³ and g = 9.8 N/kg. Pressure increases with depth. | Upthrust: the upward force from a fluid; an object floats when its weight equals the upthrust. | USE IT WHEN – Calculating pressure, and explaining upthrust and floating (Triple). | STEPS – 1. Surface: p = F ÷ A (convert cm² to m² by ÷ 10 000). | 2. Liquid: p = h ρ g. | 3. Deeper means more pressure. | 4. Upthrust: pressure on the bottom of an object is greater than on the top.
Forces & Motion 05.06 – Worked example: Find the pressure from the water at a depth of 5 m. (ρ = 1000 kg/m³, g = 9.8 N/kg) [Triple]	p = h × ρ × g | = 5 × 1000 × 9.8 | = 49 000 | Answer: 49 000 Pa
Forces & Motion 05.06 – Pressure and upthrust: what is the common mistake? [Triple]	Forgetting to convert cm² to m². 1 m² = 10 000 cm².
Forces & Motion 05.07 – Speed and acceleration: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Speed: distance ÷ time (v = s ÷ t), in m/s. | Velocity: speed in a given direction. | Acceleration: the change in velocity per second, a = Δv ÷ t, in m/s². Δv = final velocity − initial velocity. | For uniform acceleration: v² − u² = 2 a s (u = initial velocity, v = final velocity, s = distance). | Typical speeds: walking 1.5 m/s, running 3 m/s, cycling 6 m/s, sound 330 m/s. | USE IT WHEN – Calculating speed, acceleration and distances in motion questions. | STEPS – 1. List what you know: s, u, v, a, t. | 2. Pick the equation that uses them. | 3. Starting from rest means u = 0. | 4. Substitute, rearrange and calculate.
Forces & Motion 05.07 – Worked example: A cyclist speeds up from 2 m/s to 8 m/s in 3 s. Find the acceleration.	Δv = 8 − 2 = 6 m/s | a = Δv ÷ t = 6 ÷ 3 = 2 | Answer: 2 m/s²
Forces & Motion 05.07 – Speed and acceleration: what is the common mistake?	Using the final velocity instead of the CHANGE in velocity (8 ÷ 3).
Forces & Motion 05.08 – Motion graphs: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Distance–time graph: its gradient = speed; flat = stationary; curved = changing speed. | Velocity–time graph: its gradient = acceleration; area under the graph = distance travelled. | Free fall near Earth: acceleration about 9.8 m/s². | USE IT WHEN – Reading speed, acceleration and distance from motion graphs. | STEPS – 1. Check the axes: distance–time or velocity–time? | 2. Gradient = change in y ÷ change in x. | 3. Distance–time gradient = speed; velocity–time gradient = acceleration. | 4. Velocity–time area (rectangles and triangles) = distance.
Forces & Motion 05.08 – Worked example: A velocity–time graph rises in a straight line from 0 to 10 m/s in 5 s. Find the acceleration and distance.	Gradient = 10 ÷ 5 = 2 m/s² | Area of triangle = ½ × 5 × 10 = 25 m | Answer: 2 m/s² and 25 m
Forces & Motion 05.08 – Motion graphs: what is the common mistake?	Reading a flat line on a velocity–time graph as 'stopped'. It means a constant VELOCITY.
Forces & Motion 05.09 – Terminal velocity: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Terminal velocity: the maximum steady speed of a falling object, reached when air resistance equals weight. | Air resistance (drag): increases with speed. | Resultant force zero: no acceleration, so constant speed. A parachute increases air resistance, so the terminal velocity is lower. | USE IT WHEN – Explaining how a falling object reaches terminal velocity. | STEPS – 1. At first, weight > air resistance, so it accelerates. | 2. As speed increases, air resistance increases. | 3. The resultant force gets smaller, so the acceleration decreases. | 4. When air resistance = weight, the resultant is 0: terminal velocity.
Forces & Motion 05.09 – Worked example: Explain why a skydiver slows down when the parachute opens.	The parachute increases the surface area | Air resistance becomes bigger than weight | The resultant force is upwards, so they decelerate until the forces balance again | Answer: Air resistance exceeds weight, so they slow to a new, lower terminal velocity
Forces & Motion 05.09 – Terminal velocity: what is the common mistake?	Saying the skydiver moves upwards when the parachute opens. They keep falling, just more slowly.
Forces & Motion 05.10 – Newton's laws: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Newton's first law: if the resultant force is zero, an object stays still or keeps moving at the same velocity. | Second law: F = m a (force in N, mass in kg, acceleration in m/s²). | Third law: when two objects interact, they exert equal and opposite forces on each other. | Inertia: the tendency to stay at rest or keep moving at a constant velocity (Higher). | USE IT WHEN – Applying Newton's laws, especially F = m a. | STEPS – 1. Find the RESULTANT force first. | 2. Use F = m a (rearrange: a = F ÷ m, m = F ÷ a). | 3. Zero resultant: no change in motion (first law). | 4. Third-law pairs act on DIFFERENT objects.
Forces & Motion 05.10 – Worked example: A 1500 kg car has a resultant force of 4500 N. Find its acceleration.	a = F ÷ m | = 4500 ÷ 1500 = 3 | Answer: 3 m/s²
Forces & Motion 05.10 – Newton's laws: what is the common mistake?	Thinking third-law pairs cancel out. They act on different objects, so they can't cancel.
Forces & Motion 05.11 – Stopping distances: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Thinking distance: travelled during the reaction time (affected by tiredness, drugs, alcohol, distractions, and speed). | Braking distance: travelled while braking (affected by speed, wet or icy roads, worn brakes or tyres, and mass). | Typical reaction time: 0.2 to 0.9 s. Braking transfers kinetic energy to thermal energy in the brakes. | USE IT WHEN – Explaining and calculating stopping distances. | STEPS – 1. Thinking distance = speed × reaction time. | 2. Braking distance depends on the car and the road. | 3. Add them for the stopping distance. | 4. Say which factors affect which part.
Forces & Motion 05.11 – Worked example: A car travels at 20 m/s and the driver's reaction time is 0.7 s. Find the thinking distance.	Thinking distance = speed × reaction time | = 20 × 0.7 = 14 | Answer: 14 m
Forces & Motion 05.11 – Stopping distances: what is the common mistake?	Saying a wet road increases the thinking distance. It affects the BRAKING distance; the driver's reactions are unchanged.
Forces & Motion 05.12 – Momentum: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Momentum (p): mass × velocity, p = m v, in kg m/s (Higher). | Conservation of momentum: in a closed system, total momentum before = total momentum after. | Force = rate of change of momentum: F = m Δv ÷ Δt (Triple). Safety features (airbags, seat belts, crumple zones) increase the time for momentum to change, which reduces the force. | USE IT WHEN – Calculating momentum and using conservation of momentum. | STEPS – 1. Momentum = mass × velocity; give one direction as negative. | 2. Total momentum before = total momentum after. | 3. Solve for the unknown mass or velocity. | 4. Force: change in momentum ÷ time.
Forces & Motion 05.12 – Worked example: A 2 kg trolley at 3 m/s hits a stationary 1 kg trolley and they move off together. Find their speed.	Momentum before = 2 × 3 + 1 × 0 = 6 kg m/s | After: (2 + 1) × v = 6 | v = 6 ÷ 3 = 2 | Answer: 2 m/s
Forces & Motion 05.12 – Momentum: what is the common mistake?	Forgetting the direction: momenta in opposite directions must have opposite signs.
Forces & Motion 05.13 – Circular motion: key words and steps? [Higher]	KEY WORDS – Circular motion: an object moving in a circle at constant speed is accelerating, because its direction (and so its velocity) keeps changing. | Centripetal force: the resultant force towards the centre of the circle that causes this (e.g. gravity for orbits, tension for a ball on a string). | USE IT WHEN – Explaining why circular motion means acceleration (Higher). | STEPS – 1. A resultant force towards the centre causes this.
Forces & Motion 05.13 – Worked example: A ball is swung in a circle on a string at a steady speed. Is it accelerating? What provides the force? Forces & Motion 05.13 – Circular motion: what is the common mistake? [Higher]	Saying constant speed means no acceleration. Changing DIRECTION is also acceleration.

Waves 06.01 – Transverse and longitudinal waves: what is the common mistake?	Thinking the water travels with the wave. Only the ENERGY moves along.
Waves 06.02 – Describing waves: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Amplitude: the maximum displacement of a point from its rest position. | Wavelength (λ): the distance from one point on a wave to the same point on the next wave, in m. | Frequency (f): the number of waves passing a point each second, in hertz (Hz). | Period (T): the time for one wave, in s. T = 1 ÷ f. | USE IT WHEN – Labelling waves and calculating period or frequency. | STEPS – 1. Amplitude: rest line to peak (not peak to trough). | 2. Waves 06.02 – Worked example: A wave has a frequency of 25 Hz. Find its period.	T = 1 ÷ f | = 1 ÷ 25 = 0.04 | Answer: 0.04 s
Waves 06.02 – Describing waves: what is the common mistake?	Measuring amplitude from peak to trough. That is TWICE the amplitude.
Waves 06.03 – Wave speed: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Wave speed (v): how fast the wave travels, in m/s. | Wave equation: v = f λ, where f is frequency in Hz and λ is wavelength in m. Convert units: kHz to Hz (× 1000), cm to m (÷ 100). | 3. Substitute and calculate. | 4. Rearrange: f = v ÷ λ, λ = v ÷ f.

Waves 06.10 – Sound, ultrasound and seismic waves: what is the common mistake? [Triple]	Forgetting to halve. The echo travels to the boundary AND back.
Magnetism & Electromagnetism 07.01 – Magnets and magnetic fields: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Magnetic poles: north and south; the force is strongest at the poles. Like poles repel; unlike poles attract. | Permanent magnet: has its own magnetic field. | Induced magnet: becomes magnetic only in a magnetic field, and is always attracted. | Magnetic materials: iron, steel, cobalt and nickel. Field lines go from north to south. | USE IT WHEN – Describing magnets, magnetic materials and magnetic fields. | STEPS – 1. Like poles repel; unlike poles attract. | 2. Induced magnets are always ATTRACTED, never repelled. | 3. Field lines point from N to S; closer lines mean a stronger field. | 4. A compass points along the field lines.
Magnetism & Electromagnetism 07.01 – Worked example: A steel paperclip is attracted to both ends of a bar magnet. Why?	The paperclip is not a permanent magnet | The magnet induces magnetism in it | Induced magnets are always attracted, whichever pole is near | Answer: It becomes an induced magnet, which is always attracted
Magnetism & Electromagnetism 07.01 – Magnets and magnetic fields: what is the common mistake?	Thinking all metals are magnetic. Only iron, steel, cobalt and nickel are; aluminium and copper are not.
Magnetism & Electromagnetism 07.02 – Electromagnetism: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Electromagnetism: a current in a wire creates a magnetic field around it, in circles. The field is stronger with a bigger current and closer to the wire. | Solenoid: a coil of wire; its field is like a bar magnet's, strong and uniform inside. | Electromagnet: a solenoid with an iron core, which can be switched on and off. | USE IT WHEN – Explaining the field around a wire or solenoid and how to make it stronger. | STEPS – 1. Current in a wire → circular magnetic field around it. | 2. Coil the wire into a solenoid to concentrate the field. | 3. Make it stronger: more current, more turns, or an iron core. | 4. Reverse the current to reverse the field.
Magnetism & Electromagnetism 07.02 – Worked example: Give three ways to make an electromagnet stronger.	Increase the current | Add more turns to the coil | Put an iron core inside the coil | Answer: More current, more turns, an iron core
Magnetism & Electromagnetism 07.02 – Electromagnetism: what is the common mistake?	Saying a steel core makes a good electromagnet. Steel stays magnetised; soft iron is used so it can switch off.
Magnetism & Electromagnetism 07.03 – The motor effect: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Motor effect: a wire carrying a current in a magnetic field experiences a force (Higher). | Equation: F = B I l, with F in N, B (magnetic flux density) in tesla (T), I in A and l (length in the field) in m. | Fleming's left-hand rule: thumb = force (motion), first finger = field, second finger = current. | USE IT WHEN – Calculating the force on a wire, and explaining motors and loudspeakers. | STEPS – 1. Use F = B I l (convert cm to m). | 2. Find the direction with Fleming's left-hand rule. | 3. In a motor, the forces on each side of the coil act in opposite directions, so it turns. | 4. Reverse the current or the field to reverse the force.
Magnetism & Electromagnetism 07.03 – Worked example: A 0.2 m wire carries 3 A at 90° to a 0.5 T field. Find the force.	F = B × I × l | = 0.5 × 3 × 0.2 | = 0.3 | Answer: 0.3 N
Magnetism & Electromagnetism 07.03 – The motor effect: what is the common mistake?	Using the right hand. The MOTOR effect uses the LEFT hand.
Magnetism & Electromagnetism 07.04 – The generator effect: key words and steps? [Higher] [Triple]	KEY WORDS – Generator effect: a pd is induced when a wire moves relative to a magnetic field, or when the field around it changes (Higher, Triple). If the wire is part of a circuit, a current flows. | Alternator: makes ac, using slip rings. | Dynamo: makes dc, using a split-ring commutator. | USE IT WHEN – Explaining how a pd is induced and how to increase it. | STEPS – 1. There must be RELATIVE movement (or a changing field). | 2. Increase the pd: move faster, use a stronger magnet, or use more turns. | 3. Reverse the movement (or the pole) to reverse the pd. | 4. Alternator → ac; dynamo → dc.
Magnetism & Electromagnetism 07.04 – Worked example: A magnet is pushed into a coil connected to a meter, then held still. What does the meter show? [Higher] [Triple]	Moving in: the field changes, so a pd is induced and the needle moves | Held still: no change in the field | So no pd: the meter reads zero | Answer: A reading while moving, zero when still
Magnetism & Electromagnetism 07.04 – The generator effect: what is the common mistake? [Higher] [Triple]	Thinking a magnet just sitting in a coil induces a current. There must be MOVEMENT or a changing field.
Magnetism & Electromagnetism 07.05 – Transformers: key words and steps? [Higher] [Triple]	KEY WORDS – Transformer: two coils of wire on an iron core. An alternating current in the primary coil makes a changing magnetic field in the core, which induces a pd in the secondary coil (Higher, Triple). | Step-up: more turns on the secondary, so a higher pd. | Step-down: fewer turns. | Equations: Vp ÷ Vs = np ÷ ns, and Vs × Is = Vp × Ip (if 100% efficient). | USE IT WHEN – Calculating transformer voltages, turns and currents. | STEPS – 1. Write Vp ÷ Vs = np ÷ ns. | 2. Substitute the three known values. | 3. Rearrange to find the missing one. | 4. For current, use Vp Ip = Vs Is.
Magnetism & Electromagnetism 07.05 – Worked example: A transformer has 100 turns on the primary and 500 on the secondary. The input is 12 V. Find the output pd. [Higher] [Triple]	Vp ÷ Vs = np ÷ ns | 12 ÷ Vs = 100 ÷ 500 | Vs = 12 × 500 ÷ 100 = 60 | Answer: 60 V (step-up)
Magnetism & Electromagnetism 07.05 – Transformers: what is the common mistake? [Higher] [Triple]	Thinking transformers work with dc. They need an ALTERNATING current to make a changing field.

Space Physics 08.03 – Orbits: what is the common mistake? [Triple]	Saying there is no gravity in orbit. Gravity is what KEEPS satellites in orbit.

Required Practicals 09.01 – Specific heat capacity practical: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Specific heat capacity (c): the energy to raise 1 kg by 1 °C. | Equation: c = ΔE ÷ (m Δθ). | Joulemeter: measures the energy supplied (or use E = V × I × t). | Immersion heater: heats the block. | Insulation: reduces heat loss to the surroundings, making the result more accurate. | USE IT WHEN – Describing the specific heat capacity practical and processing its results. | STEPS – 1. Measure the block's mass. Insulate it and put in the heater and thermometer. | 2. Record the starting temperature, then heat for a set time. | 3. Required Practicals 09.01 – Worked example: A 1 kg block is heated with 9000 J and its temperature rises by 10 °C. Find c.	c = ΔE ÷ (m × Δθ) | = 9000 ÷ (1 × 10) | = 900 | Answer: 900 J/kg °C

Required Practicals 09.02 – Resistance and I–V practicals: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Independent variable: what you change (e.g. length of wire). | Dependent variable: what you measure (resistance). | Control variables: kept the same (material, thickness, temperature).
