Energy 01.01.1: Name the 8 energy stores.	Kinetic, gravitational potential, elastic potential, thermal (internal), chemical, magnetic, electrostatic and nuclear. | Example: A stretched bow: elastic potential store. A cup of tea: thermal store. A battery: chemical store.
Energy 01.01.2: What are the 4 ways energy can be transferred?	Mechanically (a force doing work), electrically, by heating, and by radiation (e.g. light or sound waves). | Example: A kettle: energy is transferred electrically to the element, then by heating to the water.
Energy 01.02.1: State the principle of conservation of energy.	Energy can be transferred usefully, stored or dissipated, but it can never be created or destroyed. | Example: A ball dropped from 2 m has 20 J of GPE. Just before landing it has 20 J of kinetic energy (ignoring air resistance).
Energy 01.02.2: What does it mean when energy is 'dissipated'?	It is transferred to less useful stores, usually by heating the surroundings. It is often called 'wasted' energy. | Example: A car engine: some chemical energy is transferred to the thermal store of the engine and the air.
Energy 01.03.1: Equation for kinetic energy (with units).	Method: Square the speed FIRST, then multiply by the mass and halve. To find v, rearrange: v = √(2Eₖ ÷ m). Mass must be in kg (÷1000 if given in g). | Ek = ½ m v² | Ek in J, m in kg, v in m/s | Example: A 0.5 kg ball at 4 m/s: Ek = ½ × 0.5 × 4² = 4 J.
Energy 01.03.2: A 2 kg ball moves at 3 m/s. What is its kinetic energy?	Method: Write the equation, substitute with units checked, then calculate. Square the speed before anything else: 3² = 9, then ½ × 2 × 9. | Ek = ½ × 2 × 3² = 9 J
Energy 01.04.1: Equation for gravitational potential energy (with units).	Method: Multiply the three together. To find the height, rearrange: h = Eₚ ÷ (m g). Use the g value the question gives (usually 9.8 N/kg). | Ep = m g h | Ep in J, m in kg, g in N/kg, h in m | Example: A 3 kg box lifted 2 m: Ep = 3 × 9.8 × 2 = 58.8 J.
Energy 01.04.2: A 5 kg box is lifted 2 m. How much GPE does it gain? (g = 9.8 N/kg)	Method: Substitute straight into Eₚ = m g h. All the values are already in kg, N/kg and m, so no conversions are needed. | Ep = 5 × 9.8 × 2 = 98 J
Energy 01.05.1: Equation for elastic potential energy (with units).	Method: Square the extension first, then multiply by k and halve. The extension is the CHANGE in length (stretched length − original length), in metres. | Ee = ½ k e² | k = spring constant in N/m, e = extension in m. Only valid up to the limit of proportionality. | Example: k = 100 N/m, e = 0.1 m: Ee = ½ × 100 × 0.1² = 0.5 J.
Energy 01.06.1: Define specific heat capacity.	The energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1 °C. | Example: Water's c is 4200 J/kg °C: it takes 4200 J to heat 1 kg of water by 1 °C.
Energy 01.06.2: Equation for change in thermal energy (with units).	Method: Δθ is the temperature CHANGE (final − start), not the final temperature. To find c, rearrange: c = ΔE ÷ (m Δθ). | ΔE = m c Δθ | ΔE in J, m in kg, c in J/kg °C, Δθ in °C | Example: Heating 0.5 kg of water by 10 °C: ΔE = 0.5 × 4200 × 10 = 21 000 J.
Energy 01.07.1: Equation for work done (with units).	Method: Only the distance moved in the direction of the force counts. To find the force, rearrange: F = W ÷ s. | W = F s | W in J, F in N, s = distance moved along the line of the force, in m. 1 J = 1 N m. | Example: Pushing with 50 N for 3 m: W = 50 × 3 = 150 J.
Energy 01.07.2: Define power.	The rate at which energy is transferred, or the rate at which work is done. 1 watt = 1 joule per second. | Example: A 60 W bulb transfers 60 J of energy every second.
Energy 01.07.3: Two equations for power.	Method: Choose the version that matches what you are given (energy or work). Time must be in seconds, so convert minutes (× 60) and hours (× 3600). | P = E ÷ t and P = W ÷ t | P in W, E and W in J, t in s | Example: 1200 J in 10 s: P = 1200 ÷ 10 = 120 W.
Energy 01.08.1: Two equations for efficiency.	Method: Divide useful by total, using the same quantity on top and bottom (energy with energy, power with power). The answer can never be more than 1 (100%). | Efficiency = useful output energy ÷ total input energy | Efficiency = useful power output ÷ total power input | Multiply by 100 for a percentage. | Example: 100 J in, 60 J useful: efficiency = 60 ÷ 100 = 0.6 = 60%.
Energy 01.08.2: A motor takes in 200 J and gives out 150 J usefully. What is its efficiency?	Method: Efficiency = useful output ÷ total input. Put the smaller number (useful) on top, then × 100 for a percentage. | 150 ÷ 200 = 0.75 = 75%
Energy 01.09.1: Two ways to reduce unwanted energy transfers in a machine.	Lubrication to reduce friction, and thermal insulation to reduce heat loss. | Example: Oiling a bike chain reduces friction, so less energy is wasted heating the chain.
Energy 01.09.2: What does a high thermal conductivity mean?	Energy is transferred through the material quickly by conduction. | Example: A metal spoon in hot tea warms quickly; a wooden spoon doesn't (wood has a low thermal conductivity).
Energy 01.09.3: How does wall thickness and material affect how fast a house loses heat?	Thicker walls and materials with lower thermal conductivity reduce the rate of cooling. | Example: A house with thick brick walls and cavity insulation cools more slowly than one with thin walls.
Energy 01.10.1: Name the non-renewable energy resources.	Fossil fuels (coal, oil, gas) and nuclear fuel (e.g. uranium). They will run out. | Example: Coal, oil and gas take millions of years to form, so once used they are gone.
Energy 01.10.2: Name 7 renewable energy resources.	Bio-fuel, wind, hydroelectricity, geothermal, tides, solar and water waves. They can be replenished as they are used. | Example: Wind keeps blowing and the sun keeps shining, so they won't run out.
Energy 01.10.3: Pros and cons of fossil fuels.	Pros: reliable, can meet demand quickly, relatively cheap. Cons: release CO₂ (climate change), sulfur dioxide (acid rain), and will run out. | Example: A gas power station can start up quickly when demand rises at 6 pm, but it releases CO₂.
Energy 01.10.4: Why are wind and solar power unreliable?	They depend on the weather and time of day, so they can't always meet demand. | Example: Solar panels produce nothing at night, and wind turbines stop on a calm day.
Energy 01.10.5: Which renewable resources are reliable?	Hydroelectricity, geothermal, tidal and bio-fuels. | Example: A hydroelectric dam can release water whenever extra electricity is needed.
Electricity 02.01.1: Define electric current.	The rate of flow of electrical charge. Current is measured in amps (A). | Example: A current of 2 A means 2 coulombs of charge pass a point every second.
Electricity 02.01.2: Equation linking charge, current and time.	Method: Time must be in seconds. To find current: I = Q ÷ t; to find time: t = Q ÷ I. | Q = I t | Q in coulombs (C), I in A, t in s | Example: 3 A for 10 s: Q = 3 × 10 = 30 C.
Electricity 02.02.1: Equation linking potential difference, current and resistance.	Method: Use the formula triangle: cover the one you want. V = I R, I = V ÷ R, R = V ÷ I. Convert kΩ to Ω (× 1000) and mA to A (÷ 1000) first. | V = I R | V in volts (V), I in A, R in ohms (Ω) | Example: I = 2 A through R = 6 Ω: V = 2 × 6 = 12 V.
Electricity 02.02.2: A resistor has 12 V across it and 3 A through it. What is its resistance?	Method: You want R, so rearrange V = I R to R = V ÷ I. Then substitute: 12 ÷ 3. | R = V ÷ I = 12 ÷ 3 = 4 Ω
Electricity 02.03.1: Current and potential difference in a series circuit?	Current is the same everywhere. The total pd from the supply is shared between the components. | Example: A 6 V battery with two identical bulbs in series: each bulb gets 3 V, and the same current flows through both.
Electricity 02.03.2: Total resistance of resistors in series?	Method: In series the current has to pass through every resistor, so their resistances simply add. | Add them up: Rtotal = R1 + R2 | Example: 3 Ω and 5 Ω in series: Rtotal = 3 + 5 = 8 Ω.
Electricity 02.03.3: Current and potential difference in a parallel circuit?	The pd is the same across each branch. The total current is the sum of the currents in each branch. | Example: Two branches carrying 1 A and 2 A: the total current is 3 A, and each branch has the full supply pd.
Electricity 02.03.4: What happens to total resistance when you add resistors in parallel?	It decreases. The total is always less than the smallest individual resistor. | Example: Two 10 Ω resistors in parallel have a total resistance of 5 Ω, less than either one.
Electricity 02.04.1: Describe the I–V graph for a fixed resistor (at constant temperature).	A straight line through the origin. Current is directly proportional to pd, so resistance is constant (ohmic conductor). | Example: Doubling the pd across a 5 Ω resistor doubles the current: 5 V gives 1 A, 10 V gives 2 A.
Electricity 02.04.2: Describe the I–V graph for a filament lamp and explain it.	A curve (S-shape). As current increases, the filament heats up, so its resistance increases. | Example: At low pd a lamp might be 2 Ω; when glowing brightly its resistance might rise to 10 Ω.
Electricity 02.04.3: How does a diode behave?	Current flows through it in one direction only. It has a very high resistance in the reverse direction. | Example: An LED (a type of diode) only lights up when connected the right way round.
Electricity 02.05.1: How does a thermistor's resistance change? Give a use.	Resistance decreases as temperature increases. Used in thermostats and fire alarms. | Example: A fire alarm: as the room heats up, the thermistor's resistance falls, the current rises and the alarm sounds.
Electricity 02.05.2: How does an LDR's resistance change? Give a use.	Resistance decreases as light intensity increases. Used in automatic night lights and street lights. | Example: A street light: at dusk the LDR's resistance rises, which switches the lamp on.
Electricity 02.06.1: Difference between direct and alternating current.	Direct current (dc) flows in one direction only. Alternating current (ac) keeps changing direction. | Example: A battery gives dc: the current always flows the same way. Mains gives ac: it changes direction 50 times a second.
Electricity 02.06.2: UK mains supply: type, voltage and frequency.	Alternating current, about 230 V, 50 Hz. | Example: 50 Hz means the current changes direction 50 times every second.
Electricity 02.06.3: Name the 3 wires in a plug: colour and job.	Live (brown): 230 V, carries the current in. Neutral (blue): about 0 V, completes the circuit. Earth (green & yellow): 0 V, safety wire. | Example: If a metal toaster case touches the live wire, the earth wire carries the current away safely and the fuse blows.
Electricity 02.06.4: Why can touching the live wire be dangerous, even if the switch is off?	The live wire is still at 230 V. Your body is at 0 V, so a large current could flow through you to earth. | Example: Changing a light bulb with the switch off can still be risky if the live wire is exposed, so turn off at the mains.
Electricity 02.07.1: Two equations for electrical power.	Method: Use P = V I when you know the pd; use P = I² R when you know the resistance but not the pd. Square the current before multiplying. | P = V I and P = I² R | P in W, V in V, I in A, R in Ω | Example: 6 V and 2 A: P = 6 × 2 = 12 W. Or 2 A through 3 Ω: P = 2² × 3 = 12 W.
Electricity 02.07.2: Two equations for energy transferred electrically.	Method: E = P t when you know the power and time (t in seconds); E = Q V when you know the charge and pd. | E = P t and E = Q V | E in J, P in W, t in s, Q in C, V in V | Example: A 100 W lamp for 60 s: E = 100 × 60 = 6000 J. Or 5 C through 12 V: E = 5 × 12 = 60 J.
Electricity 02.07.3: A kettle runs on 230 V and draws 10 A. What is its power?	Method: Pick the power equation that uses what you are given: V and I, so P = V I. Divide by 1000 to give kW. | P = V I = 230 × 10 = 2300 W (2.3 kW)
Electricity 02.08.1: What is the National Grid?	A system of cables and transformers linking power stations to homes and businesses. | Example: Electricity from a power station travels on pylons at very high pd, then through substations to homes.
Electricity 02.08.2: Why does the National Grid use step-up transformers?	They increase the pd and decrease the current in the cables. Lower current means less energy lost by heating, so it's more efficient. | Example: For the same power, raising the pd from 25 000 V to 400 000 V makes the current 16 times smaller.
Electricity 02.08.3: Why are step-down transformers used near homes?	To reduce the pd to a safe level (about 230 V) for use in homes. | Example: A substation transformer lowers 11 000 V to 230 V for your home.
Electricity 02.09.1: How does an insulator become charged by rubbing?	Electrons are transferred from one material to the other. The one that gains electrons becomes negative; the one that loses them becomes positive. | Example: Rubbing a balloon on your hair moves electrons onto the balloon: the balloon becomes negative and your hair positive.
Electricity 02.09.2: What happens when two charged objects are brought close?	Like charges repel and unlike charges attract. This is a non-contact force. | Example: A charged balloon sticks to a wall; two negatively charged balloons push apart.
Electricity 02.09.3: What is an electric field? [Triple]	The region around a charged object where another charged object feels a force. It is strongest closest to the charge. | Example: A small charged bead near a charged dome is pushed or pulled; the force is stronger the closer it gets.
Electricity 02.09.4: Why does sparking happen? [Triple]	A high pd between a charged object and an earthed conductor causes charge to jump across the gap. | Example: A spark jumps when you touch a metal door handle after walking on a nylon carpet.
Particle Model 03.01.1: Equation for density (with units).	Method: Divide mass by volume. Check the units match the answer you want: g and cm³ give g/cm³; kg and m³ give kg/m³. | ρ = m ÷ V | ρ in kg/m³, m in kg, V in m³ | Example: 2 kg of a liquid takes up 0.002 m³: ρ = 2 ÷ 0.002 = 1000 kg/m³ (water).
Particle Model 03.01.2: A 400 g block has a volume of 50 cm³. What is its density?	Method: Mass in g ÷ volume in cm³ gives g/cm³. To change g/cm³ into kg/m³, multiply by 1000. | 400 ÷ 50 = 8 g/cm³ = 8000 kg/m³
Particle Model 03.02.1: Describe the particles in a solid, liquid and gas.	Solid: close, regular pattern, vibrate in place. Liquid: close, random, move past each other. Gas: far apart, random, move quickly. | Example: Ice (solid): particles vibrate in fixed places. Water (liquid): they slide past each other. Steam (gas): they move fast and far apart.
Particle Model 03.02.2: Why is a gas less dense than the same substance as a solid?	The particles in a gas are much further apart, so the same mass takes up a bigger volume. | Example: 1 kg of steam takes up about 1700 times more space than 1 kg of liquid water.
Particle Model 03.02.3: Name the changes of state.	Melting, freezing, boiling (evaporating), condensing and sublimating. | Example: Ice melting, water freezing, water boiling, steam condensing on a window, and dry ice (solid CO₂) sublimating.
Particle Model 03.02.4: Why is a change of state a physical change?	No new substance is made. Mass is conserved, and the material gets its original properties back if the change is reversed. | Example: Melting 10 g of ice gives exactly 10 g of water, which can be frozen back into ice.
Particle Model 03.03.1: Define internal energy.	The total kinetic energy and potential energy of all the particles in a system. | Example: Heating a gas makes its particles move faster, increasing their kinetic energy and so the internal energy.
Particle Model 03.03.2: What does heating a substance do to it?	Increases its internal energy. This either raises its temperature or changes its state. | Example: Heating water at 20 °C raises its temperature; heating water at 100 °C turns it into steam.
Particle Model 03.03.3: Define specific latent heat.	The energy needed to change the state of 1 kg of a substance with no change in temperature. | Example: Water's latent heat of vaporisation is 2 260 000 J/kg: it takes 2.26 MJ to boil away 1 kg at 100 °C.
Particle Model 03.03.4: Equation for energy needed to change state (with units).	Method: Mass in kg. To find L, rearrange: L = E ÷ m. There is no temperature change in this equation, because the temperature stays constant while the state changes. | E = m L | E in J, m in kg, L = specific latent heat in J/kg | Example: Melting 2 kg of ice (L = 334 000 J/kg): E = 2 × 334 000 = 668 000 J.
Particle Model 03.03.5: Latent heat of fusion vs latent heat of vaporisation?	Fusion: solid ↔ liquid. Vaporisation: liquid ↔ gas. | Example: Ice melting uses the latent heat of fusion; a kettle boiling dry uses the latent heat of vaporisation.
Particle Model 03.03.6: Why is there a flat section on a heating curve?	The substance is changing state. Energy is breaking bonds between particles, so the temperature stays the same. | Example: When ice is heated, the temperature stays at 0 °C until all the ice has melted.
Particle Model 03.04.1: What causes gas pressure?	Gas particles colliding with the walls of their container and exerting a force. | Example: Air particles inside a balloon hit the rubber millions of times a second, pushing it outwards.
Particle Model 03.04.2: Why does gas pressure rise when temperature rises (fixed volume)?	Particles move faster, so they hit the walls more often and harder. This increases the pressure. | Example: A car tyre's pressure is higher after a long drive, because the air inside has warmed up.
Particle Model 03.04.3: Relationship between pressure and volume of a gas (constant temperature). [Triple]	Method: For a fixed mass at constant temperature, p₁V₁ = p₂V₂. Multiply the first pair, then divide by the known value in the second pair. | p V = constant | If volume halves, pressure doubles. | Example: 100 kPa in 2 m³, squashed to 1 m³: p = 100 × 2 ÷ 1 = 200 kPa.
Particle Model 03.04.4: Why does pumping up a bike tyre make the pump warm? [Higher] [Triple]	Work is done on the gas, which increases the internal energy of the particles, so the temperature rises. | Example: Pushing the pump handle does work on the air inside; the barrel feels warm.
Atomic Structure 04.01.1: Roughly how big is an atom? How big is the nucleus?	Atom radius is about 1 × 10⁻¹⁰ m. The nucleus is less than 1/10 000 of the atom's radius. | Example: If an atom were a football stadium, the nucleus would be about the size of a pea in the centre.
Atomic Structure 04.01.2: Describe the structure of an atom.	A tiny positive nucleus of protons and neutrons, with electrons orbiting in energy levels (shells). | Example: Helium: 2 protons and 2 neutrons in the nucleus, with 2 electrons orbiting.
Atomic Structure 04.01.3: Relative charge and mass of a proton, neutron and electron.	Proton: +1, mass 1 | Neutron: 0, mass 1 | Electron: −1, mass very small | Example: An atom of lithium (3 protons, 4 neutrons, 3 electrons) has almost all its mass in the nucleus.
Atomic Structure 04.02.1: What do the atomic number and mass number tell you?	Atomic number = number of protons. Mass number = protons + neutrons. | Example: Sodium: atomic number 11 and mass number 23, so 11 protons and 23 − 11 = 12 neutrons.
Atomic Structure 04.02.2: What is an isotope?	Atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but a different number of neutrons. | Example: Carbon-12 and carbon-14 both have 6 protons, but 6 and 8 neutrons.
Atomic Structure 04.02.3: What is an ion?	An atom that has lost or gained electrons, so it is charged. | Example: A sodium atom that loses 1 electron becomes a Na⁺ ion.
Atomic Structure 04.02.4: What happens when an electron absorbs or emits EM radiation?	Absorbs: it moves to a higher energy level (further from the nucleus). Emits: it drops to a lower energy level. | Example: An electron absorbs light and jumps up a shell; when it drops back down it emits light.
Atomic Structure 04.03.1: Describe the plum pudding model.	A ball of positive charge with negative electrons embedded in it. | Example: Like a 'Christmas pudding': positive sponge with negative electrons as the currants.
Atomic Structure 04.03.2: Alpha scattering: what happened and what did it show?	Most alpha particles went straight through (atom mostly empty space). Some deflected (nucleus is positive). A few bounced back (mass concentrated in a tiny nucleus). | Example: If the atom were solid, the alpha particles would all have been stopped or deflected. Most went straight through.
Atomic Structure 04.03.3: Put the atomic models in order.	Solid sphere (Dalton) → plum pudding (Thomson) → nuclear model (Rutherford) → energy levels (Bohr) → neutrons discovered (Chadwick). | Example: Bohr's energy levels explained why atoms give out light only at certain colours.
Atomic Structure 04.04.1: What is radioactive decay?	A random process where an unstable nucleus gives out radiation to become more stable. | Example: Carbon-14 in a dead tree slowly decays; you can't predict which atom will decay next.
Atomic Structure 04.04.2: Define activity. What is its unit?	The rate at which a source of unstable nuclei decays. Measured in becquerels (Bq). | Example: A source with an activity of 300 Bq has 300 nuclei decaying every second.
Atomic Structure 04.04.3: What is count rate?	The number of decays recorded each second by a detector, such as a Geiger-Müller tube. | Example: A Geiger–Müller tube near a source clicks 50 times in 10 s: the count rate is 5 counts per second.
Atomic Structure 04.05.1: Alpha radiation: what is it, range and what stops it?	2 protons + 2 neutrons (a helium nucleus). Travels a few cm in air. Stopped by paper. Strongly ionising. | Example: Alpha is used in smoke alarms: it ionises the air, and smoke blocks it.
Atomic Structure 04.05.2: Beta radiation: what is it and what stops it?	A fast electron from the nucleus (a neutron turns into a proton). Stopped by a few mm of aluminium. Moderately ionising. | Example: Beta is used to monitor the thickness of aluminium foil in a factory.
Atomic Structure 04.05.3: Gamma radiation: what is it and what stops it?	An electromagnetic wave from the nucleus. Stopped by thick lead or concrete. Weakly ionising. | Example: Gamma is used to sterilise medical equipment because it passes through the packaging.
Atomic Structure 04.06.1: How does alpha decay change the nucleus? Give an example.	Mass number −4, atomic number −2. | ²¹⁹₈₆Rn → ²¹⁵₈₄Po + ⁴₂He | Example: Polonium-210 (atomic number 84) alpha decays to lead-206 (atomic number 82).
Atomic Structure 04.06.2: How does beta decay change the nucleus? Give an example.	Mass number stays the same, atomic number +1. | ¹⁴₆C → ¹⁴₇N + ⁰₋₁e | Example: Carbon-14 (atomic number 6) beta decays to nitrogen-14 (atomic number 7).
Atomic Structure 04.06.3: How does gamma emission change the nucleus?	It doesn't change the mass number or atomic number. The nucleus just loses energy. | Example: After alpha or beta decay, the new nucleus often gives out gamma rays to lose extra energy.
Atomic Structure 04.07.1: Define half-life.	The time it takes for the number of unstable nuclei in a sample (or the count rate) to halve. | Example: Iodine-131 has a half-life of 8 days: after 8 days, half of it is left; after 16 days, a quarter.
Atomic Structure 04.07.2: A source has an activity of 800 Bq and a half-life of 2 hours. What is its activity after 6 hours?	Method: Work out how many half-lives fit into the time (6 ÷ 2 = 3), then halve the activity that many times. | 6 h = 3 half-lives: 800 → 400 → 200 → 100 Bq
Atomic Structure 04.07.3: A sample's activity falls from 400 Bq to 100 Bq. What is the net decline as a ratio of the initial value? [Higher]	Method: Find how much it fell (start − end), then divide by the START value. Each halving is one half-life: 400 → 200 → 100 is two. | It fell by 300 Bq, so the ratio is 300 ÷ 400 = 3/4. That's 2 half-lives.
Atomic Structure 04.08.1: Irradiation vs contamination?	Irradiation: exposed to radiation from outside; the object does not become radioactive. Contamination: radioactive atoms get on or inside the object. | Example: An X-ray irradiates you, but you don't become radioactive. Radon gas breathed in contaminates your lungs.
Atomic Structure 04.08.2: Why must scientists publish findings on radiation effects?	So they can be checked by other scientists (peer review). | Example: A new claim that low-dose radiation is harmless would be checked by other scientists before being accepted.
Atomic Structure 04.08.3: Give 4 sources of background radiation. [Triple]	Rocks (e.g. radon gas), cosmic rays from space, fallout from nuclear weapons testing, and nuclear accidents. Dose is measured in sieverts (Sv). | Example: Radon gas from granite rocks is the biggest source of background radiation in parts of the UK.
Atomic Structure 04.09.1: What is nuclear fission? [Triple]	A large unstable nucleus (e.g. uranium-235) absorbs a neutron and splits into two smaller nuclei, releasing 2–3 neutrons and energy. | Example: Uranium-235 absorbs a neutron, splits into two smaller nuclei and releases 2 or 3 more neutrons.
Atomic Structure 04.09.2: What is a chain reaction? How is it controlled? [Triple]	Neutrons from one fission cause more fissions. In a reactor, control rods absorb neutrons to control the rate. | Example: Lowering the control rods further absorbs more neutrons, slowing the reaction down.
Atomic Structure 04.09.3: What is nuclear fusion? [Triple]	Two light nuclei join to form a heavier nucleus. Some mass is converted into energy. This powers stars. | Example: In the Sun, hydrogen nuclei fuse to form helium, releasing huge amounts of energy.
Atomic Structure 04.09.4: Two medical uses of radiation. [Triple]	Tracers to explore inside the body (gamma, short half-life), and radiotherapy to kill cancer cells. | Example: Technetium-99m (half-life 6 hours) is injected as a tracer and detected outside the body.
Forces & Motion 05.01.1: Scalar vs vector?	Scalar: magnitude only. Vector: magnitude and direction. | Example: Speed: 20 m/s. Velocity: 20 m/s north.
Forces & Motion 05.01.2: Give 4 scalar and 4 vector quantities.	Scalars: distance, speed, mass, energy. Vectors: displacement, velocity, force, acceleration (also momentum). | Example: Mass 5 kg (scalar). Weight 49 N downwards (vector).
Forces & Motion 05.01.3: Contact vs non-contact forces: examples.	Contact: friction, air resistance, tension, normal contact force. Non-contact: gravity, magnetic, electrostatic. | Example: Kicking a ball: contact force. A magnet pulling a paperclip: non-contact force.
Forces & Motion 05.02.1: Equation for weight (with units).	Method: Mass must be in kg (÷ 1000 from g). On Earth g = 9.8 N/kg; on other planets use the g value given. To find mass: m = W ÷ g. | W = m g | W in N, m in kg, g in N/kg (9.8 N/kg on Earth) | Example: A 50 kg person: W = 50 × 9.8 = 490 N.
Forces & Motion 05.02.2: Mass vs weight?	Mass is the amount of matter (kg) and is the same everywhere. Weight is the force of gravity on it (N) and depends on g. | Example: A 10 kg bag weighs 98 N on Earth but only 16 N on the Moon; its mass stays 10 kg.
Forces & Motion 05.03.1: What is a resultant force?	The single force that has the same effect as all the original forces acting together. | Example: 10 N right and 4 N left: the resultant is 6 N right.
Forces & Motion 05.03.2: What happens when work is done against friction?	Energy is transferred to thermal stores, so the object and surroundings get warmer. | Example: Rubbing your hands together does work against friction, and they get warm.
Forces & Motion 05.04.1: State Hooke's law as an equation.	Method: The extension is the change in length, in metres (÷ 100 from cm). To find k, rearrange: k = F ÷ e. | F = k e | F in N, k = spring constant in N/m, e = extension in m. Works up to the limit of proportionality. | Example: k = 25 N/m, e = 0.2 m: F = 25 × 0.2 = 5 N.
Forces & Motion 05.04.2: Elastic vs inelastic deformation?	Elastic: returns to its original shape when the forces are removed. Inelastic: it stays deformed. | Example: A rubber band springs back (elastic). Modelling clay stays squashed (inelastic).
Forces & Motion 05.04.3: A spring (k = 50 N/m) is stretched by 0.2 m. What force is applied?	Method: Substitute into F = k e. The extension is already in metres, so multiply: 50 × 0.2. | F = k e = 50 × 0.2 = 10 N
Forces & Motion 05.05.1: Equation for the moment of a force. [Triple]	Method: Use the PERPENDICULAR distance from the pivot to the line of the force, in metres. For balance problems, set clockwise moments equal to anticlockwise moments. | M = F d | M in N m, F in N, d = perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of the force, in m | Example: A 20 N force 0.3 m from a pivot: M = 20 × 0.3 = 6 N m.
Forces & Motion 05.05.2: State the principle of moments. [Triple]	When an object is balanced, the total clockwise moment equals the total anticlockwise moment about the pivot. | Example: 30 N at 2 m on the left balances 60 N at 1 m on the right: 30 × 2 = 60 × 1.
Forces & Motion 05.06.1: Equation for pressure on a surface. [Triple]	Method: Area must be in m² (cm² ÷ 10 000). A smaller area gives a bigger pressure for the same force. | p = F ÷ A | p in pascals (Pa), F in N, A in m² | Example: A 600 N box on 0.5 m²: p = 600 ÷ 0.5 = 1200 Pa.
Forces & Motion 05.06.2: Equation for pressure in a liquid. [Higher] [Triple]	Method: Depth h is measured down from the surface, in metres. Multiply the three together; this gives the pressure from the liquid only. | p = h ρ g | h = depth in m, ρ = density in kg/m³, g in N/kg. Pressure increases with depth. | Example: 10 m down in water: p = 10 × 1000 × 9.8 = 98 000 Pa.
Forces & Motion 05.06.3: What causes upthrust? When does an object float? [Triple]	Pressure is greater on the bottom of a submerged object than the top. It floats if its weight equals the upthrust (it is less dense than the fluid). | Example: A ship floats because the water's upthrust equals its weight.
Forces & Motion 05.07.1: Equation for speed. Typical speeds for walking, running, cycling and sound?	Method: Distance in m and time in s gives m/s. To find distance: s = v t; to find time: t = s ÷ v. | v = s ÷ t | Walking about 1.5 m/s, running 3 m/s, cycling 6 m/s, sound in air 330 m/s | Example: Walking 300 m in 200 s: v = 300 ÷ 200 = 1.5 m/s.
Forces & Motion 05.07.2: Equation for acceleration (with units).	Method: Δv = final velocity − initial velocity. If the object slows down, Δv (and so a) is negative (a deceleration). | a = Δv ÷ t | a in m/s², Δv = change in velocity in m/s, t in s | Example: From 4 m/s to 10 m/s in 2 s: a = (10 − 4) ÷ 2 = 3 m/s².
Forces & Motion 05.07.3: Equation for uniform acceleration linking v, u, a and s.	Method: List what you know (v, u, a, s) and what you want. Starting from rest means u = 0. Rearrange: a = (v² − u²) ÷ 2s. | v² − u² = 2 a s | v = final velocity, u = initial velocity | Example: From rest (u = 0) with a = 2 m/s² over 25 m: v² = 0 + 2 × 2 × 25 = 100, so v = 10 m/s.
Forces & Motion 05.07.4: A car goes from rest to 20 m/s over 50 m. What is its acceleration?	Method: Write down v = 20, u = 0, s = 50. Substitute into v² − u² = 2 a s, simplify, then divide to find a. | 20² − 0² = 2 × a × 50 → 400 = 100a → a = 4 m/s²
Forces & Motion 05.08.1: What does the gradient of a distance–time graph show?	The speed. A curved line means the speed is changing. | Example: A line rising 60 m in 12 s: speed = 60 ÷ 12 = 5 m/s.
Forces & Motion 05.08.2: Velocity–time graph: what do the gradient and the area show?	Gradient = acceleration. Area under the graph = distance travelled. | Example: From 0 to 8 m/s in 4 s: a = 8 ÷ 4 = 2 m/s², distance = ½ × 4 × 8 = 16 m.
Forces & Motion 05.08.3: What is the acceleration of a freely falling object near Earth?	About 9.8 m/s². | Example: A dropped stone: after 1 s it falls at about 9.8 m/s; after 2 s, about 19.6 m/s (ignoring air resistance).
Forces & Motion 05.09.1: Explain terminal velocity for a falling object.	Weight stays the same, but air resistance increases with speed. When they are equal, the resultant force is zero, so it falls at a constant (terminal) velocity. | Example: A skydiver speeds up until air resistance equals their weight, then falls at a steady speed of about 55 m/s.
Forces & Motion 05.10.1: State Newton's first law.	If the resultant force is zero, a stationary object stays still and a moving object carries on at the same speed in the same direction. | Example: A book on a table stays still; a hockey puck on smooth ice keeps sliding at the same speed.
Forces & Motion 05.10.2: State Newton's second law as an equation.	Method: Use the RESULTANT force (add forces in the same direction, subtract opposing ones). To find a: a = F ÷ m. | F = m a | Acceleration is directly proportional to the resultant force and inversely proportional to mass. | Example: A 2 kg trolley with a resultant force of 6 N: a = 6 ÷ 2 = 3 m/s².
Forces & Motion 05.10.3: A 1200 kg car accelerates at 2 m/s². What resultant force acts on it?	Method: Substitute into F = m a. Mass is already in kg and acceleration in m/s². | F = m a = 1200 × 2 = 2400 N
Forces & Motion 05.10.4: State Newton's third law.	When two objects interact, they exert equal and opposite forces on each other. | Example: You push on a wall with 50 N; the wall pushes back on you with 50 N.
Forces & Motion 05.10.5: What is inertia? [Higher]	The tendency of an object to stay at rest or keep moving at a constant velocity. | Example: Passengers lurch forwards when a bus brakes suddenly, because their bodies keep moving.
Forces & Motion 05.11.1: What makes up stopping distance?	Stopping distance = thinking distance + braking distance. | Example: Thinking distance 15 m + braking distance 38 m = stopping distance 53 m.
Forces & Motion 05.11.2: What affects reaction time (thinking distance)?	Tiredness, drugs, alcohol and distractions. Thinking distance also increases with speed. | Example: A tired driver with a 0.9 s reaction time at 20 m/s travels 18 m before braking.
Forces & Motion 05.11.3: What affects braking distance?	Speed, wet or icy roads, worn brakes, worn tyres and the mass of the vehicle. | Example: On an icy road, a car's braking distance can be up to 10 times longer.
Forces & Motion 05.11.4: Typical human reaction time?	0.2 to 0.9 seconds. | Example: A ruler-drop test: catching after it falls 20 cm shows a reaction time of about 0.2 s.
Forces & Motion 05.11.5: What happens to the energy when a car brakes?	Friction between the brakes and wheels does work, transferring kinetic energy to thermal energy. The brakes heat up. | Example: After an emergency stop, the brake discs can be too hot to touch.
Forces & Motion 05.12.1: Equation for momentum (with units). [Higher]	Method: Momentum has a direction: give one direction positive and the other negative. Mass in kg. | p = m v | p in kg m/s, m in kg, v in m/s | Example: A 70 kg runner at 5 m/s: p = 70 × 5 = 350 kg m/s.
Forces & Motion 05.12.2: State the conservation of momentum. [Higher]	In a closed system, the total momentum before an event equals the total momentum after. | Example: A 1 kg trolley at 4 m/s hits a stationary 1 kg trolley and they join: 4 = 2 × v, so v = 2 m/s.
Forces & Motion 05.12.3: A 2 kg trolley moves at 3 m/s. What is its momentum? [Higher]	Method: Substitute into p = m v and give the unit kg m/s. | p = 2 × 3 = 6 kg m/s
Forces & Motion 05.12.4: Equation linking force and change in momentum. [Higher] [Triple]	Method: Δv is the change in velocity (watch the signs if it changes direction) and Δt is the time in seconds. A longer time gives a smaller force. | F = m Δv ÷ Δt | Force equals the rate of change of momentum. | Example: A 60 kg person stopping from 10 m/s in 0.5 s: F = 60 × 10 ÷ 0.5 = 1200 N.
Forces & Motion 05.12.5: How do airbags, seat belts and crumple zones keep you safe? [Triple]	They increase the time taken for your momentum to change, which reduces the force on you. | Example: An airbag makes a stop take 0.5 s instead of 0.05 s, so the force is 10 times smaller.
Forces & Motion 05.13.1: Why is an object moving in a circle at constant speed accelerating? [Higher]	Its direction keeps changing, so its velocity changes. A change in velocity is acceleration. | Example: The Moon orbits Earth at a steady speed, but its direction changes, so it is accelerating towards Earth.
Waves 06.01.1: Transverse vs longitudinal waves (with examples).	Transverse: oscillations perpendicular to the direction of energy transfer (light, ripples on water). Longitudinal: oscillations parallel (sound), with compressions and rarefactions. | Example: Light and water ripples are transverse. Sound is longitudinal.
Waves 06.01.2: What do waves transfer?	Energy, not matter. | Example: A sound wave reaches your ear, but the air near the speaker doesn't travel to you.
Waves 06.02.1: Define amplitude.	The maximum displacement of a point on a wave from its undisturbed position. | Example: A wave rises 3 cm above its rest line: its amplitude is 3 cm.
Waves 06.02.2: Define wavelength.	The distance from a point on one wave to the same point on the next wave. | Example: Peaks 2 m apart: the wavelength is 2 m.
Waves 06.02.3: Define frequency.	The number of waves passing a point each second. Measured in hertz (Hz). | Example: 10 waves pass a point in 2 s: f = 10 ÷ 2 = 5 Hz.
Waves 06.02.4: Equation linking period and frequency.	Method: Period and frequency are reciprocals: T = 1 ÷ f and f = 1 ÷ T. Convert kHz to Hz (× 1000) first. | T = 1 ÷ f | T in s, f in Hz | Example: f = 50 Hz: T = 1 ÷ 50 = 0.02 s.
Waves 06.03.1: Wave speed equation (with units).	Method: Frequency in Hz and wavelength in m gives m/s. To find wavelength: λ = v ÷ f; to find frequency: f = v ÷ λ. | v = f λ | v in m/s, f in Hz, λ (wavelength) in m | Example: f = 200 Hz and λ = 1.5 m: v = 200 × 1.5 = 300 m/s.
Waves 06.03.2: A wave has a frequency of 50 Hz and a wavelength of 0.2 m. What is its speed?	Method: Substitute into v = f λ. Wavelength is already in metres, so multiply: 50 × 0.2. | v = f λ = 50 × 0.2 = 10 m/s
Waves 06.04.1: List the EM spectrum from longest to shortest wavelength.	Radio, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays, gamma rays. | Example: Radio waves can be kilometres long; gamma rays are smaller than an atom.
Waves 06.04.2: 3 things all EM waves have in common.	They are transverse, travel at the same speed in a vacuum (3 × 10⁸ m/s), and transfer energy from source to absorber. | Example: Light from the Sun reaches Earth in about 8 minutes, at 3 × 10⁸ m/s.
Waves 06.04.3: Uses of radio waves, microwaves and infrared.	Radio: TV and radio. Microwaves: satellite communications, cooking. Infrared: electric heaters, cooking, infrared cameras. | Example: Your TV remote uses infrared. Mobile phones use microwaves.
Waves 06.04.4: Uses of visible light, UV, X-rays and gamma rays.	Visible: fibre optic communications. UV: energy-efficient lamps, sun tanning. X-rays and gamma: medical imaging and treatment. | Example: Hospitals use X-rays to image broken bones and gamma rays to treat cancer.
Waves 06.04.5: Dangers of UV, X-rays and gamma rays.	UV: ages skin prematurely, increases risk of skin cancer. X-rays and gamma: ionising radiation, can cause gene mutations and cancer. | Example: Sunscreen blocks UV to reduce the risk of skin cancer; radiographers stand behind a screen during X-rays.
Waves 06.05.1: What is refraction?	A change in direction when a wave crosses a boundary and changes speed. It bends towards the normal when it slows down. | Example: A straw looks bent in a glass of water, because light refracts at the surface.
Waves 06.05.2: Why does refraction happen? (wavefronts) [Higher]	One part of the wavefront enters the new material first and changes speed first, so the wave changes direction. | Example: Wavefronts hitting glass at an angle: the edge that enters first slows first, turning the wave.
Waves 06.06.1: How are radio waves produced and absorbed? [Higher]	Produced by oscillations in electrical circuits. When absorbed, they can create an alternating current with the same frequency. | Example: A radio station's oscillating current makes radio waves; they induce a matching current in your radio's aerial.
Waves 06.06.2: Where do gamma rays come from?	Changes in the nucleus of an atom. | Example: Cobalt-60 nuclei give out gamma rays as they become more stable.
Waves 06.07.1: Law of reflection. Specular vs diffuse reflection? [Triple]	Angle of incidence = angle of reflection. Specular: from a smooth surface, one direction. Diffuse: from a rough surface, scattered. | Example: A mirror gives a clear image (specular). Paper scatters light in all directions (diffuse).
Waves 06.07.2: Why does a red object look red? [Triple]	It reflects red light and absorbs all other colours. Filters let through only their own colour. | Example: A red filter in front of white light lets only red light through.
Waves 06.08.1: Convex vs concave lens? [Triple]	Convex: converging, can form real images. Concave: diverging, always forms virtual images. | Example: A magnifying glass is convex. Glasses for short sight use concave lenses.
Waves 06.08.2: Equation for magnification. [Triple]	Method: Both heights must be in the same units. Magnification has no units because the units cancel. | Magnification = image height ÷ object height | No units | Example: An object 3 cm tall with an image 12 cm tall: magnification = 12 ÷ 3 = 4.
Waves 06.09.1: What is a perfect black body? [Triple]	An object that absorbs all the radiation that hits it. It is also the best possible emitter. | Example: A matt black surface is close to a perfect black body.
Waves 06.09.2: How does temperature affect the radiation an object emits? [Triple]	A hotter object emits more radiation in total, and more at shorter wavelengths. | Example: A cooker ring glows red when very hot; at room temperature it gives off only infrared.
Waves 06.10.1: Range of human hearing? What is ultrasound used for? [Triple]	About 20 Hz to 20 kHz. Ultrasound (above 20 kHz) is used for medical scans and industrial imaging, because it partly reflects at boundaries. | Example: A dog whistle at 25 kHz can't be heard by humans. Ultrasound scans check babies before birth.
Waves 06.10.2: P waves vs S waves. What do they show about Earth? [Triple]	P waves: longitudinal, travel through solids and liquids. S waves: transverse, solids only. S waves can't pass through the outer core, so it must be liquid. | Example: S waves don't reach the opposite side of Earth, which shows the outer core is liquid.
Magnetism & Electromagnetism 07.01.1: What happens when two magnets' poles are brought together?	Like poles repel and unlike poles attract. The forces are strongest at the poles. | Example: Two north poles push apart; a north and a south pull together.
Magnetism & Electromagnetism 07.01.2: Permanent vs induced magnet?	Permanent: has its own magnetic field. Induced: becomes a magnet only when placed in a magnetic field, and it always attracts. | Example: An iron nail near a magnet becomes magnetic and picks up pins, then loses most of this when the magnet is removed.
Magnetism & Electromagnetism 07.01.3: Name 4 magnetic materials.	Iron, steel, cobalt and nickel. | Example: A fridge magnet sticks to the steel door but not to an aluminium can.
Magnetism & Electromagnetism 07.01.4: Which way do magnetic field lines point? What does a compass show?	From north to south. A compass points along the field lines. Compasses show Earth has a magnetic core. | Example: A compass near a bar magnet points along the field lines, away from N and towards S.
Magnetism & Electromagnetism 07.02.1: Describe the magnetic field around a wire carrying current.	Circles around the wire. It's stronger with a bigger current and closer to the wire. | Example: Doubling the current in a wire makes its magnetic field stronger.
Magnetism & Electromagnetism 07.02.2: What is a solenoid? How do you make it stronger?	A coil of wire. Inside, the field is strong and uniform. Outside, it looks like a bar magnet's. Add an iron core to make an electromagnet. | Example: A scrapyard crane uses a large electromagnet that can be switched off to drop cars.
Magnetism & Electromagnetism 07.03.1: What is the motor effect? [Higher]	A wire carrying a current in a magnetic field experiences a force. | Example: A wire carrying current between the poles of a magnet jumps up or down.
Magnetism & Electromagnetism 07.03.2: Equation for the force on a current-carrying wire. [Higher]	Method: Length in metres, current in amps. Only the part of the wire inside the field counts. To find B: B = F ÷ (I l). | F = B I l | F in N, B = magnetic flux density in tesla (T), I in A, l in m | Example: B = 0.4 T, I = 5 A, l = 0.1 m: F = 0.4 × 5 × 0.1 = 0.2 N.
Magnetism & Electromagnetism 07.03.3: Fleming's left-hand rule: what does each finger show? [Higher]	Thumb = force (motion). First finger = field. Second finger = current. | Example: Field left to right and current into the page: the left-hand rule gives a force downwards.
Magnetism & Electromagnetism 07.03.4: Why does an electric motor's coil rotate? [Higher]	Current flows through a coil in a magnetic field. The forces on each side act in opposite directions, so the coil turns. | Example: Reversing the current reverses the forces, so the motor spins the other way.
Magnetism & Electromagnetism 07.03.5: How does a loudspeaker work? [Triple]	An alternating current flows in a coil in a magnetic field. The coil and cone vibrate, making pressure waves (sound). | Example: Music makes the current alternate quickly; the cone vibrates at the same frequency and produces sound.
Magnetism & Electromagnetism 07.04.1: What is the generator effect? [Higher] [Triple]	A pd is induced when a wire moves relative to a magnetic field, or the field around it changes. | Example: Pushing a magnet into a coil makes a meter connected to the coil flicker.
Magnetism & Electromagnetism 07.04.2: 3 ways to increase the induced pd. [Higher] [Triple]	Move the wire or magnet faster, use a stronger magnet, or use more turns on the coil. | Example: Spinning a bike dynamo faster makes the lamp brighter.
Magnetism & Electromagnetism 07.04.3: Alternator vs dynamo? [Higher] [Triple]	Alternator: makes ac, uses slip rings. Dynamo: makes dc, uses a split-ring commutator. | Example: A car alternator makes ac; an old bicycle dynamo with a commutator makes dc.
Magnetism & Electromagnetism 07.05.1: How does a transformer work? [Higher] [Triple]	An alternating current in the primary coil makes a changing magnetic field in the iron core. This induces a pd in the secondary coil. | Example: A phone charger uses a step-down transformer to reduce 230 V to about 5 V.
Magnetism & Electromagnetism 07.05.2: Two transformer equations. [Higher] [Triple]	Method: The voltage ratio equals the turns ratio. For a 100% efficient transformer, power in = power out, so if the pd goes up the current goes down. | Vp ÷ Vs = np ÷ ns | Vs Is = Vp Ip (if 100% efficient) | Example: Vp = 230 V, np = 1000, ns = 50: Vs = 230 × 50 ÷ 1000 = 11.5 V.
Space Physics 08.01.1: What is in our solar system? [Triple]	One star (the Sun), eight planets, dwarf planets such as Pluto, and moons. It is part of the Milky Way galaxy. | Example: Earth is the third planet from the Sun; the Moon is its natural satellite.
Space Physics 08.02.1: How does a star form? [Triple]	A nebula (cloud of dust and gas) is pulled together by gravity into a protostar. It heats up until fusion starts. | Example: The Orion Nebula is a cloud of gas and dust where new stars are forming.
Space Physics 08.02.2: Why is a main sequence star stable? [Triple]	The outward pressure from fusion balances the inward pull of gravity. | Example: The Sun has been stable for about 4.6 billion years because fusion balances gravity.
Space Physics 08.02.3: Life cycle of a star about the size of the Sun. [Triple]	Main sequence → red giant → white dwarf → black dwarf. | Example: The Sun will swell into a red giant in about 5 billion years, then shrink to a white dwarf.
Space Physics 08.02.4: Life cycle of a star much bigger than the Sun. [Triple]	Main sequence → red supergiant → supernova → neutron star or black hole. | Example: Betelgeuse is a red supergiant that will explode as a supernova.
Space Physics 08.02.5: Where do elements heavier than iron come from? [Triple]	They are made in supernovae, which spread elements throughout the universe. | Example: The gold and uranium on Earth were made in supernovae long ago.
Space Physics 08.03.1: What keeps planets and satellites in orbit? [Triple]	Gravity. In a circular orbit the speed stays the same but the direction (so velocity) keeps changing. | Example: The International Space Station is held in orbit by Earth's gravity.
Space Physics 08.03.2: How does orbit size affect speed? [Higher] [Triple]	A smaller orbit needs a higher speed to stay in a stable orbit. | Example: The ISS (about 400 km up) travels at about 7.7 km/s; the Moon (384 000 km away) moves at about 1 km/s.
Space Physics 08.04.1: What is red-shift? [Triple]	Light from distant galaxies has a longer wavelength than expected: it's shifted towards the red end of the spectrum. | Example: A galaxy's light at 656 nm is observed at 670 nm: shifted to a longer wavelength.
Space Physics 08.04.2: What does red-shift tell us about the universe? [Triple]	More distant galaxies are moving away faster, so the universe is expanding. This supports the Big Bang theory. | Example: A galaxy twice as far away is moving away about twice as fast.
Space Physics 08.04.3: What can't we explain about the universe yet? [Triple]	Dark mass (dark matter) and dark energy. | Example: Galaxies spin too fast for the matter we can see, which suggests dark matter.
Required Practicals 09.01.1: Specific heat capacity practical: method in brief.	Method: Plan the calculation: energy from the joulemeter (or V × I × t), the mass in kg, and the temperature change. Then c = ΔE ÷ (m Δθ). | Heat a block with an immersion heater. Measure energy supplied (joulemeter, or V × I × t) and the temperature rise. c = ΔE ÷ (m Δθ). Insulate the block. | Example: 1 kg aluminium block, 10 500 J supplied, 12 °C rise: c = 10 500 ÷ (1 × 12) = 875 J/kg °C.
Required Practicals 09.02.1: Resistance of a wire: independent, dependent and control variables.	Independent: length of wire. Dependent: resistance. Control: material, thickness, temperature (use a low current). | Example: Doubling the wire's length from 50 cm to 100 cm roughly doubles its resistance.
Required Practicals 09.02.2: I–V characteristics practical: method in brief.	Use a variable resistor to change the pd. Record current and pd for each setting. Swap the connections to get negative values. | Example: For a diode, the current stays at zero for negative pd and rises sharply above about 0.6 V.
Required Practicals 09.03.1: How do you find the density of an irregular object?	Method: Find the volume by displacement: the volume of water pushed out equals the object's volume. Then divide mass by volume. | Measure its mass on a balance. Find its volume by displacement in a eureka can or measuring cylinder. ρ = m ÷ V. | Example: A key with mass 24 g displaces 3 cm³ of water: ρ = 24 ÷ 3 = 8 g/cm³.
Required Practicals 09.04.1: Spring practical: how do you find the spring constant?	Add masses, measure the extension with a ruler each time. Plot force against extension. The gradient of the straight part = k. | Example: Force 2 N gives 0.04 m; 4 N gives 0.08 m: k = 4 ÷ 0.08 = 50 N/m.
Required Practicals 09.05.1: Acceleration practical: how do you keep mass constant when changing force?	Move masses from the trolley to the hanger, so the total mass of the system stays the same. | Example: Move a 100 g mass from the trolley to the hanger: the force increases by about 1 N, but the total mass stays the same.
Required Practicals 09.06.1: Ripple tank: how do you measure wavelength and frequency?	Wavelength: measure across 10 waves with a ruler, then divide by 10. Frequency: count waves passing a point in 10 s, then divide by 10. | Example: 10 waves span 25 cm, so λ = 2.5 cm. 30 waves pass in 10 s, so f = 3 Hz. v = 3 × 0.025 = 0.075 m/s.
Required Practicals 09.07.1: Leslie cube: which surface emits the most infrared? [Triple]	Matt black emits the most. Shiny silver emits the least. | Example: Readings: matt black 80, shiny black 60, matt white 40, shiny silver 20.
Required Practicals 09.08.1: Reflection and refraction practical: method in brief. [Triple]	Shine a ray from a ray box at a glass block. Mark the rays, draw the normal and measure the angles with a protractor. | Example: A ray enters a glass block at 40° to the normal and refracts to about 25°.
Required Practicals 09.09.1: Thermal insulation practical: method in brief. [Triple]	Wrap beakers of hot water in different materials. Measure the temperature drop over the same time. Keep the starting temperature and volume the same. | Example: Over 10 minutes: no insulation drops 20 °C, cotton wool 12 °C, bubble wrap 8 °C. Bubble wrap insulates best.
Units & Skills 10.01.1: SI prefixes: giga, mega, kilo, centi, milli, micro, nano.	Method: Big prefixes have positive powers and small ones have negative powers. To convert to the base unit, multiply by the power of 10. | G = 10⁹, M = 10⁶, k = 10³, c = 10⁻², m = 10⁻³, µ = 10⁻⁶, n = 10⁻⁹ | Example: 5 km = 5 × 10³ m = 5000 m. 3 ms = 3 × 10⁻³ s = 0.003 s.
Units & Skills 10.01.2: Convert 2.5 kW to W, and 45 minutes to seconds.	Method: kilo means × 1000, so 2.5 × 1000. There are 60 seconds in a minute, so multiply minutes by 60. | 2.5 kW = 2500 W. 45 min × 60 = 2700 s.
Units & Skills 10.01.3: How do you convert km/h to m/s?	Method: 1 km = 1000 m and 1 hour = 3600 s, so km/h × 1000 ÷ 3600, which is the same as ÷ 3.6. | Divide by 3.6. For example, 72 km/h ÷ 3.6 = 20 m/s.
Units & Skills 10.01.4: Write 300 000 000 m/s in standard form.	Method: Move the decimal point until the number is between 1 and 10. Count the places it moved: that is the power of 10. | 3 × 10⁸ m/s
Units & Skills 10.02.1: Define resolution.	The smallest change in a quantity that a measuring instrument can detect. | Example: A ruler with mm markings has a resolution of 1 mm; a digital thermometer reading to 0.1 °C has a resolution of 0.1 °C.
Units & Skills 10.02.2: Accurate vs precise?	Accurate: close to the true value. Precise: repeat readings are close together (little spread). | Example: True value 10.0 cm. Readings 9.1, 9.1, 9.2 are precise but not accurate. Readings 9.8, 10.3, 9.9 are more accurate.
Units & Skills 10.02.3: Random error vs systematic error?	Random: readings vary unpredictably (reduce by repeating and averaging). Systematic: every reading is off by the same amount, e.g. a zero error. | Example: Timing a pendulum by hand varies each time (random). A ruler with a worn end adds 2 mm to every reading (systematic).
Units & Skills 10.03.1: Independent, dependent and control variables?	Independent: the one you change. Dependent: the one you measure. Control: the ones you keep the same. | Example: Testing how ramp height affects a car's speed: height is independent, speed is dependent, the same car is a control.
Units & Skills 10.03.2: Repeatable vs reproducible?	Repeatable: same results when the same person repeats it. Reproducible: same results when someone else does it, or with different equipment. | Example: You get 4.1 s, 4.2 s, 4.1 s (repeatable). Your friend gets 4.2 s with a light gate (reproducible).
Units & Skills 10.03.3: What does a straight-line graph through the origin show?	The two variables are directly proportional. | Example: Force and extension for a spring (before the limit of proportionality) give a straight line through the origin.
