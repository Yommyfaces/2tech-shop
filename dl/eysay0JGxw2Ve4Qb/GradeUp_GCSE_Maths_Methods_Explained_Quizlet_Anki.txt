Number 01.01 – Factors, multiples and primes: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Factor: a number that divides exactly into another (factors of 12: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12). | Multiple: a number in the times table (multiples of 4: 4, 8, 12…). | Prime number: has exactly two factors, 1 and itself (2, 3, 5, 7, 11…). 1 is NOT prime, and 2 is the only even prime. | Square number: a number times itself (5² = 25). | Cube number: a number times itself three times (2³ = 8). | USE IT WHEN – Recognising factors, multiples, primes, squares and cubes. These facts are used in almost every Number question. | STEPS – 1. To list factors, find pairs that multiply to the number, starting from 1 (1 × 36, 2 × 18, 3 × 12…). | 2. Stop when the pairs meet or repeat; every number in a pair is a factor. | 3. A number is prime if its only factor pair is 1 × itself. | 4. Multiples: count up in the number's times table.
Number 01.01 – Worked example: List the factors of 36. Is 37 prime?	Factor pairs of 36: 1 × 36, 2 × 18, 3 × 12, 4 × 9, 6 × 6 | Factors: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18, 36 | 37: try 2, 3, 5: none divide exactly. 7² = 49 is bigger than 37, so stop | Answer: Factors of 36: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18, 36. 37 is prime.
Number 01.01 – Factors, multiples and primes: what is the common mistake?	Thinking 1 is prime. A prime has exactly TWO factors, and 1 only has one.
Number 01.02 – Prime factors, HCF and LCM: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Factor: a number that divides exactly into another (factors of 12: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12). | Multiple: a number in the times table (multiples of 4: 4, 8, 12, 16…). | Prime number: has exactly two factors, 1 and itself (2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13…). 1 is NOT prime, and 2 is the only even prime. | Product of primes: a number written as primes multiplied together (12 = 2 × 2 × 3 = 2² × 3). | USE IT WHEN – A question asks for the highest common factor, the lowest common multiple, or when two repeating events next happen together. | STEPS – 1. Write each number as a product of primes (use a factor tree). | 2. HCF: multiply the primes the numbers SHARE, using the lower power of each. | 3. LCM: multiply EVERY prime that appears, using the higher power of each. | 4. Check: HCF × LCM = the two numbers multiplied together.
Number 01.02 – Worked example: Find the HCF and LCM of 60 and 84.	Factor tree for 60: 60 → 6 × 10 → (2 × 3) × (2 × 5) | So 60 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 5 = 2² × 3 × 5 | Factor tree for 84: 84 → 4 × 21 → (2 × 2) × (3 × 7) | So 84 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 7 = 2² × 3 × 7 | Shared primes: 2² and 3, so HCF = 4 × 3 = 12 | Every prime, highest power: 2², 3, 5, 7 | LCM = 4 × 3 × 5 × 7 = 420 | Answer: HCF = 12, LCM = 420
Number 01.02 – Prime factors, HCF and LCM: what is the common mistake?	Multiplying the two numbers (60 × 84 = 5040) gives A common multiple, not the LOWEST one.
Number 01.03 – Order of operations and negative numbers: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – BIDMAS: the order to do a calculation: Brackets, Indices (powers and roots), Division and Multiplication (left to right), then Addition and Subtraction (left to right). | Negative number: a number less than 0 (−5). | Same signs: × or ÷ gives a positive answer. | Different signs: × or ÷ gives a negative answer. | USE IT WHEN – Any calculation with more than one operation, or with negative numbers. | STEPS – 1. Work out anything in brackets first. | 2. Then powers and roots. | 3. Then × and ÷, working left to right. | 4. Then + and −, left to right. Subtracting a negative is the same as adding.
Number 01.03 – Worked example: Work out (−3)² − 4 × (5 − 7)	Brackets: 5 − 7 = −2 | Indices: (−3)² = 9 | Multiply: 4 × (−2) = −8 | Subtract: 9 − (−8) = 9 + 8 | Answer: 17
Number 01.03 – Order of operations and negative numbers: what is the common mistake?	Working left to right without BIDMAS: 3 + 4 × 2 is 11, not 14. Also, −3² means −(3²) = −9, but (−3)² = 9.
Number 01.04 – Multiplying and dividing without a calculator: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Product: the answer to a multiplication. | Quotient: the answer to a division. | Place value: the value of a digit from its position (in 47, the 4 means 40). | Grid method: split numbers into tens and units and multiply each part. | USE IT WHEN – Non-calculator multiplication and division, including decimals. | STEPS – 1. Multiply: split one number by place value (47 = 40 + 7), multiply each part, then add. | 2. Decimals: multiply as whole numbers, then put the decimal places back. | 3. Divide by a decimal: multiply both numbers by 10, 100… until you divide by a whole number. | 4. Finish with short division (bus stop).
Number 01.04 – Worked example: Work out 2.3 × 4.7 and 7.2 ÷ 0.06	23 × 47 = 23 × 40 + 23 × 7 = 920 + 161 = 1081 | 2 decimal places in total, so 2.3 × 4.7 = 10.81 | 7.2 ÷ 0.06: multiply both by 100 → 720 ÷ 6 | 720 ÷ 6 = 120 | Answer: 10.81 and 120
Number 01.04 – Multiplying and dividing without a calculator: what is the common mistake?	Forgetting to put the decimal point back, or multiplying only ONE of the numbers by 100 when dividing by a decimal.
Number 01.05 – Rounding and estimating: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Estimate: an approximate answer using rounded numbers. ≈ means 'approximately equal to'. | USE IT WHEN – Rounding answers to a given accuracy, or estimating a calculation without a calculator. | STEPS – 1. Find the last digit you are keeping. | 2. Look at the next digit: 5 or more rounds up; 4 or less stays the same. | 3. Keep zeros that hold the place value (4720 to 2 s.f. is 4700, not 47). | 4. To estimate, round every number to 1 s.f., then calculate.
Number 01.05 – Worked example: Estimate (61.4 × 2.93) ÷ 0.48	Round to 1 s.f.: 61.4 ≈ 60, 2.93 ≈ 3, 0.48 ≈ 0.5 | 60 × 3 = 180 | 180 ÷ 0.5 = 360 (dividing by 0.5 doubles) | Answer: ≈ 360
Number 01.05 – Rounding and estimating: what is the common mistake?	Rounding 0.48 to 0, which makes the division impossible. Round to 1 SIGNIFICANT figure (0.5), not to a whole number.
Number 01.06 – Error intervals and bounds: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Rounded value: the number after rounding. | Bounds: the smallest (lower bound) and largest (upper bound) values it could have been before rounding. 6.2 to 1 d.p. could be anything from 6.15 up to, but not including, 6.25. | Error interval: written as 6.15 ≤ x < 6.25. d.p. means decimal places. | USE IT WHEN – A measurement is rounded, and you need its smallest and largest possible values, or the bounds of a calculation. | STEPS – 1. Halve the rounding unit (nearest 0.1 → 0.05 either side). | 2. Lower bound = value − half; upper bound = value + half. Write LB ≤ x < UB. | 3. For a calculation, pick the bounds that make the answer biggest (or smallest). | 4. Divide: largest answer = upper ÷ lower; smallest answer = lower ÷ upper.
Number 01.06 – Worked example: x = 6.2 (1 d.p.) and y = 3 (nearest whole number). Find the upper bound of x ÷ y.	x: 6.15 ≤ x < 6.25 | y: 2.5 ≤ y < 3.5 | Biggest answer = 6.25 ÷ 2.5 | Answer: 2.5
Number 01.06 – Error intervals and bounds: what is the common mistake?	Using upper ÷ upper (6.25 ÷ 3.5). To make a division big, you need a big top and a SMALL bottom.
Number 01.07 – Adding and subtracting fractions: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Numerator: the top number (how many pieces). | Denominator: the bottom number (the size of the pieces: 4 means quarters). | Mixed number: a whole number and a fraction (2⅓). | Improper fraction: the top is bigger than the bottom (7/3). | Convert: whole × denominator + numerator, so 2⅓ = (2 × 3 + 1)/3 = 7/3. | Equivalent fractions: the same value (1/2 = 2/4 = 3/6). | USE IT WHEN – Any + or − with fractions or mixed numbers. | STEPS – 1. Change mixed numbers into improper fractions. | 2. Find a common denominator (the LCM of the denominators). | 3. Change each fraction to that denominator: whatever you do to the bottom, do to the top. | 4. Add or subtract the numerators only, then simplify.
Number 01.07 – Worked example: Work out 2⅓ − 1¾	7/3 − 7/4 | Common denominator 12: 28/12 − 21/12 | = 7/12 | Answer: 7/12
Number 01.07 – Adding and subtracting fractions: what is the common mistake?	Adding the denominators too: 3/4 + 2/5 is NOT 5/9. The denominator only tells you the size of the pieces.
Number 01.08 – Multiplying and dividing fractions: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Reciprocal: the fraction turned upside down (the reciprocal of 3/4 is 4/3, and of 5 is 1/5). | Simplify: divide the top and bottom by the same number (6/8 = 3/4). | Improper fraction: the top is bigger than the bottom (12/5 = 2⅖). | USE IT WHEN – Any × or ÷ with fractions or mixed numbers. | STEPS – 1. Change mixed numbers into improper fractions first. | 2. Dividing? Keep the first fraction, Change ÷ to ×, Flip the second (KCF). | 3. Multiply the tops together and the bottoms together. | 4. Simplify, and change back to a mixed number if needed.
Number 01.08 – Worked example: Work out 2⅖ ÷ 1⅓	12/5 ÷ 4/3 | = 12/5 × 3/4 | = 36/20 = 9/5 | Answer: 1⅘
Number 01.08 – Multiplying and dividing fractions: what is the common mistake?	Flipping the FIRST fraction, or multiplying mixed numbers without making them improper (2 × 1 and ⅖ × ⅓ separately is wrong).
Number 01.09 – Fractions, decimals and recurring decimals: key words and steps? [Higher]	KEY WORDS – Recurring decimal: digits that repeat forever (1/3 = 0.333…). A dot over a digit shows it repeats (0.2̇ = 0.222…). Dots over the first and last digits show a repeating block (0.4̇7̇ = 0.474747…). 0.23̇ means only the 3 repeats: 0.2333… | USE IT WHEN – Proving or showing that a recurring decimal equals a fraction. | STEPS – 1. Let x = the decimal and write out several digits. | 2. Multiply by powers of 10 to get two numbers with the SAME recurring part after the point. | 3. Subtract to remove the recurring part. | 4. Solve for x and simplify the fraction.
Number 01.09 – Worked example: Write 0.23̇ (0.2333…) as a fraction in its simplest form. [Higher]	10x = 2.333… | 100x = 23.333… | 100x − 10x: 90x = 21 | x = 21/90 | Answer: 7/30
Number 01.09 – Fractions, decimals and recurring decimals: what is the common mistake? [Higher]	Using x and 10x when the recurring part starts later: 10x − x = 2.1 leaves a decimal. Line up the recurring parts first.
Number 01.10 – Percentages and percentage change: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Percent: 'out of 100' (15% = 15/100 = 0.15). | Multiplier: the decimal you multiply by to get the new amount. 100% + 15% = 115%, so the multiplier is 1.15; 100% − 15% = 85%, so it is 0.85. | Original value: the amount before the change. | USE IT WHEN – Increasing or decreasing by a percentage, especially more than once, or finding a percentage change. | STEPS – 1. Increase by r%: multiply by (1 + r/100). A 15% increase is × 1.15. | 2. Decrease by r%: multiply by (1 − r/100). A 15% decrease is × 0.85. | 3. Several changes: multiply by each multiplier in turn. | 4. Percentage change = change ÷ ORIGINAL × 100.
Number 01.10 – Worked example: A £480 TV is reduced by 15%, then the new price rises by 10%. Find the final price.	480 × 0.85 = 408 | 408 × 1.10 = 448.80 | Answer: £448.80
Number 01.10 – Percentages and percentage change: what is the common mistake?	Combining the changes: −15% then +10% is NOT −5% (that would give £456). The 10% is of the NEW price.
Number 01.11 – Reverse percentages: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Original: the amount BEFORE the change (the one you are finding). | Multiplier: a 20% increase is × 1.2 and a 20% decrease is × 0.8 (see Method 04). | Reverse: you undo a multiplication by dividing. | USE IT WHEN – You are given the amount AFTER a percentage change and asked for the original. | STEPS – 1. Write the change as a multiplier (e.g. a 20% increase is 1.2). | 2. The original × multiplier = the new amount. | 3. So original = new amount ÷ multiplier. | 4. Check by multiplying back.
Number 01.11 – Worked example: After a 20% increase a bike costs £180. What did it cost before?	Multiplier = 1.2 | Original = 180 ÷ 1.2 = 150 | Check: 150 × 1.2 = 180 ✓ | Answer: £150
Number 01.11 – Reverse percentages: what is the common mistake?	Taking 20% OFF the new price (180 × 0.8 = £144). The 20% was of the original, not of £180.
Number 01.12 – Simple and compound interest: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Interest: money added to savings. | Compound interest: each year's interest is added on, so next year's interest is worked out on the bigger amount. | Simple interest: the same amount every year, worked out on the original only. | Depreciation: a value going DOWN each year (e.g. a car). 1.025⁴ means 1.025 × 1.025 × 1.025 × 1.025. | USE IT WHEN – A value grows or shrinks by the same percentage every year (or month). | STEPS – 1. Find the multiplier: growth 1 + r/100, depreciation 1 − r/100. | 2. Amount = original × multiplier^n, where n is the number of years. | 3. Interest earned = final amount − original amount. | 4. Round money to 2 decimal places only at the end.
Number 01.12 – Worked example: £3000 is invested at 2.5% compound interest per year. Find the value after 4 years.	Multiplier = 1.025 | 3000 × 1.025⁴ = 3311.4387… | Answer: £3311.44
Number 01.12 – Simple and compound interest: what is the common mistake?	Using simple interest: 2.5% of £3000 is £75, × 4 = £300 gives £3300. Compound interest is worked out on the NEW amount each year.
Number 01.13 – Laws of indices: key words and steps? [Higher]	KEY WORDS – Power (index): the small number showing how many times to multiply (2⁴ = 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 16). | Base: the number being multiplied (the 2 in 2⁴). | Root: the opposite of a power (√9 = 3 because 3² = 9; ∛8 = 2 because 2³ = 8). | Reciprocal: 1 over the number (the reciprocal of 8 is 1/8). | USE IT WHEN – Simplifying expressions with powers, or evaluating negative and fractional powers. | STEPS – 1. Multiply: add the powers. Divide: subtract the powers. Power of a power: multiply. | 2. Negative power: 'one over' (a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ). | 3. Fractional power: the bottom is a root, the top is a power. Do the root first. | 4. Apply the power to the number AND each letter inside a bracket.
Number 01.13 – Worked example: Simplify (8x⁶)^(2/3) [Higher]	8^(2/3) = (∛8)² = 2² = 4 | (x⁶)^(2/3) = x^(6 × 2/3) = x⁴ | Answer: 4x⁴
Number 01.13 – Laws of indices: what is the common mistake? [Higher]	Thinking a negative power makes the answer negative: 2⁻³ = 1/8, not −8.
Number 01.14 – Standard form: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Standard form: a × 10ⁿ, where a is at least 1 and less than 10 (3400 = 3.4 × 10³; 0.005 = 5 × 10⁻³). A positive power means a big number; a negative power means a small number. 10³ = 1000 and 10⁻² = 0.01. | USE IT WHEN – Very large or very small numbers, especially multiplying or dividing them. | STEPS – 1. Multiply (or divide) the number parts. | 2. Add the powers of 10 when multiplying; subtract them when dividing. | 3. If the number part is not between 1 and 10, adjust it and change the power to match. | 4. Check: a bigger number part means a smaller power, and vice versa.
Number 01.14 – Worked example: Work out (3 × 10⁴) × (6 × 10⁵)	3 × 6 = 18 and 10⁴ × 10⁵ = 10⁹ | 18 × 10⁹ = 1.8 × 10 × 10⁹ | Answer: 1.8 × 10¹⁰
Number 01.14 – Standard form: what is the common mistake?	Leaving the answer as 18 × 10⁹. It is not in standard form until the first number is between 1 and 10.
Number 01.15 – Surds: key words and steps? [Higher]	KEY WORDS – Surd: a square root that does not give a whole number (√2, √50). √4 = 2 is NOT a surd. | Square numbers: 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 64, 81, 100. | Rule: √(a × b) = √a × √b. | Rationalise: rewrite a fraction so there is no root on the bottom. | USE IT WHEN – Simplifying square roots, or removing a root from the denominator (rationalising). | STEPS – 1. Split the number into a square number × something: √50 = √25 × √2 = 5√2. | 2. Only add or subtract surds with the same root (like terms). | 3. Rationalise: multiply top and bottom by the root in the denominator. | 4. Simplify the final fraction.
Number 01.15 – Worked example: (a) Simplify √50 + √18   (b) Write 12/√3 in the form a√3 [Higher]	(a) 5√2 + 3√2 = 8√2 | (b) 12/√3 × √3/√3 = 12√3/3 | Answer: (a) 8√2   (b) 4√3
Number 01.15 – Surds: what is the common mistake? [Higher]	Adding inside the roots: √50 + √18 is NOT √68. Simplify each surd first.
Number 01.16 – Counting outcomes: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Outcome: one possible result. | Product rule: if one choice can be made in a ways and another in b ways, together there are a × b ways. | Without repetition: once something is used it can't be used again, so the number of choices goes down by 1 each time. | USE IT WHEN – Counting how many combinations, codes or arrangements are possible. | STEPS – 1. Count the choices for each stage. | 2. If items can't repeat, reduce the choices by 1 at each stage. | 3. Multiply the choices together. | 4. Check whether repeats are allowed in the question.
Number 01.16 – Worked example: A password is 2 letters (A–Z) then 3 digits (0–9). Letters can repeat; digits cannot. How many passwords?	Letters: 26 × 26 = 676 | Digits, no repeats: 10 × 9 × 8 = 720 | Total = 676 × 720 | Answer: 486 720
Number 01.16 – Counting outcomes: what is the common mistake?	Adding the choices (26 + 26 + 10…) instead of multiplying them.
Algebra 02.01 – Simplifying and substituting: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Term: a single part of an expression (3x, −5, 4ab). | Like terms: exactly the same letters and powers (3x and 7x, but not 3x and 3x²). | Coefficient: the number in front of a letter (the 5 in 5y). | Expression: no equals sign (3x + 2). | Equation: has an equals sign and can be solved (3x + 2 = 11). | Formula: links variables (A = πr²). | Identity: true for every value, shown with ≡. | USE IT WHEN – Tidying up expressions, and finding the value of an expression when you know the letters. | STEPS – 1. Simplify: group like terms, keeping the sign in front of each term. | 2. Add or subtract the coefficients of like terms. | 3. Substitute: replace each letter with its value, in brackets. | 4. Follow BIDMAS: powers first, then ×, then + and −.
Algebra 02.01 – Worked example: Simplify 4x + 3y − x + 5y, then find its value when x = 2 and y = −1.	x terms: 4x − x = 3x | y terms: 3y + 5y = 8y | So 3x + 8y | 3(2) + 8(−1) = 6 − 8 | Answer: 3x + 8y = −2
Algebra 02.01 – Simplifying and substituting: what is the common mistake?	Combining unlike terms: 3x + 8y is NOT 11xy.
Algebra 02.02 – Expanding brackets: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Expand: multiply out the brackets. | Single bracket: multiply the term outside by every term inside (3(x + 4) = 3x + 12). | Double brackets: multiply every term in the first bracket by every term in the second (FOIL: First, Outer, Inner, Last). | Simplify: collect like terms afterwards. | USE IT WHEN – Removing brackets from an expression or equation. | STEPS – 1. Single bracket: multiply the outside term by each term inside. | 2. Double brackets: First, Outer, Inner, Last. | 3. Watch the signs: a negative times a negative is positive. | 4. Collect like terms.
Algebra 02.02 – Worked example: Expand and simplify (2x − 3)(x + 5)	First: 2x × x = 2x² | Outer: 2x × 5 = 10x | Inner: −3 × x = −3x | Last: −3 × 5 = −15 | 2x² + 10x − 3x − 15 | Answer: 2x² + 7x − 15
Algebra 02.02 – Expanding brackets: what is the common mistake?	Writing (x + 5)² = x² + 25. A squared bracket has a middle term: (x + 5)(x + 5) = x² + 10x + 25.
Algebra 02.03 – Factorising: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Factorise: put back into brackets (the reverse of expanding). | Highest common factor (HCF): the biggest number and letters that go into every term. | Quadratic: an expression where x² is the highest power (x² + 7x + 12). | Difference of two squares: a² − b² = (a + b)(a − b). | USE IT WHEN – Writing an expression as brackets multiplied together, e.g. to solve a quadratic or simplify a fraction. | STEPS – 1. Always take out the HCF first. | 2. x² + bx + c: find two numbers that multiply to c and add to b. | 3. Something² − something²: use the difference of two squares. | 4. Check by expanding your answer.
Algebra 02.03 – Worked example: Factorise (a) 8x² − 12x   (b) x² − 2x − 15	(a) HCF of 8x² and 12x is 4x: 4x(2x − 3) | (b) Multiply to −15, add to −2: −5 and +3 | (x − 5)(x + 3) | Check: x² + 3x − 5x − 15 = x² − 2x − 15 ✓ | Answer: (a) 4x(2x − 3)   (b) (x − 5)(x + 3)
Algebra 02.03 – Factorising: what is the common mistake?	Not factorising FULLY: 2x(4x − 6) still has a common factor of 2 inside the bracket.
Algebra 02.04 – Solving linear equations: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Equation: a statement with an equals sign that is true for a certain value. | Solve: find the value of the letter. | Inverse operation: the opposite operation that undoes another (+ undoes −, × undoes ÷). | Balance: whatever you do to one side, do to the other. | USE IT WHEN – Finding x in an equation with no x² term. | STEPS – 1. Expand any brackets, and clear fractions by multiplying both sides. | 2. Collect the x terms on the side with more x. | 3. Move the numbers to the other side using inverse operations. | 4. Divide by the number in front of x, then check by substituting.
Algebra 02.04 – Worked example: Solve 3(2x − 1) = 4x + 9	Expand: 6x − 3 = 4x + 9 | Subtract 4x: 2x − 3 = 9 | Add 3: 2x = 12 | Divide by 2: x = 6 | Check: 3(12 − 1) = 33 and 24 + 9 = 33 ✓ | Answer: x = 6
Algebra 02.04 – Solving linear equations: what is the common mistake?	Using the wrong inverse: from 2x − 3 = 9, writing 2x = 6 (subtracting 3 instead of adding 3).
Algebra 02.05 – Rearranging formulae: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Formula: a rule linking variables (v = u + at). | Subject: the letter on its own on one side (v is the subject of v = u + at). | Rearrange: change which letter is the subject, using inverse operations. | USE IT WHEN – Making a different letter the subject of a formula. | STEPS – 1. Circle the letter you want on its own. | 2. Undo what is done to it in reverse BIDMAS order, doing the same to both sides. | 3. If the letter appears twice, collect those terms on one side and factorise it out. | 4. Write the answer with the new subject on the left.
Algebra 02.05 – Worked example: Make r the subject of A = πr²	r is squared, then multiplied by π | Divide both sides by π: A/π = r² | Square-root both sides | Answer: r = √(A/π)
Algebra 02.05 – Rearranging formulae: what is the common mistake?	Undoing in the wrong order: square-rooting before dividing by π gives √A/π, which is wrong.
Algebra 02.06 – Solving quadratics by factorising: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Quadratic equation: x² is the highest power (x² − 5x + 6 = 0). | Roots (solutions): the values of x that make it true; there are usually two. | Zero product rule: if A × B = 0, then A = 0 or B = 0. | USE IT WHEN – Solving a quadratic equation that factorises. | STEPS – 1. Rearrange so one side is 0. | 2. Factorise into two brackets. | 3. Set each bracket equal to 0. | 4. Solve each one to get two answers.
Algebra 02.06 – Worked example: Solve x² + 2x = 24	Make it = 0: x² + 2x − 24 = 0 | Multiply to −24, add to 2: +6 and −4 | (x + 6)(x − 4) = 0 | x + 6 = 0 or x − 4 = 0 | Answer: x = −6 or x = 4
Algebra 02.06 – Solving quadratics by factorising: what is the common mistake?	Getting the signs back to front: from x + 6 = 0 the answer is x = −6, not 6.
Algebra 02.07 – The quadratic formula: key words and steps? [Higher]	KEY WORDS – Quadratic formula: x = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac)) ÷ 2a. ± means work it out once with + and once with −. | USE IT WHEN – Solving a quadratic that will not factorise, especially when the question asks for decimal answers. | STEPS – 1. Rearrange into ax² + bx + c = 0 and write down a, b and c with their signs. | 2. Work out b² − 4ac first. | 3. Substitute into the formula, using brackets for negative numbers. | 4. Work out the + answer and the − answer, then round.
Algebra 02.07 – Worked example: Solve x² + 3x − 5 = 0, giving answers to 2 d.p. [Higher]	a = 1, b = 3, c = −5 | b² − 4ac = 9 − 4(1)(−5) = 9 + 20 = 29 | x = (−3 ± √29) ÷ 2 | x = (−3 + 5.385…) ÷ 2 = 1.192… | x = (−3 − 5.385…) ÷ 2 = −4.192… | Answer: x = 1.19 or x = −4.19
Algebra 02.07 – The quadratic formula: what is the common mistake? [Higher]	Dividing only the square root by 2a. The WHOLE top, −b ± √(b² − 4ac), is divided by 2a.
Algebra 02.08 – Completing the square: key words and steps? [Higher]	KEY WORDS – Completing the square: writing x² + bx + c as (x + p)² + q. p: half of b. q: what is left after taking away p². | Turning point: y = (x + p)² + q has its minimum point at (−p, q). | USE IT WHEN – Writing a quadratic as (x + p)² + q, finding its turning point, or solving it exactly. | STEPS – 1. Halve the x coefficient: that number goes in the bracket. | 2. Expanding the bracket adds p², so subtract p². | 3. Combine with the original number to find q. | 4. Turning point: x = −p, y = q.
Algebra 02.08 – Worked example: Write x² + 6x + 2 in the form (x + p)² + q and give the turning point. [Higher]	Half of 6 is 3: (x + 3)² | (x + 3)² = x² + 6x + 9, so subtract 9 | (x + 3)² − 9 + 2 = (x + 3)² − 7 | Turning point: (−3, −7) | Answer: (x + 3)² − 7, turning point (−3, −7)
Algebra 02.08 – Completing the square: what is the common mistake? [Higher]	Forgetting to subtract p², or giving the turning point as (3, −7). The x-coordinate changes sign.
Algebra 02.09 – Simultaneous equations: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Simultaneous equations: two equations with two unknowns, both true at the same time. | Elimination: add or subtract the equations to remove one letter. | Substitution: replace one letter using the other equation. | Coefficient: the number in front of a letter. | USE IT WHEN – Finding two unknowns from two equations. | STEPS – 1. Make the coefficients of one letter match (multiply an equation if needed). | 2. Same signs: subtract the equations. Different signs: add them. | 3. Solve for the letter that is left. | 4. Substitute back to find the other letter, then check in the other equation.
Algebra 02.09 – Worked example: Solve 2x + 3y = 13 and 5x − 3y = 1	+3y and −3y have different signs, so add the equations | 7x = 14, so x = 2 | Substitute: 2(2) + 3y = 13 → 3y = 9 → y = 3 | Check: 5(2) − 3(3) = 1 ✓ | Answer: x = 2, y = 3
Algebra 02.09 – Simultaneous equations: what is the common mistake?	Subtracting when the signs are different (or adding when they're the same), which doubles the letter instead of removing it.
Algebra 02.10 – Linear inequalities: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Inequality: shows a range of values. < less than; > greater than; ≤ less than or equal to; ≥ greater than or equal to. | Integer: a whole number. | Number line: an open circle ○ means not included (< or >); a filled circle ● means included (≤ or ≥). | USE IT WHEN – Solving inequalities, listing integer solutions, or showing them on a number line. | STEPS – 1. Solve it like an equation, doing the same to both sides. | 2. If you multiply or divide by a negative number, flip the sign. | 3. For a double inequality, do the same to all three parts. | 4. List integers carefully, checking whether each end is included.
Algebra 02.10 – Worked example: Solve 5 − 2x > 11	Subtract 5: −2x > 6 | Divide by −2 and FLIP the sign: x < −3 | Check with x = −4: 5 + 8 = 13, and 13 > 11 ✓ | Answer: x < −3
Algebra 02.10 – Linear inequalities: what is the common mistake?	Forgetting to flip the sign when dividing by a negative number (writing x > −3).
Algebra 02.11 – Quadratic inequalities: key words and steps? [Higher]	KEY WORDS – Quadratic: has an x² term (x² − x − 12). | Inequality signs: < less than, > greater than, ≤ less than or equal to, ≥ greater than or equal to. | Roots: the values of x that make the expression equal 0. A quadratic with +x² has a U-shaped graph. | USE IT WHEN – Solving an inequality with an x² term, such as x² − x − 12 < 0. | STEPS – 1. Rearrange to 0 on one side, then factorise. | 2. Find the roots (where the quadratic equals 0). | 3. Sketch the U-shaped graph through the roots. | 4. < 0 is the part below the axis (between the roots); > 0 is above it (two separate regions).
Algebra 02.11 – Worked example: Solve x² − x − 12 < 0 [Higher]	(x − 4)(x + 3) < 0 | Roots: x = 4 and x = −3 | Below the axis between the roots | Answer: −3 < x < 4
Algebra 02.11 – Quadratic inequalities: what is the common mistake? [Higher]	Writing x < 4 and x < −3, as if it were a linear inequality. Always sketch the graph to see which region you need.
Algebra 02.12 – Sequences and the nth term: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Sequence: a list of numbers following a rule. | Term: each number in it. | Common difference: the amount added each time in a linear sequence. nth term: a formula for any term (3n + 4: put n = 1, 2, 3… to get 7, 10, 13…). | Geometric sequence: multiply by the same number (the common ratio) each time. | USE IT WHEN – Finding or using the nth term of a sequence. | STEPS – 1. Find the difference between terms: this is the number in front of n. | 2. Find the zero term (go back one step from the first term). | 3. nth term = difference × n + zero term. | 4. Check with the first two terms.
Algebra 02.12 – Worked example: Find the nth term of 5, 9, 13, 17, …	Difference = 4, so 4n | Zero term = 5 − 4 = 1 | nth term = 4n + 1 | Check: n = 1 gives 5 ✓, n = 2 gives 9 ✓ | Answer: 4n + 1
Algebra 02.12 – Sequences and the nth term: what is the common mistake?	Writing n + 4 instead of 4n + 1. The difference is the number MULTIPLYING n.
Algebra 02.13 – Algebraic fractions: key words and steps? [Higher]	KEY WORDS – Factorise: put into brackets, the reverse of expanding (x² + 5x + 6 = (x + 2)(x + 3)). | Numerator and denominator: the top and bottom of the fraction. | Difference of two squares: a² − b² = (a + b)(a − b), so x² − 9 = (x + 3)(x − 3). | USE IT WHEN – An algebraic fraction can be simplified, usually with quadratics on the top and bottom. | STEPS – 1. Factorise the numerator fully. | 2. Factorise the denominator fully. | 3. Cancel any bracket that appears on BOTH top and bottom. | 4. Leave the answer factorised.
Algebra 02.13 – Worked example: Simplify (x² − 9) / (x² + x − 6) [Higher]	Top: (x + 3)(x − 3) | Bottom: (x + 3)(x − 2) | Cancel (x + 3) | Answer: (x − 3)/(x − 2)
Algebra 02.13 – Algebraic fractions: what is the common mistake? [Higher]	Cancelling single terms: crossing out the x² terms is wrong. You can only cancel whole factors (brackets).
Algebra 02.14 – Algebraic proof: key words and steps? [Higher]	KEY WORDS – Integer: a whole number (…, −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, …). | Consecutive: one after another (5, 6, 7 is written n, n + 1, n + 2). | Even number: 2n. | Odd number: 2n + 1. | Multiple of 3: can be written as 3 × (something). | USE IT WHEN – 'Prove that…' or 'Show that…' questions about odd, even or consecutive numbers. | STEPS – 1. Write the numbers algebraically: n, n + 1 (consecutive); 2n (even); 2n + 1 (odd). | 2. Write the expression the question describes and simplify it. | 3. Factorise to show the property (e.g. 3( … ) is a multiple of 3). | 4. Finish with a sentence that states what you have proved.
Algebra 02.14 – Worked example: Prove that the sum of three consecutive integers is a multiple of 3. [Higher]	n + (n + 1) + (n + 2) | = 3n + 3 | = 3(n + 1) | Answer: 3(n + 1) is a multiple of 3
Algebra 02.14 – Algebraic proof: what is the common mistake? [Higher]	Testing a few numbers (1 + 2 + 3 = 6 …). Examples never prove a rule is true for ALL numbers.
Algebra 02.15 – Iteration: key words and steps? [Higher]	KEY WORDS – Iteration: repeating a process, using each answer as the next input. x₀ is the starting value, x₁ the next, and so on; xₙ₊₁ means 'the next value after xₙ'. | ANS button: recalls your last answer on the calculator, so nothing is rounded. | USE IT WHEN – Using an iterative formula to find an approximate solution to an equation. | STEPS – 1. Substitute the starting value x₀ into the formula to get x₁. | 2. Put x₁ back in to get x₂, and so on. | 3. Use the ANS button so you keep full accuracy. | 4. Round only at the end, to the accuracy asked for.
Algebra 02.15 – Worked example: Use xₙ₊₁ = 2 + 1/xₙ with x₀ = 2 to find x₄ to 4 d.p. [Higher]	x₁ = 2 + 1/2 = 2.5 | x₂ = 2 + 1/2.5 = 2.4 | x₃ = 2.416666… | x₄ = 2.413793… | Answer: x₄ = 2.4138
Algebra 02.15 – Iteration: what is the common mistake? [Higher]	Rounding each value and retyping it. Small rounding errors build up and can change the last digit.
Graphs & Functions 03.01 – Straight-line graphs: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Gradient: how steep a line is, i.e. how far up for every 1 across (positive slopes up, negative slopes down). y-intercept (c): where the line crosses the y-axis. | Coordinates (x, y): across first, then up. In y = mx + c, m is the gradient and c is the y-intercept. | USE IT WHEN – Finding a line's equation from two points, or from a point and a gradient. | STEPS – 1. Gradient m = change in y ÷ change in x (rise over run). | 2. Put m into y = mx + c. | 3. Substitute one point's x and y to find c. | 4. Write the final equation y = mx + c.
Graphs & Functions 03.01 – Worked example: Find the equation of the line through (2, 5) and (6, 13).	m = (13 − 5) ÷ (6 − 2) = 8 ÷ 4 = 2 | 5 = 2 × 2 + c, so c = 1 | Answer: y = 2x + 1
Graphs & Functions 03.01 – Straight-line graphs: what is the common mistake?	Dividing the change in x by the change in y. It is always y on top.
Graphs & Functions 03.02 – Parallel and perpendicular lines: key words and steps? [Higher]	KEY WORDS – Parallel: lines that never meet; they have the same steepness. | Perpendicular: lines that meet at a right angle (90°). | Negative reciprocal: flip the fraction and change the sign (2 becomes −½; −¾ becomes 4/3). | USE IT WHEN – Finding the equation of a line parallel or perpendicular to another line. | STEPS – 1. Parallel lines have the SAME gradient. | 2. Perpendicular gradient: flip the fraction and change the sign (2 becomes −½). The two gradients multiply to −1. | 3. Put the new gradient into y = mx + c. | 4. Substitute the given point to find c.
Graphs & Functions 03.02 – Worked example: Find the line perpendicular to y = 3x − 2 that passes through (6, 1). [Higher]	Perpendicular gradient = −1/3 | 1 = −1/3 × 6 + c, so 1 = −2 + c | c = 3 | Answer: y = −⅓x + 3
Graphs & Functions 03.02 – Parallel and perpendicular lines: what is the common mistake? [Higher]	Only changing the sign (−3) or only flipping (⅓). You must do BOTH.
Graphs & Functions 03.03 – Shapes of graphs: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Linear: a straight line (y = 2x + 1). | Quadratic: x² is the highest power; a U-shape (∩ if the x² is negative). | Cubic: x³ is the highest power; an S-shape. | Reciprocal: y = 1/x; two curves that never touch the axes. | Exponential: y = 2ˣ; passes through (0, 1) and grows faster and faster. | USE IT WHEN – Recognising or sketching a graph from its equation. | STEPS – 1. Find the highest power of x. | 2. Power 1: straight line. Power 2: U or ∩. Power 3: S-shape. | 3. x on the bottom of a fraction: reciprocal. x as a power: exponential. | 4. Put x = 0 to find where it crosses the y-axis.
Graphs & Functions 03.03 – Worked example: Name the shape of y = x³ − 2, y = 3/x and y = 5 − x².	y = x³ − 2: power 3, an S-shaped cubic crossing the y-axis at −2 | y = 3/x: x on the bottom, a reciprocal graph | y = 5 − x²: −x², an ∩-shaped quadratic with maximum (0, 5) | Answer: Cubic, reciprocal, ∩-shaped quadratic
Graphs & Functions 03.03 – Shapes of graphs: what is the common mistake?	Thinking −x² gives a U-shape. If the x² term is negative, the curve is upside down (∩).
Graphs & Functions 03.04 – Quadratic graphs: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Roots: where the curve crosses the x-axis (y = 0). y-intercept: where it crosses the y-axis (x = 0). | Turning point: the lowest point of a U-shaped curve (or the highest of an ∩ shape). The curve is symmetrical, so the turning point is halfway between the roots. | USE IT WHEN – Sketching or describing y = ax² + bx + c: its roots, y-intercept and turning point. | STEPS – 1. Roots: set y = 0, factorise and solve. | 2. y-intercept: set x = 0 (it is the constant c). | 3. Turning point x-coordinate: halfway between the roots. | 4. Substitute that x to find the y-coordinate (or complete the square).
Graphs & Functions 03.04 – Worked example: For y = x² − 6x + 5, find the roots, y-intercept and turning point.	(x − 1)(x − 5) = 0, so x = 1, x = 5 | x = 0: y = 5 | Halfway: x = 3, y = 9 − 18 + 5 = −4 | Answer: Roots 1 and 5, y-int (0, 5), TP (3, −4)
Graphs & Functions 03.04 – Quadratic graphs: what is the common mistake?	Giving the turning point's x only. A point needs both coordinates.
Graphs & Functions 03.05 – Circles and tangents: key words and steps? [Higher]	KEY WORDS – Circle centred at (0, 0): x² + y² = r², where r is the radius. | Tangent: a straight line that touches the circle at one point; it is perpendicular to the radius there. | Negative reciprocal: the perpendicular gradient (flip and change the sign). | USE IT WHEN – Equations of circles centred at the origin, and the tangent at a point on the circle (Higher). | STEPS – 1. Radius: square-root the number on the right of x² + y² = r². | 2. Tangent: find the gradient of the radius from (0, 0) to the point. | 3. Use the negative reciprocal for the tangent's gradient. | 4. Substitute the point into y = mx + c to find c.
Graphs & Functions 03.05 – Worked example: Find the tangent to x² + y² = 20 at the point (2, 4). [Higher]	Radius gradient = 4 ÷ 2 = 2 | Tangent gradient = −½ | 4 = −½ × 2 + c, so 4 = −1 + c and c = 5 | Answer: y = −½x + 5
Graphs & Functions 03.05 – Circles and tangents: what is the common mistake? [Higher]	Using the radius gradient (2) for the tangent. The tangent is at 90° to the radius, so use −½.
Graphs & Functions 03.06 – Composite and inverse functions: key words and steps? [Higher]	KEY WORDS – Function: a rule that turns an input into an output (f(x) = 3x − 1, so an input of 2 gives 5). f(a): use a as the input. | Composite fg(x): do g first, then f. | Inverse f⁻¹(x): the rule that undoes f, taking the output back to the input. | USE IT WHEN – Questions using f(x) notation, such as fg(x) or f⁻¹(x). | STEPS – 1. f(a): substitute a for x. | 2. fg(x): do g FIRST, then put the result into f (work from the inside out). | 3. Inverse: write y = f(x) and rearrange to make x the subject. | 4. Then swap x and y to write f⁻¹(x).
Graphs & Functions 03.06 – Worked example: f(x) = 3x − 1 and g(x) = x². Find fg(2) and f⁻¹(x). [Higher]	g(2) = 4, so fg(2) = f(4) = 11 | y = 3x − 1, so x = (y + 1)/3 | Answer: fg(2) = 11, f⁻¹(x) = (x + 1)/3
Graphs & Functions 03.06 – Composite and inverse functions: what is the common mistake? [Higher]	Doing f first for fg. The function next to x acts first: fg(2) = f(g(2)).
Graphs & Functions 03.07 – Graph transformations: key words and steps? [Higher]	KEY WORDS – Transformation: a change in a graph's position or shape. | Translation: a slide without turning. | Reflection: a mirror image. f(x) + a changes the y-values; f(x + a) changes the x-values and works the opposite way to what you'd expect. | USE IT WHEN – Describing or sketching y = f(x) + a, f(x + a), −f(x) and f(−x) (Higher). | STEPS – 1. Number outside the bracket, f(x) + a: move UP a (down if a is negative). | 2. Number inside the bracket, f(x + a): move LEFT a (right if negative). | 3. −f(x): reflect in the x-axis. | 4. f(−x): reflect in the y-axis.
Graphs & Functions 03.07 – Worked example: y = x² has its turning point at (0, 0). Where is the turning point of y = (x − 3)² + 2? [Higher]	(x − 3): inside the bracket, so move RIGHT 3 | + 2: outside, so move UP 2 | (0, 0) moves to (3, 2) | Answer: (3, 2)
Graphs & Functions 03.07 – Graph transformations: what is the common mistake? [Higher]	Moving the wrong way for f(x + a): f(x + 3) moves LEFT 3, not right.
Graphs & Functions 03.08 – Distance–time and velocity–time graphs: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Distance–time graph: distance up the side, time along the bottom. Its gradient = speed; a flat line = not moving. | Velocity–time graph: velocity up the side. Its gradient = acceleration (negative = slowing down); the area under it = distance travelled. | USE IT WHEN – Reading speed, acceleration or distance from a motion graph. | STEPS – 1. Check which graph it is: distance–time or velocity–time. | 2. Gradient = change in y ÷ change in x. | 3. Distance–time: gradient = speed. Velocity–time: gradient = acceleration. | 4. Velocity–time: split the area into rectangles and triangles to find the distance.
Graphs & Functions 03.08 – Worked example: A car speeds up from 0 to 12 m/s in 4 s, then travels at 12 m/s for 6 s. Find its acceleration and the total distance.	Acceleration = 12 ÷ 4 = 3 m/s² | Triangle area = ½ × 4 × 12 = 24 m | Rectangle area = 6 × 12 = 72 m | Total = 24 + 72 = 96 m | Answer: 3 m/s² and 96 m
Graphs & Functions 03.08 – Distance–time and velocity–time graphs: what is the common mistake?	Reading the gradient of a velocity–time graph as speed. There, the gradient is ACCELERATION.
Ratio & Proportion 04.01 – Simplifying ratios: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Ratio: compares quantities in order (boys : girls = 3 : 5). | Simplest form: divide all parts by their HCF until nothing else divides them all. | Fraction of the whole: one part ÷ the total parts (3 : 5 means girls are 5/8). | USE IT WHEN – Simplifying a ratio, or changing a ratio into fractions. | STEPS – 1. Make the units the same first (e.g. both in cm). | 2. Divide every part by the HCF. | 3. For a fraction, add the parts to get the total. | 4. The fraction is that part over the total.
Ratio & Proportion 04.01 – Worked example: Simplify 45 cm : 1.2 m, and give the first amount as a fraction of the total.	Same units: 1.2 m = 120 cm, so 45 : 120 | HCF is 15: 3 : 8 | Total parts = 3 + 8 = 11 | First amount = 3/11 of the total | Answer: 3 : 8, and 3/11
Ratio & Proportion 04.01 – Simplifying ratios: what is the common mistake?	Writing 3/8 as the fraction. The denominator is the TOTAL number of parts, 3 + 8 = 11.
Ratio & Proportion 04.02 – Sharing in a ratio: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Ratio: compares amounts (2 : 5 means for every 2 of one there are 5 of the other). | Parts: each number in the ratio is a number of equal parts, so 2 : 5 is 7 parts in total. | Share in a ratio: split the amount into those parts. | USE IT WHEN – Dividing an amount in a given ratio, or using a known part to find the rest. | STEPS – 1. Add the ratio numbers to find the total number of parts. | 2. Divide the amount by the total parts to find ONE part. | 3. Multiply one part by each ratio number. | 4. Check the shares add up to the original amount.
Ratio & Proportion 04.02 – Worked example: Share £84 in the ratio 2 : 5.	2 + 5 = 7 parts | One part = 84 ÷ 7 = £12 | 2 × 12 = 24, 5 × 12 = 60 | Answer: £24 and £60
Ratio & Proportion 04.02 – Sharing in a ratio: what is the common mistake?	Dividing 84 by 2 and by 5 separately. Always find the value of one part first.
Ratio & Proportion 04.03 – Best buys and the unitary method: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Unit price: the cost of one unit (per item, per 100 g, per litre). | Better value: more for your money, so the LOWER price per unit wins. £1 = 100p and 1 kg = 1000 g. | USE IT WHEN – Deciding which pack or offer is better value. | STEPS – 1. Choose a common amount to compare (per 100 g, per item, or per £1). | 2. Work out the price of that amount for each option. | 3. Use the same units throughout (all pence or all pounds). | 4. State the answer with your figures as evidence.
Ratio & Proportion 04.03 – Worked example: Which is better value: 500 g for £1.80 or 750 g for £2.55?	500 g: 180p ÷ 5 = 36p per 100 g | 750 g: 255p ÷ 7.5 = 34p per 100 g | Answer: 750 g (34p per 100 g)
Ratio & Proportion 04.03 – Best buys and the unitary method: what is the common mistake?	Writing 'the big one' with no working. Without the comparison figures you get no marks.
Ratio & Proportion 04.04 – Direct and inverse proportion: key words and steps? [Higher]	KEY WORDS – Directly proportional (y ∝ x): when one doubles, the other doubles (y = kx). | Inversely proportional: when one doubles, the other halves (y = k/x). k: the constant of proportionality, a fixed number you find from the values you are given. | USE IT WHEN – 'y is proportional to…' or 'inversely proportional to…' questions. | STEPS – 1. Write the formula: direct y = kx (or kx², k√x); inverse y = k/x (or k/x²). | 2. Substitute the given pair of values to find k. | 3. Write the formula with your value of k. | 4. Use it to find the missing value.
Ratio & Proportion 04.04 – Worked example: y is directly proportional to x². When x = 4, y = 48. Find y when x = 5. [Higher]	y = kx² | 48 = k × 16, so k = 3 | y = 3 × 5² = 75 | Answer: y = 75
Ratio & Proportion 04.04 – Direct and inverse proportion: what is the common mistake? [Higher]	Forgetting to square x when finding k (48 = 4k gives k = 12). Use the exact relationship in the question.
Ratio & Proportion 04.05 – Compound measures: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Compound measure: a measure made from two others (speed uses distance and time). | The units show the formula: km/h = km ÷ hours; g/cm³ = g ÷ cm³; N/m² = N ÷ m². Average speed = total distance ÷ total time. | USE IT WHEN – Speed, density, pressure or any 'per' rate. | STEPS – 1. Pick the formula: speed = distance ÷ time, density = mass ÷ volume, pressure = force ÷ area. | 2. Change units so they match (minutes into hours, g into kg). | 3. Rearrange if needed, then substitute. | 4. Give the units of the answer.
Ratio & Proportion 04.05 – Worked example: A car travels 150 km in 1 hour 40 minutes. Find its average speed.	40 min = 40 ÷ 60 = ⅔ hour, so time = 5/3 hours | Speed = 150 ÷ 5/3 = 90 | Answer: 90 km/h
Ratio & Proportion 04.05 – Compound measures: what is the common mistake?	Writing 1 hour 40 minutes as 1.4 hours. There are 60 minutes in an hour, not 100.
Ratio & Proportion 04.06 – Converting units, map scales and currency: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Conversion factor: the number to multiply or divide by (1 m = 100 cm). | Area units are squared: 1 m² = 100 × 100 = 10 000 cm². | Volume units are cubed: 1 m³ = 1 000 000 cm³. | Map scale 1 : 25 000: 1 cm on the map is 25 000 cm in real life. | Exchange rate: £1 = €1.15 means multiply pounds by 1.15 to get euros. | USE IT WHEN – Changing units, using map scales, or converting currencies. | STEPS – 1. Write down the conversion factor. | 2. To a smaller unit: multiply. To a bigger unit: divide. | 3. For areas, square the factor; for volumes, cube it. | 4. Check the answer makes sense.
Ratio & Proportion 04.06 – Worked example: A map has scale 1 : 50 000. A road is 7 cm long on the map. How long is it in km?	7 × 50 000 = 350 000 cm | ÷ 100 = 3500 m | ÷ 1000 = 3.5 km | Answer: 3.5 km
Ratio & Proportion 04.06 – Converting units, map scales and currency: what is the common mistake?	Converting area units with the length factor: 1 m² is 10 000 cm², not 100 cm².
Ratio & Proportion 04.07 – Similar shapes: area and volume: key words and steps? [Higher]	KEY WORDS – Similar shapes: the same shape in different sizes; all angles are equal and all lengths are in the same ratio. | Scale factor (k): how many times bigger (6 cm → 9 cm, so k = 1.5). Area units are squared (cm²) and volume units are cubed (cm³), which is why area uses k² and volume uses k³. | USE IT WHEN – Two shapes are similar, and you need a missing length, area or volume. | STEPS – 1. Find the length scale factor k = new length ÷ old length. | 2. Lengths × k, areas × k², volumes × k³. | 3. If you are given areas or volumes, square-root or cube-root to get back to k. | 4. Check the answer is bigger or smaller as expected.
Ratio & Proportion 04.07 – Worked example: Two similar cylinders have heights 6 cm and 9 cm. The smaller has volume 160 cm³. Find the volume of the larger. [Higher]	k = 9 ÷ 6 = 1.5 | k³ = 1.5³ = 3.375 | 160 × 3.375 = 540 | Answer: 540 cm³
Ratio & Proportion 04.07 – Similar shapes: area and volume: what is the common mistake? [Higher]	Multiplying the volume by 1.5. Volume has three dimensions, so it uses k³.
Geometry & Measures 05.01 – Angle facts and parallel lines: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Straight line: angles add to 180°. | Around a point: 360°. | Triangle: 180°. | Quadrilateral: 360°. | Vertically opposite angles: equal. | Alternate angles (Z shape, between parallel lines): equal. | Corresponding angles (F shape): equal. | Co-interior angles (C shape): add to 180°. | USE IT WHEN – Finding missing angles and giving a reason for each step. | STEPS – 1. Mark the angles you know on the diagram. | 2. Look for straight lines, points, triangles and parallel lines. | 3. Find one angle at a time, writing the rule as your reason. | 4. Check the angles in each triangle add to 180°.
Geometry & Measures 05.01 – Worked example: Two parallel lines are crossed by another line. One angle is 65°. Find its alternate angle and its co-interior angle.	Alternate angles are equal: 65° | Co-interior angles add to 180°: 180 − 65 = 115° | Answer: 65° and 115°
Geometry & Measures 05.01 – Angle facts and parallel lines: what is the common mistake?	Giving answers with no reasons. Angle questions often award marks for stating the rule, e.g. 'alternate angles are equal'.
Geometry & Measures 05.02 – Angles in polygons: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Polygon: a flat shape with straight sides (pentagon 5, hexagon 6, octagon 8, decagon 10). | Regular: all sides and all angles are equal. | Interior angle: the angle inside the shape at a corner. | Exterior angle: the angle between one side and the next side extended outwards. | USE IT WHEN – Interior or exterior angles of a polygon, or finding the number of sides. | STEPS – 1. Exterior angles of ANY polygon add up to 360°. | 2. Interior + exterior = 180° at each vertex. | 3. Sum of interior angles = (n − 2) × 180°. | 4. For a regular polygon: number of sides = 360 ÷ exterior angle.
Geometry & Measures 05.02 – Worked example: Each interior angle of a regular polygon is 156°. How many sides does it have?	Exterior angle = 180 − 156 = 24° | Sides = 360 ÷ 24 = 15 | Answer: 15 sides
Geometry & Measures 05.02 – Angles in polygons: what is the common mistake?	Dividing 360 by the INTERIOR angle. The 360° rule is only true for exterior angles.
Geometry & Measures 05.03 – Properties of quadrilaterals: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Quadrilateral: a 4-sided shape; its angles add to 360°. | Parallel: lines that never meet. | Diagonal: a line joining opposite corners. | Bisect: cut exactly in half. | Perpendicular: at 90°. | USE IT WHEN – Identifying a shape from its properties, or using them to find lengths and angles. | STEPS – 1. Square: 4 equal sides, 4 right angles. Rectangle: opposite sides equal, 4 right angles. | 2. Parallelogram: opposite sides parallel and equal, opposite angles equal. | 3. Rhombus: 4 equal sides, opposite angles equal, diagonals cross at 90°. | 4. Trapezium: one pair of parallel sides. Kite: two pairs of equal sides next to each other, one pair of equal angles.
Geometry & Measures 05.03 – Worked example: A parallelogram has one angle of 70°. Find the other three angles.	Opposite angles are equal: 70° | Neighbouring angles add to 180° (co-interior): 180 − 70 = 110° | Angles: 70°, 110°, 70°, 110° (total 360° ✓) | Answer: 70°, 110° and 110°
Geometry & Measures 05.03 – Properties of quadrilaterals: what is the common mistake?	Mixing up a rhombus and a parallelogram: a rhombus has ALL four sides equal; a parallelogram only has opposite sides equal.
Geometry & Measures 05.04 – Area of 2D shapes: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Area: the space inside a 2D shape, in square units (cm²). | Perpendicular height: measured at 90° to the base, not along a slanted side. | Rectangle: length × width. | Triangle: ½ × base × height. | Parallelogram: base × height. | Trapezium: ½(a + b)h, where a and b are the parallel sides. | USE IT WHEN – Finding the area of rectangles, triangles, parallelograms, trapeziums and compound shapes. | STEPS – 1. Identify the shape (split compound shapes into simple ones). | 2. Choose the formula and find the perpendicular height. | 3. Substitute and calculate. | 4. Add (or subtract) the parts and give square units.
Geometry & Measures 05.04 – Worked example: An L-shape is a 10 cm × 6 cm rectangle with a 4 cm × 3 cm rectangle cut out of one corner. Find its area.	Big rectangle: 10 × 6 = 60 cm² | Cut-out: 4 × 3 = 12 cm² | 60 − 12 = 48 | Answer: 48 cm²
Geometry & Measures 05.04 – Area of 2D shapes: what is the common mistake?	Using the slanted side instead of the perpendicular height for triangles and parallelograms.
Geometry & Measures 05.05 – Circles, arcs and sectors: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Radius (r): from the centre to the edge. | Diameter: edge to edge through the centre (= 2r). | Circumference: the distance round the circle (= 2πr). | Arc: part of the circumference. | Sector: a 'pizza slice' of the circle. π ≈ 3.142. | USE IT WHEN – Arc length, sector area or the perimeter of a sector. | STEPS – 1. Write the fraction of the circle: angle ÷ 360. | 2. Arc length = fraction × 2πr. | 3. Sector area = fraction × πr². | 4. Perimeter of a sector = arc length + 2 radii.
Geometry & Measures 05.05 – Worked example: Find the area and perimeter of a sector with radius 9 cm and angle 80°.	Fraction = 80/360 = 2/9 | Area = 2/9 × π × 81 = 18π | Arc = 2/9 × 2π × 9 = 4π | Perimeter = 4π + 9 + 9 | Answer: Area 56.5 cm², perimeter 30.6 cm
Geometry & Measures 05.05 – Circles, arcs and sectors: what is the common mistake?	Giving just the arc length as the perimeter. The two straight edges (radii) are part of the perimeter.
Geometry & Measures 05.06 – Volume and surface area: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Prism: a 3D shape with the same cross-section all the way through (a cylinder is a circular prism). | Cross-section: the shape you see if you slice straight through. | Volume: the space inside (cm³). | Surface area: the total area of all the faces (cm²). | USE IT WHEN – Prisms and cylinders: volume, or the area of all the faces. | STEPS – 1. Volume of a prism = area of the cross-section × length. | 2. Cylinder volume = πr²h. | 3. Cylinder surface area = 2 circles + curved surface = 2πr² + 2πrh. | 4. Check you used the RADIUS, and give the units (cm³ or cm²).
Geometry & Measures 05.06 – Worked example: A cylinder has radius 4 cm and height 10 cm. Find its volume and total surface area.	V = π × 4² × 10 = 160π | SA = 2π × 4² + 2π × 4 × 10 | = 32π + 80π = 112π | Answer: 502.7 cm³ and 351.9 cm²
Geometry & Measures 05.06 – Volume and surface area: what is the common mistake?	Using the diameter instead of the radius. Halve it first if you are given the diameter.
Geometry & Measures 05.07 – Transformations: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Translation: a slide, given by a column vector ((3, −2) means 3 right, 2 down). | Reflection: a mirror image in a line (e.g. y = x). | Rotation: a turn, given by angle, direction and centre. | Enlargement: a resize, given by scale factor and centre. Scale factor ½ halves the size; a negative scale factor puts the image on the other side of the centre, upside down. | USE IT WHEN – Carrying out or fully describing a transformation. | STEPS – 1. Name the transformation. | 2. Translation: give the column vector. Reflection: give the mirror line's equation. | 3. Rotation: angle, direction (clockwise or anticlockwise) and centre. | 4. Enlargement: scale factor (image length ÷ object length) and centre.
Geometry & Measures 05.07 – Worked example: Shape A has a side of 2 cm. Its image B has the matching side 6 cm, and lines through matching corners meet at (1, 1). Describe the transformation.	The size changes, so it is an enlargement | Scale factor = 6 ÷ 2 = 3 | Centre = where the lines through matching corners meet = (1, 1) | Answer: Enlargement, scale factor 3, centre (1, 1)
Geometry & Measures 05.07 – Transformations: what is the common mistake?	Leaving out details: 'a rotation' on its own scores nothing. Always give the angle, direction AND centre.
Geometry & Measures 05.08 – Congruent triangles: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Congruent: exactly the same shape and size (it may be rotated or reflected). | Similar: the same shape but a different size. | Congruent triangle conditions: SSS (3 sides), SAS (2 sides and the angle between them), ASA or AAS (2 angles and a side), RHS (right angle, hypotenuse and a side). | USE IT WHEN – Proving two triangles are congruent. | STEPS – 1. Find matching pairs of sides and angles, giving a reason for each. | 2. Check which condition they fit: SSS, SAS, ASA/AAS or RHS. | 3. For SAS, the angle must be BETWEEN the two sides. | 4. State the condition in your conclusion.
Geometry & Measures 05.08 – Worked example: Triangles ABC and DEF have AB = DE = 5 cm, angle B = angle E = 40° and BC = EF = 7 cm. Are they congruent?	Two pairs of equal sides: AB = DE and BC = EF | The equal angles B and E are between those sides | So the condition is SAS | Answer: Yes, congruent (SAS)
Geometry & Measures 05.08 – Congruent triangles: what is the common mistake?	Using AAA (three equal angles). That only proves the triangles are SIMILAR, not congruent.
Geometry & Measures 05.09 – Constructions and loci: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Locus (plural loci): all the points that follow a rule. | Perpendicular bisector: cuts a line in half at 90°; every point on it is the same distance from both ends. | Angle bisector: cuts an angle in half; every point on it is the same distance from both arms. | Equidistant: the same distance. | USE IT WHEN – Constructions with a ruler and compasses, and regions that follow rules. | STEPS – 1. Fixed distance from a point: draw a circle. | 2. Equidistant from two points: construct the perpendicular bisector. | 3. Equidistant from two lines: construct the angle bisector. | 4. Leave the construction arcs showing, then shade the region asked for.
Geometry & Measures 05.09 – Worked example: A goat is tied to a post with a 4 m rope, and must be closer to tree A than tree B. How do you show where it can graze?	Within 4 m of the post: a circle of radius 4 m (shade inside) | Closer to A than B: construct the perpendicular bisector of AB | Shade the part of the circle on A's side of the line | Answer: Inside the circle, on A's side of the perpendicular bisector
Geometry & Measures 05.09 – Constructions and loci: what is the common mistake?	Rubbing out the construction arcs. Examiners need to see them to award the marks.
Geometry & Measures 05.10 – Bearings: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Bearing: a direction given as an angle measured clockwise from North, always with 3 figures (East = 090°, South = 180°, West = 270°). 'The bearing of B from A' means stand at A and face B. | Back bearing: the reverse journey. | USE IT WHEN – Finding or using directions given as bearings. | STEPS – 1. Draw a North line at the point you are measuring FROM. | 2. Measure clockwise from North. | 3. Always write 3 figures (070°, not 70°). | 4. Back bearing: add 180° (or subtract 180° if the bearing is over 180°).
Geometry & Measures 05.10 – Worked example: B is on a bearing of 070° from A. Find the bearing of A from B.	070 + 180 = 250 | Answer: 250°
Geometry & Measures 05.10 – Bearings: what is the common mistake?	Measuring anticlockwise, or from the wrong point. 'From A' means the North line goes at A.
Geometry & Measures 05.11 – Plans and elevations: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Plan: the view from directly above. | Front elevation: the view from the front. | Side elevation: the view from the side. 3D solid: a shape with length, width and height. | USE IT WHEN – Drawing or interpreting the 2D views of a 3D shape. | STEPS – 1. Decide which direction you are looking from. | 2. Draw only what you would see from there, as a flat 2D shape. | 3. Use the real measurements from the solid. | 4. Check each view matches the solid's dimensions.
Geometry & Measures 05.11 – Worked example: A cuboid is 4 cm long, 2 cm wide and 3 cm tall. Describe its plan and front elevation.	Plan (from above): length × width | = a 4 cm × 2 cm rectangle | Front elevation: length × height = a 4 cm × 3 cm rectangle | Answer: Plan: 4 × 2 rectangle. Front elevation: 4 × 3 rectangle
Geometry & Measures 05.11 – Plans and elevations: what is the common mistake?	Drawing a 3D view instead of a flat one. Plans and elevations are always flat 2D shapes.
Geometry & Measures 05.12 – Circle theorems: key words and steps? [Higher]	KEY WORDS – Chord: a straight line joining two points on a circle. | Tangent: a line touching the circle at one point. | Cyclic quadrilateral: a 4-sided shape with every corner on the circle. | Segment: the region between a chord and the circle. | Circumference: the edge of the circle. | USE IT WHEN – Finding angles in circles, giving the theorem as your reason (Higher). | STEPS – 1. The angle at the centre is twice the angle at the circumference. The angle in a semicircle is 90°. | 2. Angles in the same segment are equal. Opposite angles in a cyclic quadrilateral add to 180°. | 3. A tangent meets a radius at 90°. Two tangents from one point are equal in length. | 4. Alternate segment theorem: the angle between a tangent and a chord equals the angle in the alternate segment.
Geometry & Measures 05.12 – Worked example: An angle at the circumference is 35°. Find the angle at the centre standing on the same arc. [Higher]	Angle at the centre = 2 × angle at the circumference | 2 × 35 = 70° | Answer: 70°
Geometry & Measures 05.12 – Circle theorems: what is the common mistake? [Higher]	Halving instead of doubling. The angle at the CENTRE is the bigger one.
Geometry & Measures 05.13 – Vectors: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Vector: has both size and direction. | Column vector (3, −2): 3 right and 2 down (top number = across, bottom number = up or down; negative = left or down). a in bold is a vector; −a is the same size in the opposite direction. | USE IT WHEN – Column vectors, or writing a path between points in terms of a and b. | STEPS – 1. Multiply a vector by a number: multiply both parts. | 2. Add or subtract: top with top, bottom with bottom. | 3. Path between points: go along known vectors (going backwards along a vector means minus). | 4. Parallel vectors are multiples of each other.
Geometry & Measures 05.13 – Worked example: a = (3, −2) and b = (−1, 4). Find 2a − b.	2a = (6, −4) | 2a − b = (6 − (−1), −4 − 4) | Answer: (7, −8)
Geometry & Measures 05.13 – Vectors: what is the common mistake?	Sign errors when subtracting a negative: 6 − (−1) = 7, not 5.
Geometry & Measures 05.14 – Metric conversions: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Length: 1 km = 1000 m, 1 m = 100 cm, 1 cm = 10 mm. | Mass: 1 kg = 1000 g, 1 tonne = 1000 kg. | Capacity: 1 litre = 1000 ml, and 1 ml = 1 cm³. | Prefixes: kilo means thousand, centi means hundredth, milli means thousandth. | USE IT WHEN – Changing between metric units of length, mass and capacity. | STEPS – 1. Write down the conversion fact. | 2. Bigger unit to smaller unit: multiply (there will be more of them). | 3. Smaller unit to bigger unit: divide. | 4. Check the answer looks sensible.
Geometry & Measures 05.14 – Worked example: Convert 3.2 km to cm, and 450 g to kg.	3.2 km × 1000 = 3200 m | 3200 m × 100 = 320 000 cm | 450 g ÷ 1000 = 0.45 kg | Answer: 320 000 cm and 0.45 kg
Geometry & Measures 05.14 – Metric conversions: what is the common mistake?	Dividing when you should multiply. Going to a smaller unit, the number must get BIGGER.
Pythagoras & Trigonometry 06.01 – Pythagoras' theorem: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Right-angled triangle: has a 90° angle, marked with a small square. | Hypotenuse: the longest side, opposite the right angle. | Pythagoras' theorem: a² + b² = c², where c is the hypotenuse. | USE IT WHEN – A right-angled triangle where you know two sides and want the third. | STEPS – 1. Label the hypotenuse c (the longest side, opposite the right angle). | 2. Finding the hypotenuse: square, ADD, square-root. | 3. Finding a shorter side: square, SUBTRACT, square-root. | 4. Check the hypotenuse is the longest side.
Pythagoras & Trigonometry 06.01 – Worked example: A 5 m ladder leans against a wall with its foot 1.4 m from the wall. How high up the wall does it reach?	h² = 5² − 1.4² | h² = 25 − 1.96 = 23.04 | h = √23.04 = 4.8 | Answer: 4.8 m
Pythagoras & Trigonometry 06.01 – Pythagoras' theorem: what is the common mistake?	Adding when you want a shorter side (√(25 + 1.96) = 5.19 m is longer than the ladder!).
Pythagoras & Trigonometry 06.02 – SOHCAHTOA: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Opposite (O): the side across from the angle. | Adjacent (A): the side next to the angle that is not the hypotenuse. | Hypotenuse (H): the longest side, opposite the right angle. sin = O ÷ H, cos = A ÷ H, tan = O ÷ A. Use sin⁻¹ (shift + sin) to find an angle. | USE IT WHEN – A right-angled triangle with an angle involved. | STEPS – 1. Label the sides from the angle: Opposite, Adjacent, Hypotenuse. | 2. Pick the ratio that uses the two sides involved (SOH, CAH or TOA). | 3. Finding a side: write the equation and rearrange. | 4. Finding an angle: use the inverse (sin⁻¹, cos⁻¹, tan⁻¹).
Pythagoras & Trigonometry 06.02 – Worked example: A right-angled triangle has hypotenuse 12 cm and an angle of 40°. Find the side x opposite the angle.	O and H, so use SOH | sin 40 = x ÷ 12 | x = 12 × sin 40 = 7.713… | Answer: 7.71 cm
Pythagoras & Trigonometry 06.02 – SOHCAHTOA: what is the common mistake?	Calculator in radians. Check it shows D (degrees): sin 30 should be 0.5.
Pythagoras & Trigonometry 06.03 – Exact trig values: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Exact value: written without rounding (√3/2, not 0.866). sin 30° = ½ and cos 60° = ½. sin 45° = cos 45° = √2/2, and tan 45° = 1. sin 60° = cos 30° = √3/2. tan 30° = √3/3 and tan 60° = √3. sin 0° = 0, cos 0° = 1, sin 90° = 1, cos 90° = 0. | USE IT WHEN – Non-calculator trigonometry questions that need exact answers. | STEPS – 1. Learn the values for 0°, 30°, 45°, 60° and 90°. | 2. Choose SOH, CAH or TOA as normal. | 3. Use the exact value instead of a calculator. | 4. Simplify, leaving any roots in the answer.
Pythagoras & Trigonometry 06.03 – Worked example: A right-angled triangle has hypotenuse 8 cm and an angle of 30°. Find the side opposite the angle without a calculator.	SOH: sin 30° = x ÷ 8 | sin 30° = ½ | x = 8 × ½ = 4 | Answer: 4 cm
Pythagoras & Trigonometry 06.03 – Exact trig values: what is the common mistake?	Mixing up sin 30° and cos 30°: sin 30° = ½ but cos 30° = √3/2.
Pythagoras & Trigonometry 06.04 – Sine and cosine rules: key words and steps? [Higher]	KEY WORDS – Non-right-angled triangle: has no 90° angle, so SOHCAHTOA does not work. | Labelling: capital letters (A, B, C) are the angles; small letters (a, b, c) are the sides opposite them. | Included angle: the angle between two given sides. | USE IT WHEN – A triangle WITHOUT a right angle. | STEPS – 1. Two sides and the angle BETWEEN them, or all three sides: use the cosine rule, a² = b² + c² − 2bc cos A. | 2. A side and its opposite angle as a pair: use the sine rule, a/sin A = b/sin B. | 3. Label so that side a is opposite angle A. | 4. Substitute carefully, then square-root (cosine rule) or rearrange (sine rule).
Pythagoras & Trigonometry 06.04 – Worked example: In triangle ABC, b = 7 cm, c = 9 cm and angle A = 50°. Find a. [Higher]	a² = 7² + 9² − 2 × 7 × 9 × cos 50 | a² = 130 − 80.99… = 49.009… | a = 7.0006… | Answer: 7.00 cm
Pythagoras & Trigonometry 06.04 – Sine and cosine rules: what is the common mistake? [Higher]	Working out (130 − 126) × cos 50. Do the multiplication 126 × cos 50 first, then subtract (BIDMAS).
Pythagoras & Trigonometry 06.05 – Area of a triangle: ½ab sin C: key words and steps? [Higher]	KEY WORDS – Included angle: the angle BETWEEN the two sides you are using. sin C: the sine of that angle, from your calculator in degrees mode. Area is measured in square units (cm²). | USE IT WHEN – The area of a triangle where you know two sides and the angle between them. | STEPS – 1. Check that the angle is BETWEEN the two sides you know. | 2. Area = ½ × a × b × sin C. | 3. Substitute and calculate in one go on the calculator. | 4. Give square units.
Pythagoras & Trigonometry 06.05 – Worked example: Find the area of a triangle with sides 8 cm and 11 cm and an angle of 30° between them. [Higher]	Area = ½ × 8 × 11 × sin 30 | = 44 × 0.5 | Answer: 22 cm²
Pythagoras & Trigonometry 06.05 – Area of a triangle: ½ab sin C: what is the common mistake? [Higher]	Using an angle that is not between the two given sides. If needed, find the included angle first.
Pythagoras & Trigonometry 06.06 – 3D Pythagoras: key words and steps? [Higher]	KEY WORDS – Space diagonal: a line through the inside of a 3D shape from one corner to the opposite corner. | Cuboid: a box shape with length, width and height. 3D Pythagoras: d² = a² + b² + c², so d = √(a² + b² + c²). | USE IT WHEN – Finding the longest line inside a cuboid, or other lengths in 3D shapes (Higher). | STEPS – 1. Find a right-angled triangle inside the 3D shape. | 2. For a cuboid's space diagonal, square all three edges and add. | 3. Square-root the total. | 4. Round only at the end.
Pythagoras & Trigonometry 06.06 – Worked example: A box is 6 cm × 8 cm × 24 cm. Will a 25 cm pencil fit inside? [Higher]	d² = 6² + 8² + 24² | = 36 + 64 + 576 = 676 | d = √676 = 26 cm | 26 cm is longer than 25 cm | Answer: Yes: the space diagonal is 26 cm
Pythagoras & Trigonometry 06.06 – 3D Pythagoras: what is the common mistake? [Higher]	Using only two of the edges. The space diagonal uses all three: length, width AND height.
Pythagoras & Trigonometry 06.07 – Trig graphs: key words and steps? [Higher]	KEY WORDS – y = sin x: a wave between −1 and 1, starting at (0, 0) and repeating every 360°. y = cos x: the same wave starting at (0, 1). y = tan x: repeats every 180°, with asymptotes (lines it never touches) at 90°, 270°… Period: how often a graph repeats. | USE IT WHEN – Sketching trig graphs, or finding a second angle with the same sine or cosine (Higher). | STEPS – 1. Mark 0°, 90°, 180°, 270° and 360° on the x-axis. | 2. sin: 0, 1, 0, −1, 0. cos: 1, 0, −1, 0, 1. | 3. Join with a smooth wave (tan: draw asymptotes at 90° and 270°). | 4. Use the symmetry of the graph to find a second angle.
Pythagoras & Trigonometry 06.07 – Worked example: sin 30° = 0.5. Find another angle between 0° and 360° where sin x = 0.5. [Higher]	The sin graph is symmetrical about 90° | Second angle = 180° − 30° = 150° | Answer: 150°
Pythagoras & Trigonometry 06.07 – Trig graphs: what is the common mistake? [Higher]	Drawing the graphs with sharp points. Trig graphs are smooth curves.
Probability 07.01 – Probability basics: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Probability: how likely something is, from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain), as a fraction, decimal or percentage. | Outcome: one possible result. | Event: the outcome or outcomes you are interested in. | P(A): the probability of A. P(not A) = 1 − P(A). | USE IT WHEN – Finding a simple probability, or a missing probability in a table. | STEPS – 1. Count the outcomes you want. | 2. Count the total number of equally likely outcomes. | 3. P = wanted ÷ total; simplify the fraction. | 4. Missing probability: all the probabilities add to 1.
Probability 07.01 – Worked example: A spinner lands on red with probability 0.4, blue 0.25 and green x. Find x and P(not red).	The probabilities add to 1: 0.4 + 0.25 + x = 1 | x = 1 − 0.65 = 0.35 | P(not red) = 1 − 0.4 = 0.6 | Answer: x = 0.35, P(not red) = 0.6
Probability 07.01 – Probability basics: what is the common mistake?	Writing a probability as a ratio (3 : 8) or getting an answer above 1. Probabilities are always between 0 and 1.
Probability 07.02 – Expected outcomes and relative frequency: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Probability: a number from 0 to 1. | Trial: one go (one spin or one roll). | Expected number: how many times you would predict it to happen. | Relative frequency: how often it actually happened, as a fraction of the trials. | Fair: every outcome has the same chance; biased: not fair. | USE IT WHEN – Predicting results, or deciding whether a dice or spinner is fair. | STEPS – 1. Expected number = probability × number of trials. | 2. Relative frequency = times it happened ÷ total trials. | 3. Compare the relative frequency with the theoretical probability. | 4. More trials give a more reliable estimate.
Probability 07.02 – Worked example: A spinner lands on red with probability 0.35. It is spun 200 times. How many reds do you expect?	0.35 × 200 = 70 | Answer: 70
Probability 07.02 – Expected outcomes and relative frequency: what is the common mistake?	Expecting EXACTLY that many. The expected number is a prediction, so the real result will vary.
Probability 07.03 – Listing outcomes and the AND/OR rules: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Sample space: a list or grid of every possible outcome. | Mutually exclusive: can't happen at the same time (rolling a 2 and a 5 on one dice). | Independent: one doesn't affect the other (two coin flips). | OR rule (mutually exclusive): add. | AND rule (independent): multiply. | USE IT WHEN – Probabilities of two events, using a list, a grid or the rules. | STEPS – 1. List every outcome systematically, or draw a sample space grid. | 2. Count the outcomes you want and divide by the total. | 3. 'A or B' (can't both happen): add the probabilities. | 4. 'A and B' (independent): multiply the probabilities.
Probability 07.03 – Worked example: Two fair dice are rolled and the scores added. Find P(total = 7).	Sample space: 6 × 6 = 36 outcomes | Totals of 7: (1,6), (2,5), (3,4), (4,3), (5,2), (6,1) = 6 ways | P = 6/36 | Answer: 1/6
Probability 07.03 – Listing outcomes and the AND/OR rules: what is the common mistake?	Counting only 3 ways (1+6, 2+5, 3+4). (1,6) and (6,1) are different outcomes.
Probability 07.04 – Tree diagrams: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Probability: how likely something is, from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain), as a fraction, decimal or %. | With replacement: the item is put back, so the probabilities stay the same. | Without replacement: it is not put back, so the total goes down by 1. AND means multiply; OR means add. | USE IT WHEN – Two or more events in a row, especially 'without replacement'. | STEPS – 1. Draw a branch for each outcome. Each set of branches adds to 1. | 2. Without replacement: the second set of fractions changes (one fewer in total). | 3. Multiply ALONG the branches for 'A and then B'. | 4. Add the end results for 'or' (all the ways it can happen).
Probability 07.04 – Worked example: A bag has 5 red and 3 blue counters. Two are taken without replacement. Find P(both the same colour).	P(RR) = 5/8 × 4/7 = 20/56 | P(BB) = 3/8 × 2/7 = 6/56 | 20/56 + 6/56 = 26/56 | Answer: 13/28
Probability 07.04 – Tree diagrams: what is the common mistake?	Keeping the second fractions the same (5/8 × 5/8). Without replacement, the counter you took is not put back.
Probability 07.05 – Venn diagrams and conditional probability: key words and steps? [Higher]	KEY WORDS – Venn diagram: overlapping circles that show groups. The overlap means in BOTH groups; outside the circles means in neither. | Conditional probability P(A | B): the probability of A GIVEN that B happened, so you only count the people in group B. | USE IT WHEN – Overlapping groups, or 'given that' questions. | STEPS – 1. Fill in the overlap (both) first. | 2. Subtract to fill in the 'only' sections. | 3. Subtract everything from the total to get 'neither'. | 4. 'Given that' means the denominator is ONLY that group.
Probability 07.05 – Worked example: 40 students: 22 play football, 15 play tennis, 6 play both. Find how many play neither, and P(tennis | football). [Higher]	Football only = 16, tennis only = 9 | Neither = 40 − (16 + 6 + 9) = 9 | P(T | F) = 6/22 | Answer: 9 students; 3/11
Probability 07.05 – Venn diagrams and conditional probability: what is the common mistake? [Higher]	Using 22 + 15 = 37 for the number playing at least one sport. That counts the 6 'both' students twice.
Statistics 08.01 – Types of data: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Qualitative data: words or categories (eye colour). | Quantitative data: numbers. | Discrete data: counted, and can only take certain values (number of pets, shoe size). | Continuous data: measured, and can take any value in a range (height, time). | Primary data: you collect it; secondary data: someone else collected it. | USE IT WHEN – Classifying data, which decides which chart or average to use. | STEPS – 1. Words or numbers? Words are qualitative. | 2. Numbers that are counted, with set values: discrete. | 3. Numbers that are measured and could be any decimal: continuous. | 4. Did you collect it (primary) or not (secondary)?
Statistics 08.01 – Worked example: Classify: (a) goals scored in a match, (b) the time to run 100 m, (c) favourite colour.	(a) Counted numbers: quantitative, discrete | (b) Measured: quantitative, continuous | (c) Words: qualitative | Answer: Discrete, continuous, qualitative
Statistics 08.01 – Types of data: what is the common mistake?	Calling shoe size continuous. Shoe sizes only come in set values (including halves), so they are discrete.
Statistics 08.02 – Averages and range: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Mean: add all the values and divide by how many. | Median: the middle value when in order (with an even number of values, halfway between the middle two). | Mode: the most common value. | Range: biggest − smallest, a measure of spread. | Outlier: a value much bigger or smaller than the rest. | USE IT WHEN – Finding averages and the range, and choosing the best average. | STEPS – 1. Put the values in order. | 2. Median: the middle value, at position (n + 1) ÷ 2. | 3. Mean: total ÷ number of values. Mode: most common. | 4. If there is an outlier, use the median: the mean gets pulled towards it.
Statistics 08.02 – Worked example: Find the mean, median, mode and range of 4, 8, 2, 8, 10, 30. Which average is best?	In order: 2, 4, 8, 8, 10, 30 | Mean = 62 ÷ 6 = 10.33… | Median = (8 + 8) ÷ 2 = 8; mode = 8 | Range = 30 − 2 = 28 | 30 is an outlier, so the median is best | Answer: Mean 10.3, median 8, mode 8, range 28; the median is best
Statistics 08.02 – Averages and range: what is the common mistake?	Finding the median without putting the values in order first.
Statistics 08.03 – Mean from frequency tables: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Mean: add up all the values and divide by how many there are. | Frequency (f): how many times something happens. | Grouped data: values sorted into classes (10 < t ≤ 20). | Midpoint: the middle of a class ((10 + 20) ÷ 2 = 15). Σ means 'add them all up'. The answer is an ESTIMATE because the exact values are unknown. | USE IT WHEN – Estimating the mean when the data is in groups. | STEPS – 1. Find the midpoint of each group. | 2. Multiply each midpoint by its frequency (fx). | 3. Add up the fx column and the frequency column. | 4. Estimated mean = Σfx ÷ Σf.
Statistics 08.03 – Worked example: Times (min): 0–10: 4, 10–20: 9, 20–30: 5, 30–40: 2. Estimate the mean.	Midpoints: 5, 15, 25, 35 | fx: 20 + 135 + 125 + 70 = 350 | Σf = 20, so 350 ÷ 20 | Answer: 17.5 minutes
Statistics 08.03 – Mean from frequency tables: what is the common mistake?	Dividing by the number of groups (4) instead of the total frequency (20).
Statistics 08.04 – Pie charts and frequency polygons: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Pie chart: a circle split into sectors; the angles add to 360°. Angle = frequency × (360 ÷ total). | Frequency polygon: a line graph for grouped data. | Midpoint: the middle of a class ((10 + 20) ÷ 2 = 15). | USE IT WHEN – Drawing or reading pie charts and frequency polygons. | STEPS – 1. Pie chart: find the angle for one item: 360 ÷ total. | 2. Multiply by each frequency to get each angle (check they add to 360°). | 3. Frequency polygon: find the midpoint of each class. | 4. Plot (midpoint, frequency) and join the points with straight lines.
Statistics 08.04 – Worked example: In a survey of 40 people, 14 chose football. What angle is football on a pie chart?	360 ÷ 40 = 9° per person | 14 × 9 = 126° | Answer: 126°
Statistics 08.04 – Pie charts and frequency polygons: what is the common mistake?	Plotting a frequency polygon at the ends of the classes. The points go at the MIDPOINTS.
Statistics 08.05 – Scatter graphs and correlation: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Scatter graph: plots pairs of data as points. | Correlation: a relationship between the two. | Positive: both increase together. | Negative: as one goes up, the other goes down. | Line of best fit: a straight line through the middle of the points. | Interpolation: estimating inside the data range (reliable). | Extrapolation: outside it (unreliable). | USE IT WHEN – Describing a relationship and making estimates from a line of best fit. | STEPS – 1. Describe the correlation: positive, negative or none (strong or weak). | 2. Draw a line of best fit with roughly equal points on each side. | 3. Read estimates from the line: up from the x value, then across to y. | 4. Comment on reliability: inside the data range is reliable; outside is not.
Statistics 08.05 – Worked example: Hours revised and test score show positive correlation, for 1 to 10 hours of revision. Is it sensible to estimate the score for 25 hours?	25 hours is outside the data range (1 to 10 hours) | That would be extrapolation | The pattern may not continue | Answer: No: it is extrapolation, so it is unreliable
Statistics 08.05 – Scatter graphs and correlation: what is the common mistake?	Saying correlation proves cause. Ice cream sales and sunburn both rise in summer, but one doesn't cause the other.
Statistics 08.06 – Sampling: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Population: the whole group you want to know about. | Sample: a smaller group chosen from it. | Random sample: everyone has an equal chance of being chosen. | Biased: not representative (e.g. only asking your friends). | Stratified sample: each group is represented in proportion to its size. | USE IT WHEN – Choosing a fair sample, or working out a stratified sample. | STEPS – 1. Use a random method (number everyone and use a random number generator). | 2. Make the sample large enough to be reliable. | 3. Stratified: group size ÷ population × sample size. | 4. Round to whole numbers and check the total matches.
Statistics 08.06 – Worked example: A club has 120 juniors and 80 seniors. A stratified sample of 30 is taken. How many juniors?	Population = 120 + 80 = 200 | Juniors: 120 ÷ 200 × 30 = 18 | Seniors: 80 ÷ 200 × 30 = 12 (total 30 ✓) | Answer: 18 juniors
Statistics 08.06 – Sampling: what is the common mistake?	Taking the same number from each group (15 and 15). Stratified sampling is in PROPORTION to the group sizes.
Statistics 08.07 – Cumulative frequency and box plots: key words and steps? [Higher]	KEY WORDS – Cumulative frequency: a running total of the frequencies, plotted at the END (upper bound) of each class. | Median: the middle value. | Lower quartile (LQ): ¼ of the way through the data; upper quartile (UQ): ¾ of the way. Interquartile range (IQR) = UQ − LQ, the spread of the middle half. | Box plot: shows the minimum, LQ, median, UQ and maximum. | USE IT WHEN – Finding the median and IQR from a cumulative frequency graph, and drawing box plots (Higher). | STEPS – 1. Add up the frequencies to get the running totals. | 2. Plot each at the upper bound of its class and join with a smooth curve. | 3. For a total of n: read across at n/2 (median), n/4 (LQ) and 3n/4 (UQ). | 4. IQR = UQ − LQ. Box plot: draw the 5 values on a scale.
Statistics 08.07 – Worked example: A cumulative frequency graph shows 80 students' times. Reading across at 20, 40 and 60 gives 31, 42 and 50 minutes. Find the median and IQR. [Higher]	n = 80, so the median is at 40: 42 minutes | LQ at 20: 31 minutes. UQ at 60: 50 minutes | IQR = 50 − 31 = 19 minutes | Answer: Median 42 minutes, IQR 19 minutes
Statistics 08.07 – Cumulative frequency and box plots: what is the common mistake? [Higher]	Plotting cumulative frequency at the midpoints. It goes at the UPPER bound of each class.
Statistics 08.08 – Histograms: key words and steps? [Higher]	KEY WORDS – Histogram: a bar chart for grouped data where the classes can have different widths. | Class width: upper bound − lower bound (20 < h ≤ 35 is 15 wide). Frequency density = frequency ÷ class width. The AREA of each bar shows the frequency. | USE IT WHEN – Drawing or reading a histogram with unequal class widths. | STEPS – 1. Frequency density = frequency ÷ class width. | 2. Plot frequency density on the vertical axis. | 3. To read a frequency: frequency = frequency density × class width (the area of the bar). | 4. Check your class widths carefully.
Statistics 08.08 – Worked example: A class 20 < h ≤ 35 has frequency 12. Find its frequency density. [Higher]	Class width = 35 − 20 = 15 | 12 ÷ 15 = 0.8 | Answer: 0.8
Statistics 08.08 – Histograms: what is the common mistake? [Higher]	Plotting the frequency as the bar height. In a histogram the AREA shows the frequency.
Statistics 08.09 – Capture–recapture: key words and steps? [Higher]	KEY WORDS – Population: the total number of animals (what you are estimating). | Sample: the animals you catch. | Marked (tagged): animals from the first catch. | Proportion: a fraction of the whole (8 out of 40 is 1/5). | USE IT WHEN – Estimating the size of a population of animals. | STEPS – 1. First sample: mark M animals and release them. | 2. Second sample: catch C animals and count how many are marked (R). | 3. Assume the proportions match: M/N = R/C. | 4. So N = M × C ÷ R.
Statistics 08.09 – Worked example: 50 fish are tagged and released. Later, 40 are caught and 8 of them are tagged. Estimate the population. [Higher]	50/N = 8/40 | N = 50 × 40 ÷ 8 | Answer: 250 fish
Statistics 08.09 – Capture–recapture: what is the common mistake? [Higher]	Forgetting the assumptions: the population doesn't change, and marked animals mix in fully.
Statistics 08.10 – Comparing distributions: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Average: a typical value (median = the middle value in order; mean = total ÷ how many). | Spread: how varied the data is (range = biggest − smallest; IQR = upper quartile − lower quartile, the spread of the middle half). A smaller spread means more consistent. | In context: say what it means for the situation. | USE IT WHEN – 'Compare' questions about two sets of data. | STEPS – 1. Compare an AVERAGE (median or mean): which is higher? | 2. Compare a SPREAD (range or IQR): which is more consistent? | 3. Use the numbers in your answer. | 4. Write each comparison in the context of the question.
Statistics 08.10 – Worked example: Class A: median 62, IQR 15. Class B: median 55, IQR 22. Compare the results.	62 > 55: Class A scored higher on average | 15 < 22: Class A's marks were more consistent | Answer: Two comparisons, both in context
Statistics 08.10 – Comparing distributions: what is the common mistake?	'Class A's median is higher' with no context. Say what it MEANS: Class A did better on average.
Exam Skills 09.01 – Exam command words: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Calculate / work out: give a number, showing working. | Show that: the answer is given, so every step must be written. | Estimate: round to 1 significant figure, then calculate. | In terms of π: leave π as a symbol (e.g. 36π). | Exact: no rounding (keep fractions, surds and π). | USE IT WHEN – Understanding exactly what a question wants, so you don't lose easy marks. | STEPS – 1. Underline the command word. | 2. Show that: write every step and finish with the given answer. | 3. Estimate: round to 1 s.f. first, even if a calculator is allowed. | 4. In terms of π, or exact: don't change it into a decimal.
Exam Skills 09.01 – Worked example: Show that the area of a circle with radius 5 cm is 78.5 cm² to 3 s.f.	Area = πr² | = π × 5² = 25π | = 78.539… cm² | = 78.5 cm² to 3 s.f. ✓ | Answer: Every step shown, ending with 78.5 cm²
Exam Skills 09.01 – Exam command words: what is the common mistake?	Writing only the final answer on a 'show that' question. The answer is given, so all the marks are for the working.
Exam Skills 09.02 – Accuracy and timing: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Premature rounding: rounding in the middle of a calculation, which can make the final answer wrong. | Marks: the number in brackets next to a question shows how much working is expected. | USE IT WHEN – Giving answers to the right accuracy and pacing yourself in the exam. | STEPS – 1. Keep full calculator values (use ANS or memory) until the end. | 2. Round only the final answer, to the accuracy asked (3 s.f. if it doesn't say). | 3. Money: always 2 decimal places (£3.50, not £3.5). | 4. Timing: about 1 minute per mark; if stuck, move on and come back.
Exam Skills 09.02 – Worked example: A paper has 80 marks and lasts 90 minutes. Roughly how long should a 5-mark question take?	90 ÷ 80 = 1.125 minutes per mark | 5 × 1.125 = 5.625 minutes | Answer: About 5 to 6 minutes
Exam Skills 09.02 – Accuracy and timing: what is the common mistake?	Rounding during the working: using 1.4 for √2 instead of 1.414… can change the final answer.
Exam Skills 09.03 – Checking and calculator skills: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Inverse operation: the opposite operation (× undoes ÷), used to check an answer. | Substitute: put your answer back into the equation. | ANS: the calculator key that recalls the last answer. | Degrees mode (D): needed for trigonometry. | USE IT WHEN – Checking answers and using a calculator correctly. | STEPS – 1. Does the answer make sense (a person isn't 20 m tall)? | 2. Check by doing the inverse operation, or substitute your answer back in. | 3. Use brackets for negatives and fractions: (−3)², (2 + 5) ÷ 3. | 4. Check the calculator shows D before doing trigonometry.
Exam Skills 09.03 – Worked example: You solve 4x − 7 = 17 and get x = 6. Check it.	Substitute x = 6: 4 × 6 − 7 | = 24 − 7 = 17 ✓ | Answer: x = 6 is correct
Exam Skills 09.03 – Checking and calculator skills: what is the common mistake?	Typing −3² into a calculator, which gives −9. You need brackets: (−3)² = 9.
Exam Skills 09.04 – Formulas to learn: key words and steps?	KEY WORDS – Formula sheet: GCSE Maths gives very few formulas; the cone, sphere and pyramid formulas are usually printed in the question. | Must learn: area formulas, circle formulas, Pythagoras, SOHCAHTOA, the sine and cosine rules, the quadratic formula, compound interest and the volume of a prism. | USE IT WHEN – Knowing which formulas to memorise before the exam. | STEPS – 1. List the formulas you need to learn on one sheet. | 2. Test yourself: cover them and write them out from memory. | 3. Practise using each one in a question. | 4. In the exam, write the formula first, then substitute.
Exam Skills 09.04 – Worked example: Which of these must you memorise: the area of a trapezium, the volume of a sphere, the quadratic formula?	Area of a trapezium, ½(a + b)h: memorise | Volume of a sphere, 4/3 πr³: usually given in the question | Quadratic formula: memorise | Answer: The trapezium area and the quadratic formula
Exam Skills 09.04 – Formulas to learn: what is the common mistake?	Expecting a full formula sheet. Most formulas are NOT given in GCSE Maths, so they must be learned.
