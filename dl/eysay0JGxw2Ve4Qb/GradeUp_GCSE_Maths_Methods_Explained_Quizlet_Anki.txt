Number: Method 01 – Prime factors, HCF and LCM. What are the steps?	Use it when: A question asks for the highest common factor, the lowest common multiple, or when two repeating events next happen together. | 1. Write each number as a product of primes (use a factor tree). | 2. HCF: multiply the primes the numbers SHARE, using the lower power of each. | 3. LCM: multiply EVERY prime that appears, using the higher power of each. | 4. Check: HCF × LCM = the two numbers multiplied together.
Number: Method 01 – Find the HCF and LCM of 60 and 84.	60 = 2² × 3 × 5 | 84 = 2² × 3 × 7 | HCF = 2² × 3 = 12 | LCM = 2² × 3 × 5 × 7 = 420 | Answer: HCF = 12, LCM = 420
Number: Method 01 – Prime factors, HCF and LCM. What is the common mistake?	Multiplying the two numbers (60 × 84 = 5040) gives A common multiple, not the LOWEST one.
Number: Method 02 – Adding and subtracting fractions. What are the steps?	Use it when: Any + or − with fractions or mixed numbers. | 1. Change mixed numbers into improper fractions. | 2. Find a common denominator (the LCM of the denominators). | 3. Change each fraction to that denominator: whatever you do to the bottom, do to the top. | 4. Add or subtract the numerators only, then simplify.
Number: Method 02 – Work out 2⅓ − 1¾	7/3 − 7/4 | Common denominator 12: 28/12 − 21/12 | = 7/12 | Answer: 7/12
Number: Method 02 – Adding and subtracting fractions. What is the common mistake?	Adding the denominators too: 3/4 + 2/5 is NOT 5/9. The denominator only tells you the size of the pieces.
Number: Method 03 – Multiplying and dividing fractions. What are the steps?	Use it when: Any × or ÷ with fractions or mixed numbers. | 1. Change mixed numbers into improper fractions first. | 2. Dividing? Keep the first fraction, Change ÷ to ×, Flip the second (KCF). | 3. Multiply the tops together and the bottoms together. | 4. Simplify, and change back to a mixed number if needed.
Number: Method 03 – Work out 2⅖ ÷ 1⅓	12/5 ÷ 4/3 | = 12/5 × 3/4 | = 36/20 = 9/5 | Answer: 1⅘
Number: Method 03 – Multiplying and dividing fractions. What is the common mistake?	Flipping the FIRST fraction, or multiplying mixed numbers without making them improper (2 × 1 and ⅖ × ⅓ separately is wrong).
Number: Method 04 – Percentage multipliers and percentage change. What are the steps?	Use it when: Increasing or decreasing by a percentage, especially more than once, or finding a percentage change. | 1. Increase by r%: multiply by (1 + r/100). A 15% increase is × 1.15. | 2. Decrease by r%: multiply by (1 − r/100). A 15% decrease is × 0.85. | 3. Several changes: multiply by each multiplier in turn. | 4. Percentage change = change ÷ ORIGINAL × 100.
Number: Method 04 – A £480 TV is reduced by 15%, then the new price rises by 10%. Find the final price.	480 × 0.85 = 408 | 408 × 1.10 = 448.80 | Answer: £448.80
Number: Method 04 – Percentage multipliers and percentage change. What is the common mistake?	Combining the changes: −15% then +10% is NOT −5% (that would give £456). The 10% is of the NEW price.
Number: Method 05 – Reverse percentages. What are the steps?	Use it when: You are given the amount AFTER a percentage change and asked for the original. | 1. Write the change as a multiplier (e.g. a 20% increase is 1.2). | 2. The original × multiplier = the new amount. | 3. So original = new amount ÷ multiplier. | 4. Check by multiplying back.
Number: Method 05 – After a 20% increase a bike costs £180. What did it cost before?	Multiplier = 1.2 | Original = 180 ÷ 1.2 = 150 | Check: 150 × 1.2 = 180 ✓ | Answer: £150
Number: Method 05 – Reverse percentages. What is the common mistake?	Taking 20% OFF the new price (180 × 0.8 = £144). The 20% was of the original, not of £180.
Number: Method 06 – Compound interest and depreciation. What are the steps?	Use it when: A value grows or shrinks by the same percentage every year (or month). | 1. Find the multiplier: growth 1 + r/100, depreciation 1 − r/100. | 2. Amount = original × multiplier^n, where n is the number of years. | 3. Interest earned = final amount − original amount. | 4. Round money to 2 decimal places only at the end.
Number: Method 06 – £3000 is invested at 2.5% compound interest per year. Find the value after 4 years.	Multiplier = 1.025 | 3000 × 1.025⁴ = 3311.4387… | Answer: £3311.44
Number: Method 06 – Compound interest and depreciation. What is the common mistake?	Using simple interest: 2.5% of £3000 is £75, × 4 = £300 gives £3300. Compound interest is worked out on the NEW amount each year.
Number: Method 07 – Error intervals and bounds. What are the steps?	Use it when: A measurement is rounded, and you need its smallest and largest possible values, or the bounds of a calculation. | 1. Halve the rounding unit (nearest 0.1 → 0.05 either side). | 2. Lower bound = value − half; upper bound = value + half. Write LB ≤ x < UB. | 3. For a calculation, pick the bounds that make the answer biggest (or smallest). | 4. Divide: largest answer = upper ÷ lower; smallest answer = lower ÷ upper.
Number: Method 07 – x = 6.2 (1 d.p.) and y = 3 (nearest whole number). Find the upper bound of x ÷ y.	x: 6.15 ≤ x < 6.25 | y: 2.5 ≤ y < 3.5 | Biggest answer = 6.25 ÷ 2.5 | Answer: 2.5
Number: Method 07 – Error intervals and bounds. What is the common mistake?	Using upper ÷ upper (6.25 ÷ 3.5). To make a division big, you need a big top and a SMALL bottom.
Number: Method 08 – Laws of indices (including negative and fractional). What are the steps? [Higher]	Use it when: Simplifying expressions with powers, or evaluating negative and fractional powers. | 1. Multiply: add the powers. Divide: subtract the powers. Power of a power: multiply. | 2. Negative power: 'one over' (a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ). | 3. Fractional power: the bottom is a root, the top is a power. Do the root first. | 4. Apply the power to the number AND each letter inside a bracket.
Number: Method 08 – Simplify (8x⁶)^(2/3) [Higher]	8^(2/3) = (∛8)² = 2² = 4 | (x⁶)^(2/3) = x^(6 × 2/3) = x⁴ | Answer: 4x⁴
Number: Method 08 – Laws of indices (including negative and fractional). What is the common mistake? [Higher]	Thinking a negative power makes the answer negative: 2⁻³ = 1/8, not −8.
Number: Method 09 – Standard form calculations. What are the steps?	Use it when: Very large or very small numbers, especially multiplying or dividing them. | 1. Multiply (or divide) the number parts. | 2. Add the powers of 10 when multiplying; subtract them when dividing. | 3. If the number part is not between 1 and 10, adjust it and change the power to match. | 4. Check: a bigger number part means a smaller power, and vice versa.
Number: Method 09 – Work out (3 × 10⁴) × (6 × 10⁵)	3 × 6 = 18 and 10⁴ × 10⁵ = 10⁹ | 18 × 10⁹ = 1.8 × 10 × 10⁹ | Answer: 1.8 × 10¹⁰
Number: Method 09 – Standard form calculations. What is the common mistake?	Leaving the answer as 18 × 10⁹. It is not in standard form until the first number is between 1 and 10.
Number: Method 10 – Recurring decimals to fractions. What are the steps? [Higher]	Use it when: Proving or showing that a recurring decimal equals a fraction. | 1. Let x = the decimal and write out several digits. | 2. Multiply by powers of 10 to get two numbers with the SAME recurring part after the point. | 3. Subtract to remove the recurring part. | 4. Solve for x and simplify the fraction.
Number: Method 10 – Write 0.23̇ (0.2333…) as a fraction in its simplest form. [Higher]	10x = 2.333… | 100x = 23.333… | 100x − 10x: 90x = 21 | x = 21/90 | Answer: 7/30
Number: Method 10 – Recurring decimals to fractions. What is the common mistake? [Higher]	Using x and 10x when the recurring part starts later: 10x − x = 2.1 leaves a decimal. Line up the recurring parts first.
Number: Method 11 – Surds. What are the steps? [Higher]	Use it when: Simplifying square roots, or removing a root from the denominator (rationalising). | 1. Split the number into a square number × something: √50 = √25 × √2 = 5√2. | 2. Only add or subtract surds with the same root (like terms). | 3. Rationalise: multiply top and bottom by the root in the denominator. | 4. Simplify the final fraction.
Number: Method 11 – (a) Simplify √50 + √18   (b) Write 12/√3 in the form a√3 [Higher]	(a) 5√2 + 3√2 = 8√2 | (b) 12/√3 × √3/√3 = 12√3/3 | Answer: (a) 8√2   (b) 4√3
Number: Method 11 – Surds. What is the common mistake? [Higher]	Adding inside the roots: √50 + √18 is NOT √68. Simplify each surd first.
Algebra: Method 12 – Simplifying algebraic fractions. What are the steps? [Higher]	Use it when: An algebraic fraction can be simplified, usually with quadratics on the top and bottom. | 1. Factorise the numerator fully. | 2. Factorise the denominator fully. | 3. Cancel any bracket that appears on BOTH top and bottom. | 4. Leave the answer factorised.
Algebra: Method 12 – Simplify (x² − 9) / (x² + x − 6) [Higher]	Top: (x + 3)(x − 3) | Bottom: (x + 3)(x − 2) | Cancel (x + 3) | Answer: (x − 3)/(x − 2)
Algebra: Method 12 – Simplifying algebraic fractions. What is the common mistake? [Higher]	Cancelling single terms: crossing out the x² terms is wrong. You can only cancel whole factors (brackets).
Algebra: Method 13 – Algebraic proof. What are the steps? [Higher]	Use it when: 'Prove that…' or 'Show that…' questions about odd, even or consecutive numbers. | 1. Write the numbers algebraically: n, n + 1 (consecutive); 2n (even); 2n + 1 (odd). | 2. Write the expression the question describes and simplify it. | 3. Factorise to show the property (e.g. 3( … ) is a multiple of 3). | 4. Finish with a sentence that states what you have proved.
Algebra: Method 13 – Prove that the sum of three consecutive integers is a multiple of 3. [Higher]	n + (n + 1) + (n + 2) | = 3n + 3 | = 3(n + 1) | Answer: 3(n + 1) is a multiple of 3
Algebra: Method 13 – Algebraic proof. What is the common mistake? [Higher]	Testing a few numbers (1 + 2 + 3 = 6 …). Examples never prove a rule is true for ALL numbers.
Algebra: Method 14 – Iteration. What are the steps? [Higher]	Use it when: Using an iterative formula to find an approximate solution to an equation. | 1. Substitute the starting value x₀ into the formula to get x₁. | 2. Put x₁ back in to get x₂, and so on. | 3. Use the ANS button so you keep full accuracy. | 4. Round only at the end, to the accuracy asked for.
Algebra: Method 14 – Use xₙ₊₁ = 2 + 1/xₙ with x₀ = 2 to find x₄ to 4 d.p. [Higher]	x₁ = 2 + 1/2 = 2.5 | x₂ = 2 + 1/2.5 = 2.4 | x₃ = 2.416666… | x₄ = 2.413793… | Answer: x₄ = 2.4138
Algebra: Method 14 – Iteration. What is the common mistake? [Higher]	Rounding each value and retyping it. Small rounding errors build up and can change the last digit.
Algebra: Method 15 – Quadratic inequalities. What are the steps? [Higher]	Use it when: Solving an inequality with an x² term, such as x² − x − 12 < 0. | 1. Rearrange to 0 on one side, then factorise. | 2. Find the roots (where the quadratic equals 0). | 3. Sketch the U-shaped graph through the roots. | 4. < 0 is the part below the axis (between the roots); > 0 is above it (two separate regions).
Algebra: Method 15 – Solve x² − x − 12 < 0 [Higher]	(x − 4)(x + 3) < 0 | Roots: x = 4 and x = −3 | Below the axis between the roots | Answer: −3 < x < 4
Algebra: Method 15 – Quadratic inequalities. What is the common mistake? [Higher]	Writing x < 4 and x < −3, as if it were a linear inequality. Always sketch the graph to see which region you need.
Graphs & Functions: Method 16 – Gradient and the equation of a straight line. What are the steps?	Use it when: Finding a line's equation from two points, or from a point and a gradient. | 1. Gradient m = change in y ÷ change in x (rise over run). | 2. Put m into y = mx + c. | 3. Substitute one point's x and y to find c. | 4. Write the final equation y = mx + c.
Graphs & Functions: Method 16 – Find the equation of the line through (2, 5) and (6, 13).	m = (13 − 5) ÷ (6 − 2) = 8 ÷ 4 = 2 | 5 = 2 × 2 + c, so c = 1 | Answer: y = 2x + 1
Graphs & Functions: Method 16 – Gradient and the equation of a straight line. What is the common mistake?	Dividing the change in x by the change in y. It is always y on top.
Graphs & Functions: Method 17 – Parallel and perpendicular lines. What are the steps? [Higher]	Use it when: Finding the equation of a line parallel or perpendicular to another line. | 1. Parallel lines have the SAME gradient. | 2. Perpendicular gradient: flip the fraction and change the sign (2 becomes −½). The two gradients multiply to −1. | 3. Put the new gradient into y = mx + c. | 4. Substitute the given point to find c.
Graphs & Functions: Method 17 – Find the line perpendicular to y = 3x − 2 that passes through (6, 1). [Higher]	Perpendicular gradient = −1/3 | 1 = −1/3 × 6 + c, so 1 = −2 + c | c = 3 | Answer: y = −⅓x + 3
Graphs & Functions: Method 17 – Parallel and perpendicular lines. What is the common mistake? [Higher]	Only changing the sign (−3) or only flipping (⅓). You must do BOTH.
Graphs & Functions: Method 18 – Key points of a quadratic graph. What are the steps?	Use it when: Sketching or describing y = ax² + bx + c: its roots, y-intercept and turning point. | 1. Roots: set y = 0, factorise and solve. | 2. y-intercept: set x = 0 (it is the constant c). | 3. Turning point x-coordinate: halfway between the roots. | 4. Substitute that x to find the y-coordinate (or complete the square).
Graphs & Functions: Method 18 – For y = x² − 6x + 5, find the roots, y-intercept and turning point.	(x − 1)(x − 5) = 0, so x = 1, x = 5 | x = 0: y = 5 | Halfway: x = 3, y = 9 − 18 + 5 = −4 | Answer: Roots 1 and 5, y-int (0, 5), TP (3, −4)
Graphs & Functions: Method 18 – Key points of a quadratic graph. What is the common mistake?	Giving the turning point's x only. A point needs both coordinates.
Graphs & Functions: Method 19 – Composite and inverse functions. What are the steps? [Higher]	Use it when: Questions using f(x) notation, such as fg(x) or f⁻¹(x). | 1. f(a): substitute a for x. | 2. fg(x): do g FIRST, then put the result into f (work from the inside out). | 3. Inverse: write y = f(x) and rearrange to make x the subject. | 4. Then swap x and y to write f⁻¹(x).
Graphs & Functions: Method 19 – f(x) = 3x − 1 and g(x) = x². Find fg(2) and f⁻¹(x). [Higher]	g(2) = 4, so fg(2) = f(4) = 11 | y = 3x − 1, so x = (y + 1)/3 | Answer: fg(2) = 11, f⁻¹(x) = (x + 1)/3
Graphs & Functions: Method 19 – Composite and inverse functions. What is the common mistake? [Higher]	Doing f first for fg. The function next to x acts first: fg(2) = f(g(2)).
Ratio & Proportion: Method 20 – Sharing in a ratio. What are the steps?	Use it when: Dividing an amount in a given ratio, or using a known part to find the rest. | 1. Add the ratio numbers to find the total number of parts. | 2. Divide the amount by the total parts to find ONE part. | 3. Multiply one part by each ratio number. | 4. Check the shares add up to the original amount.

Ratio & Proportion: Method 21 – Best buys (unit pricing). What are the steps?	Use it when: Deciding which pack or offer is better value. | 1. Choose a common amount to compare (per 100 g, per item, or per £1). | 2. Work out the price of that amount for each option. | 3. Use the same units throughout (all pence or all pounds). | 4. State the answer with your figures as evidence.

Ratio & Proportion: Method 24 – Similar shapes: length, area and volume. What are the steps? [Higher]	Use it when: Two shapes are similar, and you need a missing length, area or volume. | 1. Find the length scale factor k = new length ÷ old length. | 2. Lengths × k, areas × k², volumes × k³. | 3. If you are given areas or volumes, square-root or cube-root to get back to k. | 4. Check the answer is bigger or smaller as expected.

Geometry & Measures: Method 25 – Angles in polygons. What are the steps?	Use it when: Interior or exterior angles of a polygon, or finding the number of sides. | 1. For a regular polygon: number of sides = 360 ÷ exterior angle.

Geometry & Measures: Method 25 – Angles in polygons. What is the common mistake?	Dividing 360 by the INTERIOR angle. The 360° rule is only true for exterior angles.

Probability: Method 35 – Venn diagrams and conditional probability. What are the steps? [Higher]	Use it when: Overlapping groups, or 'given that' questions. | 1. Fill in the overlap (both) first. | 2. Subtract to fill in the 'only' sections. | 3. Subtract everything from the total to get 'neither'. | 4. 'Given that' means the denominator is ONLY that group.
Probability: Method 35 – 40 students: 22 play football, 15 play tennis, 6 play both. Find how many play neither, and P(tennis | football). [Higher]	Football only = 16, tennis only = 9 | Neither = 40 − (16 + 6 + 9) = 9 | P(T | F) = 6/22 | Answer: 9 students; 3/11
Probability: Method 35 – Venn diagrams and conditional probability. What is the common mistake? [Higher]	Using 22 + 15 = 37 for the number playing at least one sport. That counts the 6 'both' students twice.
Probability: Method 36 – Expected outcomes and relative frequency. What are the steps?	Use it when: Predicting results, or deciding whether a dice or spinner is fair. | 1. Expected number = probability × number of trials. | 2. Relative frequency = times it happened ÷ total trials. | 3. Compare the relative frequency with the theoretical probability. | 4. More trials give a more reliable estimate.
Probability: Method 36 – A spinner lands on red with probability 0.35. It is spun 200 times. How many reds do you expect?	0.35 × 200 = 70 | Answer: 70
Probability: Method 36 – Expected outcomes and relative frequency. What is the common mistake?	Expecting EXACTLY that many. The expected number is a prediction, so the real result will vary.
Statistics: Method 37 – Mean from a grouped frequency table. What are the steps?	Use it when: Estimating the mean when the data is in groups. | 1. Find the midpoint of each group. | 2. Multiply each midpoint by its frequency (fx). | 3. Add up the fx column and the frequency column. | 4. Estimated mean = Σfx ÷ Σf.
Statistics: Method 37 – Times (min): 0–10: 4, 10–20: 9, 20–30: 5, 30–40: 2. Estimate the mean.	Midpoints: 5, 15, 25, 35 | fx: 20 + 135 + 125 + 70 = 350 | Σf = 20, so 350 ÷ 20 | Answer: 17.5 minutes
Statistics: Method 37 – Mean from a grouped frequency table. What is the common mistake?	Dividing by the number of groups (4) instead of the total frequency (20).
Statistics: Method 38 – Histograms. What are the steps? [Higher]	Use it when: Drawing or reading a histogram with unequal class widths. | 1. Frequency density = frequency ÷ class width. | 2. Plot frequency density on the vertical axis. | 3. To read a frequency: frequency = frequency density × class width (the area of the bar). | 4. Check your class widths carefully.
Statistics: Method 38 – A class 20 < h ≤ 35 has frequency 12. Find its frequency density. [Higher]	Class width = 35 − 20 = 15 | 12 ÷ 15 = 0.8 | Answer: 0.8
Statistics: Method 38 – Histograms. What is the common mistake? [Higher]	Plotting the frequency as the bar height. In a histogram the AREA shows the frequency.
Statistics: Method 39 – Capture–recapture. What are the steps? [Higher]	Use it when: Estimating the size of a population of animals. | 1. First sample: mark M animals and release them. | 2. Second sample: catch C animals and count how many are marked (R). | 3. Assume the proportions match: M/N = R/C. | 4. So N = M × C ÷ R.
Statistics: Method 39 – 50 fish are tagged and released. Later, 40 are caught and 8 of them are tagged. Estimate the population. [Higher]	50/N = 8/40 | N = 50 × 40 ÷ 8 | Answer: 250 fish
Statistics: Method 39 – Capture–recapture. What is the common mistake? [Higher]	Forgetting the assumptions: the population doesn't change, and marked animals mix in fully.
Statistics: Method 40 – Comparing two distributions. What are the steps?	Use it when: 'Compare' questions about two sets of data. | 1. Compare an AVERAGE (median or mean): which is higher? | 2. Compare a SPREAD (range or IQR): which is more consistent? | 3. Use the numbers in your answer. | 4. Write each comparison in the context of the question.
Statistics: Method 40 – Class A: median 62, IQR 15. Class B: median 55, IQR 22. Compare the results.	62 > 55: Class A scored higher on average | 15 < 22: Class A's marks were more consistent | Answer: Two comparisons, both in context
Statistics: Method 40 – Comparing two distributions. What is the common mistake?	'Class A's median is higher' with no context. Say what it MEANS: Class A did better on average.
