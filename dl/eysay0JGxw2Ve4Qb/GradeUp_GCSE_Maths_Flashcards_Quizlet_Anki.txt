Ratio & Proportion 04.05.1: Speed, density and pressure formulas.	Speed = distance ÷ time | Density = mass ÷ volume | Pressure = force ÷ area | Example: 120 km in 2 hours: speed = 120 ÷ 2 = 60 km/h. 50 N on 0.5 m²: pressure = 50 ÷ 0.5 = 100 N/m².
Ratio & Proportion 04.05.2: A car travels 150 km in 2 hours 30 minutes. Find its average speed.	Method: Speed = distance ÷ time, with time as a decimal of an hour. 30 minutes is 0.5 hours, not 0.3. | 2 h 30 min = 2.5 h | 150 ÷ 2.5 = 60 km/h
Ratio & Proportion 04.05.3: A metal block has mass 400 g and volume 50 cm³. Find its density.	Method: Density = mass ÷ volume. The units come from the units you divide (g ÷ cm³ = g/cm³). | 400 ÷ 50 = 8 g/cm³
Ratio & Proportion 04.05.4: Convert 72 km/h to m/s.	Method: Change km to m (× 1000) and hours to seconds (× 3600), then divide. | 72 000 m ÷ 3600 s = 20 m/s
Ratio & Proportion 04.06.1: Convert 3.5 m² to cm² and 2.4 litres to cm³.	Method: Area units are squared, so square the length conversion (100 × 100). For capacity, 1 litre = 1000 cm³. | 1 m² = 100 × 100 = 10 000 cm² → 35 000 cm² | 1 litre = 1000 cm³ → 2400 cm³
Ratio & Proportion 04.06.2: A map has scale 1 : 25 000. Two places are 4 cm apart. How far is that in real life?	Method: Every 1 cm on the map is 25 000 cm in real life, so multiply. Then convert cm to m (÷100) and m to km (÷1000). | 4 × 25 000 = 100 000 cm = 1 km
Ratio & Proportion 04.06.3: £1 = €1.15. Convert £200 to euros and €92 to pounds.	Method: Pounds to euros: multiply by the rate. Euros to pounds: divide by it (it should give fewer pounds than euros). | £200 × 1.15 = €230 | €92 ÷ 1.15 = £80
Ratio & Proportion 04.07.1: Two similar shapes have length scale factor k. What are the area and volume scale factors? [Higher]	Method: Areas have two dimensions, so the scale factor is squared; volumes have three, so it is cubed. | Area: k². Volume: k³. | Lengths × 2 → area × 4, volume × 8
Ratio & Proportion 04.07.2: Two similar cylinders have volumes 54 cm³ and 16 cm³. Find the length scale factor. [Higher]	Method: Find the volume scale factor by dividing the volumes (new ÷ old), then cube-root it to get the length scale factor. | Volume SF = 16/54 = 8/27 | Length SF = ∛(8/27) = 2/3
Geometry & Measures 05.01.1: Angle facts: straight line, point, triangle, quadrilateral.	Straight line: 180° | Around a point: 360° | Triangle: 180° | Quadrilateral: 360° | Example: Two angles on a straight line are 110° and x: x = 180 − 110 = 70°.
Geometry & Measures 05.01.2: Name the angle rules for parallel lines.	Alternate angles are equal (Z). Corresponding angles are equal (F). Co-interior angles add to 180° (C). Vertically opposite angles are equal. | Example: One angle is 50°: its alternate angle is 50° and its co-interior angle is 180 − 50 = 130°.
Geometry & Measures 05.01.3: An isosceles triangle has a top angle of 40°. Find the base angles.	Method: Angles in a triangle add to 180°. Subtract the top angle, and share what is left equally between the two equal base angles. | (180 − 40) ÷ 2 = 70° each
Geometry & Measures 05.02.1: Sum of interior angles of a polygon with n sides.	(n − 2) × 180° | Hexagon: 4 × 180 = 720°
Geometry & Measures 05.02.2: Find the interior angle of a regular octagon.	Method: Exterior angles of any polygon add to 360°, so for a regular one divide 360 by the sides. Interior + exterior = 180°. | Exterior = 360 ÷ 8 = 45° | Interior = 180 − 45 = 135°
Geometry & Measures 05.02.3: A regular polygon has exterior angles of 24°. How many sides?	Method: All exterior angles add to 360°, so divide 360 by one exterior angle to count the sides. | 360 ÷ 24 = 15 sides
Geometry & Measures 05.03.1: Properties of a parallelogram, rhombus and kite.	Parallelogram: opposite sides parallel and equal, opposite angles equal. Rhombus: all sides equal, diagonals cross at 90°. Kite: two pairs of adjacent equal sides, one line of symmetry. | Example: A rhombus has an angle of 60°: the opposite angle is 60°, and the other two are (360 − 120) ÷ 2 = 120° each.
Geometry & Measures 05.04.1: Area of a rectangle, triangle, parallelogram and trapezium.	Rectangle: l × w | Triangle: ½ × b × h | Parallelogram: b × h | Trapezium: ½(a + b)h | Example: A triangle with base 8 cm and height 5 cm: ½ × 8 × 5 = 20 cm².
Geometry & Measures 05.04.2: A trapezium has parallel sides 5 cm and 9 cm and height 4 cm. Find its area.	Method: Add the parallel sides, halve, then multiply by the perpendicular height. | ½ × (5 + 9) × 4 = 28 cm²
Geometry & Measures 05.05.1: Circle: area and circumference formulas.	Area = πr² | Circumference = πd = 2πr | Example: Radius 5 cm: area = π × 5² = 25π ≈ 78.5 cm². Circumference = 2 × π × 5 = 10π ≈ 31.4 cm.
Geometry & Measures 05.05.2: A circle has radius 6 cm. Find its area and circumference in terms of π.	Method: Use r = 6 in each formula and leave π as a letter, as the question asks 'in terms of π'. | Area = π × 36 = 36π cm² | Circumference = 2 × π × 6 = 12π cm
Geometry & Measures 05.05.3: Arc length and sector area formulas. [Higher]	Arc length = θ/360 × 2πr | Sector area = θ/360 × πr² | Example: Radius 6 cm, angle 60°: arc = 60/360 × 2π × 6 = 2π ≈ 6.28 cm. Sector = 60/360 × π × 36 = 6π ≈ 18.8 cm².
Geometry & Measures 05.05.4: Find the area of a sector with radius 10 cm and angle 72°. [Higher]	Method: A sector is a fraction of a circle: angle ÷ 360 of the full area. | 72/360 × π × 100 = 20π ≈ 62.8 cm²
Geometry & Measures 05.06.1: Volume of a prism and a cylinder.	Prism: area of cross-section × length | Cylinder: πr²h | Example: A prism with a 12 cm² cross-section and length 10 cm: V = 12 × 10 = 120 cm³.
Geometry & Measures 05.06.2: A cylinder has radius 3 cm and height 10 cm. Find its volume and surface area.	Method: Volume: area of the circular end × height. Surface area: two circles plus the curved part, which unrolls into a rectangle (circumference × height). | Volume = π × 9 × 10 = 90π ≈ 282.7 cm³ | SA = 2πr² + 2πrh = 18π + 60π = 78π ≈ 245.0 cm²
Geometry & Measures 05.06.3: Volume formulas for a cone, sphere and pyramid. [Higher]	Cone: ⅓πr²h | Sphere: 4/3 πr³ | Pyramid: ⅓ × base area × h | These are usually given in the question. | Example: A cone with r = 3 cm and h = 4 cm: V = ⅓ × π × 9 × 4 = 12π ≈ 37.7 cm³.
Geometry & Measures 05.06.4: Find the volume of a sphere with radius 3 cm. [Higher]	Method: Substitute r = 3 into 4/3 πr³. Cube the radius before multiplying. | 4/3 × π × 27 = 36π ≈ 113.1 cm³
Geometry & Measures 05.07.1: Describe the 4 transformations fully.	Rotation: angle, direction, centre. Reflection: equation of the mirror line. Translation: column vector. Enlargement: scale factor, centre. | Example: 'Rotation 90° clockwise about (0, 0)', or 'Translation by the vector (4, −1)', which means 4 right and 1 down.
Geometry & Measures 05.07.2: What does an enlargement with scale factor ½ do?	Makes the shape half the size. Distances from the centre are halved. | Example: Centre (0, 0), scale factor ½: the point (6, 4) moves to (3, 2).
Geometry & Measures 05.07.3: What does a negative scale factor enlargement do? [Higher]	The image is on the opposite side of the centre and upside down. | Example: Centre (0, 0), scale factor −2: the point (1, 3) moves to (−2, −6).
Geometry & Measures 05.08.1: 4 conditions for congruent triangles.	SSS, SAS, ASA (or AAS), RHS | AAA only proves similar, not congruent! | Example: Two triangles both with sides 3 cm, 4 cm and 5 cm are congruent (SSS).
Geometry & Measures 05.09.1: Loci and constructions: what does each give you?	Perpendicular bisector: points equidistant from two points. Angle bisector: equidistant from two lines. Fixed distance from a point: a circle. | Example: All the points 3 cm from point A form a circle of radius 3 cm around A.
Geometry & Measures 05.10.1: How do you measure a bearing? Bearing of B from A is 070°. Find A from B.	Method: Bearings are measured clockwise from North, in 3 figures. The back bearing faces the opposite way, so add 180° (or subtract if over 180°). | From North, clockwise, 3 figures. | Back bearing: 070 + 180 = 250°
Geometry & Measures 05.11.1: What are plans and elevations?	Plan: view from above. Front elevation: view from the front. Side elevation: view from the side. | Example: An upright cylinder: its plan is a circle and its front elevation is a rectangle.
Geometry & Measures 05.12.1: Circle theorems (part 1). [Higher]	Angle at the centre = 2 × angle at the circumference. Angle in a semicircle = 90°. Angles in the same segment are equal. | Example: The angle at the circumference is 40°, so the angle at the centre on the same arc is 80°.
Geometry & Measures 05.12.2: Circle theorems (part 2). [Higher]	Opposite angles in a cyclic quadrilateral add to 180°. Tangent meets radius at 90°. Two tangents from a point are equal. Alternate segment theorem. | Example: A cyclic quadrilateral has an angle of 75°, so the opposite angle is 180 − 75 = 105°.
Geometry & Measures 05.13.1: a = (3, −1) and b = (2, 4) as column vectors. Find 2a + b	Method: Multiply each part of the vector by the number, then add top to top and bottom to bottom. | 2a = (6, −2) | 2a + b = (8, 2)
Geometry & Measures 05.13.2: How do you show two vectors are parallel? [Higher]	Method: Parallel vectors point the same way, so one is a number multiplied by the other. Check that the same number works for both parts. | One is a multiple of the other. e.g. (2, 3) and (6, 9) are parallel because (6, 9) = 3 × (2, 3).
Geometry & Measures 05.14.1: Metric conversions to learn.	1 km = 1000 m, 1 m = 100 cm, 1 cm = 10 mm | 1 kg = 1000 g, 1 tonne = 1000 kg | 1 litre = 1000 ml, 1 ml = 1 cm³ | Example: 2.5 litres = 2500 ml. 350 cm = 3.5 m. 4 tonnes = 4000 kg.
Pythagoras & Trigonometry 06.01.1: Pythagoras' theorem.	a² + b² = c² | c is the hypotenuse (longest side, opposite the right angle). | Example: Shorter sides 5 and 12: c² = 25 + 144 = 169, so c = √169 = 13.
Pythagoras & Trigonometry 06.01.2: A right-angled triangle has shorter sides 6 cm and 8 cm. Find the hypotenuse.	Method: The hypotenuse is the longest side, so add the squares of the two shorter sides, then square-root. | 6² + 8² = 36 + 64 = 100 | √100 = 10 cm
Pythagoras & Trigonometry 06.01.3: Hypotenuse 13 cm, one side 5 cm. Find the other side.	Method: To find a shorter side, subtract: hypotenuse² minus the other side², then square-root. | Shorter side: subtract | 13² − 5² = 169 − 25 = 144 → 12 cm
Pythagoras & Trigonometry 06.01.4: Find the distance between (1, 2) and (4, 6).	Method: Draw a right-angled triangle between the points: the horizontal and vertical differences are the two short sides. Use Pythagoras. | Pythagoras: √(3² + 4²) = √25 = 5
Pythagoras & Trigonometry 06.02.1: SOHCAHTOA.	sin θ = O/H | cos θ = A/H | tan θ = O/A | Example: Opposite 6, hypotenuse 12: sin θ = 6 ÷ 12 = 0.5, so θ = 30°.
Pythagoras & Trigonometry 06.02.2: Hypotenuse 10 cm, angle 30°. Find the opposite side.	Method: Label the sides from the angle. You know H and want O, so use SOH: sin = O ÷ H, then rearrange. | sin 30° = x/10 | x = 10 × sin 30° = 5 cm
Pythagoras & Trigonometry 06.02.3: Opposite = 7 cm, adjacent = 7 cm. Find the angle.	Method: You know O and A, so use TOA. To find an angle, use the inverse (tan⁻¹) on your calculator. | tan θ = 7/7 = 1 | θ = tan⁻¹(1) = 45°
Pythagoras & Trigonometry 06.03.1: Exact trig values for 30°, 45° and 60°.	sin 30 = ½, cos 30 = √3/2, tan 30 = √3/3 | sin 45 = cos 45 = √2/2, tan 45 = 1 | sin 60 = √3/2, cos 60 = ½, tan 60 = √3 | Example: Hypotenuse 10, angle 60°: adjacent = 10 × cos 60° = 10 × ½ = 5.
Pythagoras & Trigonometry 06.03.2: Exact values of sin, cos and tan of 0° and 90°.	sin 0 = 0, cos 0 = 1, tan 0 = 0 | sin 90 = 1, cos 90 = 0, tan 90 is undefined | Example: 5 × sin 90° = 5 × 1 = 5, and 5 × cos 90° = 5 × 0 = 0.
Pythagoras & Trigonometry 06.04.1: The sine rule and when to use it. [Higher]	a/sin A = b/sin B = c/sin C | Use with a side and its opposite angle (a matching pair). | Example: a = 8, A = 30°, B = 45°: b = 8 × sin 45° ÷ sin 30° = 11.3 (3 s.f.).
Pythagoras & Trigonometry 06.04.2: The cosine rule and when to use it. [Higher]	a² = b² + c² − 2bc cos A | Use with 3 sides, or 2 sides and the angle between them. | Example: Sides 5, 6 and 7: cos A = (6² + 7² − 5²) ÷ (2 × 6 × 7) = 60 ÷ 84, so A = 44.4°.
Pythagoras & Trigonometry 06.04.3: b = 5 cm, c = 7 cm, angle A = 60°. Find a. [Higher]	Method: Two sides and the angle between them, so use the cosine rule. Substitute carefully, then square-root at the end. | a² = 25 + 49 − 2 × 5 × 7 × cos 60 = 39 | a = √39 = 6.24 cm
Pythagoras & Trigonometry 06.05.1: Area of a triangle using sine. [Higher]	Method: Use two sides and the angle BETWEEN them. Half the product of the sides, times the sine of that angle. | Area = ½ ab sin C | a = 8, b = 5, C = 30°: ½ × 8 × 5 × ½ = 10 cm²
Pythagoras & Trigonometry 06.06.1: Find the space diagonal of a 3 cm × 4 cm × 12 cm cuboid. [Higher]	Method: Use Pythagoras in 3D: add the squares of all three edges, then square-root. | 3D Pythagoras: √(3² + 4² + 12²) = √169 = 13 cm
Pythagoras & Trigonometry 06.07.1: Describe the graphs of y = sin x, cos x and tan x. [Higher]	sin and cos: waves between −1 and 1, repeat every 360°. sin starts at 0, cos starts at 1. tan repeats every 180°, with asymptotes at 90°, 270°… | Example: sin x = 0.5 at x = 30° and 150°. cos 180° = −1. tan 45° = 1 and tan 225° = 1.
Probability 07.01.1: Probability rules: scale and 'not'.	Probabilities go from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain). | P(not A) = 1 − P(A) | Example: P(rolling a 4 on a dice) = 1/6, so P(not a 4) = 1 − 1/6 = 5/6.
Probability 07.01.2: A bag has 3 red and 5 blue balls. Find P(red) and P(not red).	Method: Probability = number of ways it can happen ÷ total number of outcomes. 'Not' is 1 minus the probability. | P(red) = 3/8 | P(not red) = 5/8
Probability 07.01.3: Probabilities in a table are 0.2, 0.35, x and 0.15. Find x.	Method: All the probabilities in the table add to 1. Add the ones you know and subtract from 1. | They add to 1: 1 − 0.7 = 0.3
Probability 07.02.1: P(win) = 0.2. How many wins would you expect in 150 games?	Method: Expected number = probability × number of trials. | Expected frequency = P × trials | 0.2 × 150 = 30
Probability 07.02.2: A coin lands heads 18 times in 50 throws. Find the relative frequency. How can you make it more reliable?	Method: Relative frequency = times it happened ÷ total trials. The more trials, the closer it gets to the true probability. | 18 ÷ 50 = 0.36 | More trials = more reliable estimate.
Probability 07.03.1: List all outcomes of flipping two coins. Find P(one head and one tail).	Method: List every outcome systematically so none are missed, then count the ones you want. | HH, HT, TH, TT | P = 2/4 = 1/2
Probability 07.03.2: AND vs OR rules.	Mutually exclusive (OR): P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) | Independent (AND): P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B) | Example: One spin with P(red) = 0.3 and P(blue) = 0.5: P(red or blue) = 0.8. Two coins: P(head and head) = ½ × ½ = ¼.
Probability 07.03.3: Two fair dice are rolled. Find P(both show 6).	Method: The dice do not affect each other (independent), so for 'and' you multiply the probabilities. | Independent, so multiply: 1/6 × 1/6 = 1/36
Probability 07.04.1: P(rain) = 0.3 each day. Find P(exactly one rainy day in two days).	Method: List the ways it can happen (rain then no rain, or no rain then rain). Multiply along each branch, then add the branches. | Tree: RN or NR | 0.3 × 0.7 + 0.7 × 0.3 = 0.42
Probability 07.04.2: A bag has 3 red and 5 blue. Two are taken without replacement. Find P(both red). [Higher]	Method: Without replacement, one fewer ball is left and one fewer red, so the second fraction changes. Multiply along the branch. | 3/8 × 2/7 = 6/56 = 3/28 | Without replacement: the second fraction changes.
Probability 07.05.1: Venn diagram symbols: ξ, ∪, ∩ and A′	ξ = universal set (everything). A ∪ B = A or B (union). A ∩ B = A and B (intersection). A′ = not A. | Example: ξ = {1 to 10}, A = even numbers, B = {1, 2, 3}: A ∩ B = {2}, A ∪ B = {1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 10}, A′ = {1, 3, 5, 7, 9}.
Probability 07.05.2: 30 students: 18 play football, 12 play tennis, 5 play both. Find P(neither).	Method: Fill the Venn diagram from the middle: put 'both' in first, subtract it to get 'only' each, then subtract everything from the total for 'neither'. | Football only 13, tennis only 7, both 5 → 25 | Neither = 5 → 5/30 = 1/6
Probability 07.05.3: Same group: given a student plays football, find P(they play tennis). [Higher]	Method: 'Given' means you only look at that group. The 18 footballers become the new total. | Conditional: only look at the 18 footballers | 5 of them play tennis → 5/18
Statistics 08.01.1: Discrete vs continuous data? Qualitative vs quantitative?	Discrete: counted, exact values (shoe size). Continuous: measured (height). Qualitative: words (colour). Quantitative: numbers. | Example: Number of siblings: discrete. Weight: continuous. Favourite sport: qualitative.
Statistics 08.02.1: Find the mean, median, mode and range of 3, 7, 7, 9, 14	Method: Mean: add and divide by how many. Median: the middle value in order. Mode: the most common. Range: biggest minus smallest. | Mean = 40 ÷ 5 = 8 | Median = 7, Mode = 7 | Range = 14 − 3 = 11
Statistics 08.02.2: Which average should you use if there's an outlier?	The median. The mean is affected by extreme values. | Example: For 2, 3, 3, 4, 40: mean = 52 ÷ 5 = 10.4, but median = 3, which is more typical.
Statistics 08.03.1: Goals scored: 0 (4 games), 1 (6), 2 (5), 3 (5). Find the mean.	Method: Multiply each value by its frequency and add (Σfx), then divide by the total frequency (Σf). | Σfx ÷ Σf = (0 + 6 + 10 + 15) ÷ 20 = 1.55
Statistics 08.03.2: How do you estimate the mean from grouped data? Why is it an estimate?	Use the midpoint of each class × frequency, add, then divide by the total. It's an estimate because we don't know the exact values. | Example: 10 < t ≤ 20 (f = 4) and 20 < t ≤ 30 (f = 6): (15 × 4 + 25 × 6) ÷ 10 = 210 ÷ 10 = 21.
Statistics 08.04.1: 15 out of 60 people chose pizza. What angle is that on a pie chart?	Method: The whole pie is 360°, so find the angle for one person (360 ÷ total), then multiply. | 360 ÷ 60 = 6° per person | 15 × 6 = 90°
Statistics 08.04.2: Where do you plot points on a frequency polygon?	At the midpoint of each class, then join with straight lines. | Example: For the class 10 < h ≤ 20 with frequency 7, plot the point (15, 7).
Statistics 08.05.1: Types of correlation. Does correlation prove causation?	Positive (both increase), negative (one up, one down), none. No: correlation doesn't prove one thing causes the other. | Example: Hours of sunshine and ice cream sales rise together (positive). A car's age and its value (negative).
Statistics 08.05.2: Interpolation vs extrapolation?	Interpolation: estimating inside the data range (reliable). Extrapolation: outside the range (less reliable). | Example: Data covers 1 to 10 hours of revision: estimating at 6 hours is interpolation (reliable); at 20 hours it is extrapolation (unreliable).
Statistics 08.06.1: How do you avoid bias in a sample?	Use a random sample, make it large enough, and make sure it represents the whole population. | Example: Asking only people at a gym about exercise is biased. Picking names at random from the whole school is not.
Statistics 08.06.2: A school has 600 students, 150 in Year 11. A stratified sample of 80 is taken. How many from Year 11? [Higher]	Method: The sample keeps the same proportions as the population: Year 11's fraction of the school × the sample size. | 150/600 × 80 = 20
Statistics 08.07.1: Cumulative frequency: how do you find the median and IQR? [Higher]	Median: read across at n/2. LQ at n/4, UQ at 3n/4. | IQR = UQ − LQ | Example: 60 values: median at the 30th, LQ at the 15th, UQ at the 45th. If LQ = 22 and UQ = 35, IQR = 35 − 22 = 13.
Statistics 08.07.2: What 5 values does a box plot show? [Higher]	Minimum, lower quartile, median, upper quartile and maximum. | Example: Min 4, LQ 9, median 12, UQ 15, max 21: a box from 9 to 15, a line at 12, and whiskers out to 4 and 21.
Statistics 08.08.1: Histograms: frequency density formula. [Higher]	Method: Divide the frequency by the class width (the upper bound minus the lower bound). | Frequency density = frequency ÷ class width | 30 people in 10 < x ≤ 25: 30 ÷ 15 = 2
Statistics 08.09.1: 40 fish are marked and released. Later 50 are caught; 8 are marked. Estimate the population. [Higher]	Method: Assume the proportion marked in the second catch matches the whole population: marked ÷ population = marked caught ÷ caught. Rearrange. | Capture–recapture: 40 × 50 ÷ 8 = 250 fish
Statistics 08.10.1: How do you compare two distributions?	Compare an average (median or mean) AND a measure of spread (range or IQR), in the context of the question. | Example: Team A: median 12, range 5. Team B: median 9, range 11. Team A scored more on average and more consistently.
Exam Skills 09.01.1: What does 'give your answer in terms of π' mean?	Leave π in your answer, e.g. 36π. Don't multiply it out. | Example: A circle with radius 4: area = 16π, not 50.3.
Exam Skills 09.01.2: What does 'show that' mean?	Write every step clearly. The answer is given, so the marks are for your working. | Example: 'Show that 3x + 2 = 14 gives x = 4': 3x = 12, so x = 4, writing every step.
Exam Skills 09.01.3: What does 'estimate' mean in a calculation question?	Round every number to 1 significant figure first, then calculate. | Example: 39.8 × 5.1 ≈ 40 × 5 = 200.
Exam Skills 09.02.1: How accurate should your final answer be?	Follow the question. If it doesn't say, 3 significant figures is usually safe. Never round in the middle of working. | Example: 12.3456 to 3 s.f. is 12.3. Money always has 2 d.p., e.g. £4.50.
Exam Skills 09.02.2: How long should you spend per mark?	Roughly 1 minute per mark. A 4-mark question deserves about 4 minutes. | Example: A 5-mark question: spend about 5 minutes.
Exam Skills 09.03.1: 3 ways to check your answer.	Does it make sense (a person isn't 20 m tall)? Do the inverse operation. Substitute your answer back into the equation. | Example: Got x = 7 for 2x + 3 = 17? Check: 2 × 7 + 3 = 17 ✓.
